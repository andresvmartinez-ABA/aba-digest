Summary

# Summary: Recognition of Graphic Symbols in Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder and Their Parents This study investigated which type of graphic symbol is most effective for augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) interventions in children with autism spectrum disorder. The researchers administered comprehension and preference tests for four graphic symbol systems (DAS, KAAC, WETOK, and HANSPEAK) to 95 participants across five groups: children with ASD (n=19), typically developing children (n=19), parents of children with ASD (n=19), parents of typically developing children (n=19), and AAC experts (n=19). No statistically significant differences were found in graphic symbol comprehension across groups; however, comprehension increased with higher receptive vocabulary levels, and graphic symbol preference differed significantly by group, with children with ASD preferring KAAC, typically developing children and both parent groups preferring DAS, and experts preferring HANSPEAK. The clinical significance of this research lies in demonstrating that children with ASD (the primary users of AAC) should be considered as decision-makers regarding symbol selection, rather than relying solely on adult preferences or theoretical considerations of iconicity and learnability.

Abstract

Background and Objectives: This study aimed to investigate which type of graphic symbol is most effective when implementing AAC interventions for children with autism spectrum disorder. Methods: This study included 19 children aged 4 to 12 with Autism Spectrum Disorders, 19 typically developing children whose receptive vocabulary matched them, 19 parents of Children with Autism Spectrum Disorders, 19 parents of typically developing children, and 19 experts. A total of 95 people were recruited. Four types of graphic symbols—DAS, KAAC, WETOK, and HANSPEAK—were selected, and both a graphic symbol comprehension test and a preference test were administered. Results: No statistically significant differences were found in graphic symbol comprehension. However, graphic symbol preference differed significantly across groups. Children with autism spectrum disorder preferred KAAC, typically developing children and the parents of both ASD and typically developing children preferred DAS, and experts preferred HANSPEAK over the other graphic symbols. In addition, comprehension was found to increase with higher receptive vocabulary levels. Conclusion: This study has clinical significance in that it examined both comprehension and preference in children with autism spectrum disorder, the primary users of AAC. The findings suggest that, rather than parents or experts selecting graphic symbols based on iconicity or ease of learning, it is important to consider the child’s preferences and needs.
