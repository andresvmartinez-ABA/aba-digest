Exposing Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) to a Humanoid Robot: First Observation Study

N. Ishak, Siti Nurul Mahfuzah Mohamad, S. Shamsuddin, S. A. Asmai, Mohd Fairuz Iskandar Othman, Hazmilah Hasan et al.  ·  Journal of Advanced Research in Applied Sciences and Engineering Technology  ·  2025

Summary

# Summary: Humanoid Robot Interaction with Children with ASD This observation study aimed to explore how children with autism spectrum disorder interact with a humanoid robot (NAO) in a structured learning environment and to develop an improved experimental protocol for future child-robot interactions. The methodology involved two phases with two children with ASD at an autism learning centre: Phase I included the child, teacher, and robot together, while Phase II involved only the child and robot in direct interaction. Results demonstrated marked individual differences in robot acceptance, with one child (Child 2) readily engaging with the robot while the other child (Child 1) did not accept the robot's presence; the researchers attributed Child 2's positive engagement partly to the robot's non-threatening and predictable behavior. The findings indicate that children with ASD can engage with humanoid robots during structured play sessions, though individual differences in comfort and acceptance must be considered when implementing robot-assisted interventions. BCBAs and autism practitioners should recognize that humanoid robots may serve as supplemental tools for some learners with ASD, but implementation requires individualized assessment of each child's readiness and comfort level, along with careful protocol design informed by initial observation data.

Abstract

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects social interaction, communication, and behaviour. Children with ASD face social communication and interaction challenges, hindering their daily functioning and learning. Recent technological advancements have explored the use of robots as a new approach to ASD therapy, however, there is a lack of studies on the use of humanoid robots for teaching and learning purposes for children with ASD. This observation study aims to explore the interaction between children with ASD and a humanoid robot NAO at an autism learning centre. The objectives of this study are (i) to present and reflect the fundamental methods and observation during the first engagement of children with ASD with a humanoid robot NAO and (ii) to suggest an improved experimental protocol that can suit the child-robot engagement during future interactions. The session was divided into two phases. Phase I involved each child with a teacher and the robot, while Phase II was between the child and the robot only. Observation results indicated that Child 2 easily accepted the robot, while Child 1 was not able to accept the robot’s presence. This finding highlights the individual differences in engagement and interaction outcomes when a child with ASD interacted with a robot. The researchers noted that NAO's non-threatening and predictable behaviour may have contributed to the engagement and comfort during the interaction for Child 2. In conclusion, the findings suggest that children with ASD can engage and interact with a humanoid robot in a structured play session. The improved protocol will explore the potential benefits and limitations of using humanoid robots as a tool in educating children with ASD
