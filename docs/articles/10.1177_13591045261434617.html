Improving Children's Social Skills With Virtual Reality: A Tailored Single-Session Training in Special Education.

Alsem, Sophie C, van Dijk, Anouk, de Castro, Bram O  ·  Clinical child psychology and psychiatry  ·  2026
85/100This study directly aligns with the profile's interest in virtual reality interventions for children with autism and learning disabilities, focusing on social skills outcomes in school-age children, though it's not specifically ABA-based methodology.
Summary

This study examined whether a single-session virtual reality (VR) training could improve social skills in children with behavior problems enrolled in special education programs. Forty-nine children (ages 7-13, primarily boys with ASD or ADHD) completed one 20-minute VR session targeting teacher-selected skills: staying calm, asking to join a group, or saying no. Results showed substantial immediate improvements in the targeted skills during VR observations (η²p = .35), but teacher reports two weeks later indicated no comparable improvement in classroom behavior (η²p = .07) and no changes in aggressive behavior or emotional problems. The researchers concluded that while interactive VR appears to be an effective tool for practicing social skills, transfer to real-life settings may require multiple sessions, explicit bridging strategies, and/or more emotionally engaging VR content.

Abstract

This within-child study examined whether a tailored, single-session virtual reality (VR) training can enhance social skills among children enrolled in special education programs for behavior problems. Forty-nine children (ages 7–13; 85.7% boys; 57.1% ASD, 20.4% ADHD) completed one 20-min VR session. For each child, teachers selected one skill for children to practice with: staying calm (anger regulation), asking to join a group (peer entry), or saying no (assertiveness). Relative to baseline, in-VR observations showed substantial immediate improvements of the targeted skill (η 2 p = .35), but teacher reports two weeks later showed no comparable improvement in the classroom (η 2 p = .07). Teachers also reported no changes in children’s aggressive behavior or emotional problems two weeks later. Children reported moderate levels of immersion and perceived efficacy, low-to-moderate emotional engagement, and high appreciation of the training, VR, and trainer-child relationship. Overall, findings suggest that interactive VR is an attractive tool to practice socially skilled behavior, but that transfer to real life may require more sessions, explicit bridging strategies, and/or more emotionally engaging VR.
