Digital Psychiatry with Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality: Recent Advances and Limitations

# Summary: Digital Psychiatry with Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality This narrative review examined the clinical applications and efficacy of virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) technologies across psychiatric disorders. The methodology involved a systematic literature search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and seminal works from 2015–2025, prioritizing randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, and systematic reviews that employed immersive VR or AR technologies. Key findings indicated that VR-based therapies demonstrated large effect sizes and robust efficacy for anxiety disorders and phobias, with VR exposure therapy for post-traumatic stress disorder superior to waitlist control and comparable to established psychotherapies; in autism spectrum disorder, VR and AR interventions significantly enhanced cognitive, social, and communication skills; however, limitations included small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, heterogeneous protocols, and adverse effects such as cybersickness. For BCBA practitioners, these findings suggest that VR and AR represent emerging tools with empirical support for skill development and exposure-based interventions in autism, though standardized protocols and larger-scale studies are needed before widespread clinical implementation, and practitioners should remain attentive to individual differences in tolerance and adverse effects.

Abstract

Digital psychiatry has rapidly expanded with the integration of immersive technologies such as virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR). These modalities allow for controlled, engaging, and ecologically valid interventions across a wide range of psychiatric disorders. In this review, a narrative synthesis was conducted based on systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials identified through PubMed, Google Scholar, complemented by seminal earlier works. VR-based therapies demonstrated robust efficacy for anxiety disorders and phobias, with meta-analytic evidence supporting large effect sizes. For post-traumatic stress disorder virtual reality exposure therapy was superior to waitlist control and comparable to established psychotherapies, although AR exposure therapy remains underexplored. In autism spectrum disorder, VR and AR interventions significantly enhanced cognitive, social, and communication skills. AR applications have additionally been validated for cognitive assessment in Alzheimer’s disease, and VR/AR-supported rehabilitation showed promise in attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and neurodevelopmental disorders. Despite encouraging findings, challenges remain, including small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, heterogeneity of protocols, and risks such as cybersickness or fatigue. VR and AR represent innovative tools with growing empirical support across psychiatric practice, extending from diagnostic assessment to therapeutic and rehabilitative interventions. Standardized protocols, large-scale trials, and long-term outcome studies are needed to integrate these technologies into routine clinical care.

Introduction

The rapid digitalization of psychiatric practice has fostered the emergence of digital psychiatry, a field integrating information technologies, mobile health platforms, and immersive tools into clinical care [1]. Our previous review [2] discussed how conversational agents can facilitate patient engagement, provide psychoeducation, and deliver cognitive behavioral interventions through accessible and scalable interfaces. Building upon that foundation, the present review focuses on another transformative dimension of digital psychiatry—virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR)—which offer fundamentally different modes of clinical interaction through immersive and interactive experiences.

VR and AR both aim to deliver simulations that feel realistic while remaining experimentally controllable. VR builds a complete artificial scene and replaces external sensory input so that users perceive only the simulated environment. Head and hand movements are tracked in six degrees of freedom and the viewpoint is updated with very low latency, which supports a strong sense of presence [3-5]. AR follows a different principle by adding registered digital content to the ongoing real world rather than replacing it. Sensor fusion with cameras and inertial units, together with visual inertial odometry and simultaneous localization and mapping, anchors virtual objects to physical surfaces and helps them persist as the user moves. Since AR preserves the natural context, it is well suited for guidance and assessment in everyday settings, including rehabilitation, training for instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), and just-in-time prompts [6].

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of these technologies across a wide spectrum of psychiatric disorders. VR-based exposure and cognitive training have shown efficacy in anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and psychosis [7-11], while AR applications have been explored for cognitive rehabilitation, mindfulness training, and social interaction enhancement [12-15]. Moreover, the integration of VR/AR systems with biometric sensors and artificial intelligence algorithms has allowed for real-time monitoring of physiological and emotional responses, leading to adaptive, personalized therapeutic environments [16]. Despite these promising outcomes, most studies remain limited in sample size and duration, and the long-term clinical effectiveness of immersive technologies requires further validation through large-scale controlled trials [17]. Nonetheless, challenges remain before VR and AR can be fully embedded into everyday psychiatric care. Technical barriers such as cybersickness, high device costs, disparities, and ethical concerns constrain their clinical adoption [18,19].

Considering these opportunities and challenges, the present review aims to provide an updated synthesis of recent advances and limitations of VR and AR in psychiatry. By contextualizing these findings within broader developments in digital mental health, we seek to inform clinical integration strategies and guide directions for future research. To achieve this, we conducted a literature search covering the past decade (2015–2025) using databases including PubMed and Google Scholar. Search terms such as “VR psychiatry,” “AR psychiatry,” “VR exposure therapy,” “AR exposure therapy,” and “digital psychiatry” were employed to identify relevant empirical studies, reviews, and meta-analyses. Based on the selected literature, this review summarizes current evidence regarding clinical applications, mechanisms of action, and therapeutic outcomes of VR and AR interventions.

To enhance transparency, we adopted a structured approach to literature selection. Studies were prioritized for inclusion based on the following criteria: (1) relevance to core psychiatric mechanisms or clinical outcomes; (2) use of immersive VR or AR technologies rather than non-immersive simulations; and (3) empirical designs including randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses published within the past decade. Earlier seminal studies were included selectively when they informed foundational mechanisms or clinical paradigms. Studies summarized in tables were chosen to represent areas with the strongest or most illustrative evidence within each diagnostic category.

Discussion

Digital psychiatry has entered a transformative era with the integration of immersive technologies such as VR and AR. Over the past decade, these tools have evolved from experimental interventions to clinically validated modalities across multiple psychiatric domains. VR-based therapies now hold robust empirical support for anxiety disorders, phobias, and PTSD, demonstrating efficacy comparable to conventional psychotherapies while offering unique advantages in engagement, controllability, and ecological validity. AR technologies, though less extensively studied, have demonstrated versatility from exposure therapy and psychoeducation to cognitive assessment in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease. Taken together, current evidence supports VR as an established adjunctive intervention for anxiety-related disorders, while applications in other psychiatric domains and AR-based interventions should be regarded as promising but not yet definitive. Still, these innovations illustrate how immersion, presence, and interactivity can enhance both therapeutic precision and diagnostic validity while promoting positive mental health, resilience, and subjective well-being in clinical and community populations.

Despite these advances, limitations temper the generalization of current findings. Most clinical trials remain small, short-term, and heterogeneous in design, often lacking standardized protocols or long-term follow-up. Adverse effects such as cybersickness and fatigue highlight the need for systematic monitoring and individualized exposure strategies. Ethical considerations regarding privacy, data security, and equitable access must be addressed before large-scale implementation. The convergence of AR with other digital modalities including biofeedback, wearable sensors, and artificial intelligence, highlights an important opportunity to further develop adaptive and data-informed digital psychiatric interventions. With rigorous methodology, ethical practice, and equitable access, these immersive tools can evolve from innovative adjuncts to integral components of digital mental healthcare, transforming how clinicians assess, engage, and empower patients in the years to come.
