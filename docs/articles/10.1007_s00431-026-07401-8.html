Summary

# Summary: Pediatric Social Robotics in ASD: Navigating AI Blind-Spots Toward Clinical Validation The primary objective of this mini-review is to identify and characterize three under-validated domains in social robotics for pediatric autism—robot–wearable sensor integration, immersive VR–robot hybrid systems, and generative AI conversational interfaces—and to examine their readiness for clinical implementation. The authors employed a bibliometric analysis of 6,907 PubMed-indexed pediatric ASD robotics records using VOSviewer to map the landscape of pediatric social robotics research, identifying emerging areas where clinical deployment may be advancing faster than empirical validation. Key findings indicate that while social robots can produce short-term gains in social and communication skills, the current evidence base is limited by small pilot studies, insufficient longitudinal validation, and unresolved safety concerns, including sensor intolerance, cognitive overload, privacy risks, and unconstrained AI-generated dialogue that may reinforce maladaptive communication. The authors propose a five-module clinical validation roadmap addressing pediatric calibration standards, standardized developmental outcome measures, multisite randomized controlled trials, longitudinal monitoring, and human-supported safety architectures. For BCBAs and autism practitioners, this review emphasizes that adaptive social robotics cannot currently be supported as primary therapeutic interventions and should not be widely deployed until pediatric-specific safety standards and adequate longitudinal evidence are established, with the authors cautioning that human-centered developmental care must remain central to intervention design.

Abstract

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into children’s therapeutic environments, social robots are emerging as one of the most clinically complex AI applications for pediatric autism spectrum disorder (ASD). This mini-review examines three under-validated “blind-spot” domains: robot–wearable sensor integration, immersive VR–robot hybrid systems, and generative AI conversational interfaces. Although these technologies offer promising opportunities for adaptive and personalized intervention, current evidence remains limited by small pilot studies, insufficient longitudinal validation, and unresolved developmental safety concerns. Key risks include pediatric sensor intolerance, cognitive overload during immersive exposure, privacy concerns related to continuous data collection, and unconstrained AI-generated dialogue that may reinforce maladaptive communicative patterns in vulnerable children. We propose a five-module clinical validation roadmap focused on pediatric calibration standards, standardized developmental outcome measures, multisite randomized controlled trials, longitudinal monitoring, and human-supported safety architectures.Conclusion: Adaptive social robotics offers a promising but currently unvalidated frontier in pediatric ASD care. These systems cannot yet be supported as primary therapeutic interventions, as pediatric-specific calibration standards, standardized developmental outcome measures, and adequately powered longitudinal trials remain absent. The proposed validation framework outlines a pathway for ensuring AI-supported interventions augment rather than replace authentic human-centered developmental care. What is Known:• Social robots can produce measurable short-term gains in social and communication skills in children with autism spectrum disorder.• Supporting evidence derives largely from small, short-duration pilot studies conducted in controlled settings, limiting clinical applicability.What is New:• Three under-validated blind-spot domains are identified: robot–wearable sensor integration, VR–robot hybrid systems, and generative AI conversational interfaces.• A five-module Clinical Validation Roadmap is proposed to move these domains from pilot demonstration toward pediatric standard of care.

Introduction

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into daily life, its clinical implications for pediatric populations are acquiring new urgency. Broader pediatric technology literature has already linked structured interaction with educational robotics and digital tools to gains in higher-order cognitive skills, including problem solving, creativity, and critical thinking [1]. However, this only addresses general pediatric and educational populations, leaving a significant gap in available literature on disorder-specific findings. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), in particular, has become a heightened area of interest because its profound phenotypic, sensory, and communicative heterogeneity has long challenged standardized behavioral interventions. This complexity has encouraged growing clinical and technological interest in autonomous AI-enabled modalities, with three emerging approaches now occupying an important frontier in pediatric ASD therapy: robot and wearable sensor integration, immersive VR and robot hybrid systems, and generative AI conversational interfaces [2].

These interventions represent a shift away from static, pre-programmed therapeutic tools toward adaptive closed-loop systems that can perceive, interpret, and respond to a child’s physiological, behavioral, or cognitive state in real time. While this adaptive capacity may expand therapeutic personalization, it also introduces new clinical risks that are not easily captured by evaluation metrics designed for conventional behavioral tools. These include sensor-induced sensory distress, immersion-related cognitive overload, and unpredictable AI-generated dialogue that may influence children’s communicative development in ways that remain insufficiently understood. As AI applications continue to expand in pediatric settings, the clinical community has a pressing obligation to characterize these risks before widespread deployment.

To contextualize these emerging concerns, we generated a bibliometric co-occurrence map from PubMed-indexed literature on pediatric ASD and social robotics using VOSviewer (Fig. 1). This co-occurrence map was constructed from a pediatric- and autism-specific robotics corpus of 6907 PubMed records, the full retrieved set for this domain (English-language records; title, abstract, author-keyword, and MeSH fields; searched 4 December 2025 with no date restriction and deduplicated by DOI and title). These 6907 records constitute the pediatric-ASD-specific component of the larger 106,907-record dataset assembled for our companion landscape analysis, which additionally incorporated two capped reference corpora, namely general therapeutic and rehabilitation robotics and the broader autism literature (50,000 records each). Figure 1 therefore visualizes only the 6907-record pediatric-ASD subset, whereas the figure of 106,907 records denotes the complete three-corpus dataset characterized in that companion analysis. Terms were extracted from titles, abstracts, and keywords, and thematic clusters were derived in VOSviewer using full counting and association-strength normalization. In this review, a “blind spot” refers to a known unknown: an area that is visible within the pediatric robotics literature but remains insufficiently understood, validated, or standardized for clinical implementation. These domains are not absent from the field; rather, they appear as emerging or recognized areas whose empirical depth has not yet matched their conceptual or technological promise. We derived the three blind spots by interpreting the structure of the network, with particular attention to domains that appeared relevant to pediatric ASD care but showed limited mechanistic explanation, long-term validation, safety characterization, or implementation guidance [3]. On this basis, robot and wearable sensor integration, immersive VR and robot hybrid systems, and generative AI conversational interfaces were positioned as clinically relevant blind spots. Discussion

Several methodological boundaries of this review warrant acknowledgment. The framework presented is derived exclusively from pediatric ASD populations; therefore, the calibration thresholds, dosing parameters, and safety architectures proposed here cannot be generalized to adult neurorehabilitation or other neurodevelopmental conditions without separate empirical validation. The roadmap also remains a theoretical construct, structurally derived from fragmented pilot data, and will require prospective testing across controlled multisite environments before it can serve as a trusted clinical blueprint.

The blind-spot technologies examined here, namely wearable sensor fusion, VR and robot hybrid systems, and generative AI conversational interfaces, represent the most dynamic and potentially transformative frontier in pediatric ASD therapy. Their adaptive architectures offer unprecedented personalization for heterogeneous populations that standardized behavioral tools have not fully served. However, premature clinical deployment before resolving sensor tolerability, cognitive overload, and unconstrained AI outputs risks causing acute harm, including sensory distress, device abandonment, and behavioral regression in vulnerable pediatric populations. This concern is especially important because generative AI systems can produce increasingly sophisticated representations and language-like outputs without necessarily possessing biological understanding, affective grounding, or self-regulating awareness [20, 21].

The proposed five-module clinical validation roadmap provides a sequential and actionable pathway for converting recognized “known unknowns” into evidence-based, scalable therapeutic tools. As AI increasingly shapes children’s developmental environments across clinical, educational, and home settings, the pediatric medical community must insist on rigorous empirical validation, robust safety architectures, and sustained human oversight to ensure that technological innovation supports, rather than compromises, the authentic developmental trajectory of every child on the autism spectrum. In this sense, pediatric social robotics is not a ship that should be launched at full speed simply because its engines are powerful. It is a vessel entering fog dense waters, where every sensor, algorithm, and therapeutic decision must be carefully calibrated before it can safely carry children toward meaningful growth.
