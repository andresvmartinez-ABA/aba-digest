From Passive to Proactive: Designing an Engaging VR Hand Coordination Training System for Children with Autism

Yang-Cheng Wang, Xueyinuo Li  ·  Multimodal Technologies and Interaction  ·  2026

Summary

# Summary: VR Hand Coordination Training for Children with Autism This study investigated whether a VR-based hand coordination training system could improve engagement and motor outcomes in children with autism compared to conventional practice methods. The researchers used a controlled design with 60 children with ASD (ages 6–12), implementing a VR system featuring a headset paired with haptic gloves that provided customizable visual, auditory, and tactile feedback alongside structured task progression. The VR condition produced greater improvements in hand coordination accuracy and longer training engagement compared to conventional practice, with qualitative data from children, caregivers, and therapists indicating that clear goals, predictable feedback, and manageable interaction demands facilitated participation, though individual differences in sensory tolerance and pacing preferences were noted. For BCBA practitioners, these findings suggest that structured VR systems with multimodal sensory feedback may enhance both motor skill acquisition and on-task behavior in children with ASD, though careful individualization of sensory complexity and pacing remains necessary. Future research will explore how adaptive pacing and context-sensitive supports can improve generalization of trained skills to environments outside the training system.

Abstract

Children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) often face sensory and attentional challenges that affect their engagement in fine-motor training. This study investigates a VR-based hand coordination system designed using human-centered principles, integrating VR headset configuration with haptic gloves to offer customizable visual, auditory, and tactile feedback and structured task progression. In a controlled study with 60 children with ASD aged 6–12, the VR condition showed greater improvements in hand coordination accuracy and longer training engagement compared to conventional practice. Qualitative feedback from children, caregivers, and therapists further indicated that the clarity of goals, predictable feedback, and manageable interaction demands supported participation for many children, while also revealing individual differences in tolerance for pacing and sensory complexity. The findings suggest that structured interaction design can support fine-motor practice when VR interactions match users’ regulatory needs. Future work will examine how adaptive pacing and context-sensitive cues can better support skill use beyond the training environment.
