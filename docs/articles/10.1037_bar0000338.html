Prevalence of Error Types of Synthesized Contingency Assessments.

Helvey, Casey Irwin, McCabe, Liam H, Greer, Brian D et al.  ·  Behavior analysis (Washington, D.C.)  ·  2026
PFA/IISCA
78/100Directly relevant as it addresses functional analysis methodology and behavioral assessment techniques central to the profile, with clear clinical application for treatment planning, though it lacks the specific populations (autism, preschool/school-age) that are emphasized in the stated interests.
Summary

# Summary: Prevalence of Error Types of Synthesized Contingency Assessments This study examined the accuracy of synthesized contingency assessments compared to isolated functional analyses in identifying variables that maintain problem behavior. The researchers conducted a receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) analysis aggregating data across multiple published studies comparing standardized-synthesized and interview-informed synthesized contingency assessments to functional analysis outcomes. Both assessment types demonstrated high true-positive rates (standardized: 0.84; interview-informed: 0.80) but also demonstrated consistently elevated false-positive rates (standardized: 0.76; interview-informed: 0.57), meaning they frequently misidentified non-functional stimuli as reinforcers; additionally, interview-informed assessments showed marked variability across studies in detecting attention functions (true-positive rates ranging 0.0–1.0), while performing better with tangible and escape functions. For BCBAs and autism practitioners, these findings suggest that synthesized contingency assessments are effective at identifying actual reinforcers but should be supplemented with additional assessment methods to confidently rule out non-functional variables, as including false positives in treatment may increase vulnerability to behavioral resurgence.

Abstract

The comparability of synthesized contingency assessments to isolated functional analyses has been questioned because it is assumed that synthesized contingency assessments alone can identify interactive effects. Interactive effects occur when individual contingencies combine to produce a reinforcing effect greater than the sum of their individual effects. McCabe et al. (2025) investigated these effects by comparing within-participant responding during and after synthesized contingency assessments. They found that interactive effects are rare, suggesting that synthesized contingency assessments and isolated functional analyses are comparable in most cases. Therefore, we used binary receiver-operating-characteristic plots to quantify true- and false-positive rates of synthesized contingency assessments relative to functional analyses. Implications for assessment and treatment practices are discussed.

Results

Figure 2 displays the receiver-operating-characteristic plots comparing outcomes across functional analyses and standardized-synthesized contingency analyses. The cross-study aggregated data (top left panel) for all applications of the standardized-synthesized contingency analysis by test condition show that all data points fall within Quadrant I, indicating that all test conditions had true-positive rates above 0.5 and false-positive rates above 0.5 across studies. The true-positive rate was 1.0, 0.77, and 0.71 for attention, tangible, and escape, respectively. For attention, the standardized-synthesized contingency analysis identified a true positive in 100% of applications. For tangible, the assessment identified a true positive in 77% of applications and missed it in 23%. For escape, the assessment identified a true positive in 71% of applications and missed it in 29%. The false-positive rate was 0.73, 0.78, and 0.77 for attention, tangible, and escape, respectively. Thus, the standardized-synthesized contingency assessment did not rule out attention (i.e., falsely identified it) in 73% of applications and did rule it out in 27% of applications. The assessment did not rule out tangible in 78% of applications and did so in 22%. The assessment did not rule out escape in 77% of applications and did so in 23%. In general, the standardized-synthesized contingency analysis tended to accurately identify true positives for attention, tangible, and escape, but also frequently misidentified these same stimuli as reinforcers.

For the remaining panels in Figure 2, each data point represents data from all participants in a given study for the attention (top right panel), tangible (bottom left panel), and escape (bottom right panel) test conditions. The size of a data point corresponds to the sample size of a given study, with smaller data points representing smaller sample sizes. This was done to provide additional context on the relative weight of the findings from each study. Across all test conditions, all data points fall within Quadrant I, indicating a true-positive rate above 0.5 and a false-positive rate above 0.5, similar to the interpretation from the aggregate data. Overall, the standardized-synthesized contingency analysis maintained a true-positive rate of 0.84 and a false-positive rate of 0.76.

Figure 3 displays the receiver-operating-characteristic plots comparing outcomes across functional analyses and interview-informed synthesized contingency analyses. The data in Figure 3 are displayed in the same way as those in Figure 2. Visual analysis of aggregated data shows that the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis yielded a high true-positive rate and a high false-positive rate. This finding suggests that the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis performs similarly to the standardized-synthesized contingency analysis, and all aggregated data points remain within Quadrant I. The true-positive rate was 0.67, 0.79, and 0.83 for attention, tangible, and escape, respectively. For attention, the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis identified a true positive in 67% of applications and missed it in 33%. The assessment identified a true positive for tangible in 79% of applications and missed it in 21%. The assessment identified a true positive for escape in 83% of applications and missed it in 17%. The false-positive rate was 0.57, 0.64, and 0.50 for attention, tangible, and escape, respectively. The interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis did not rule out (i.e., falsely identified) attention in 57% of applications and did rule it out in 43% of applications. The assessment did not rule out tangible in 64% of applications and ruled it out in 36%. The assessment was equally likely to falsely identify (50%) as it was to rule out (50%) escape. In general, the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis tended to accurately identify true positives for attention, tangible, and escape, but also frequently misidentified these same stimuli as reinforcers.

However, unlike the data in Figure 2, the aggregated data in Figure 3 were not representative of the wide range in variability seen within and across test conditions when the aggregated data are separated by study. For attention, one study fell in Quadrant II, whereas the remaining five studies fell in Quadrant IV. This means that only one of the six studies had a true-positive rate above 0.5, while the other five studies reported true-positive rates of zero. In other words, most studies that used the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis missed it in 100% of applications. Moreover, all but one study (the same one that had a high true-positive rate) had false-positive rates above 0.5, meaning that the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis did not rule out (i.e., falsely identified) attention in 50% or more of applications.

For the tangible test condition, data points fell across Quadrants I, II, and IV. All but one study had true-positive rates at or above 0.5, and four of six studies had false-positive rates above 0.5. Thus, despite the variability, most studies yielded outcomes similar to those in the aggregate data for the tangible condition.

For the escape test condition, all data points fell between Quadrants I and II. All studies had true-positive rates above 0.5, with many reporting perfect (1.0) true-positive rates for escape. That is, many studies identified true positives for escape in 100% of applications. In addition, four of the six studies had false-positive rates below 0.5, indicating that escape was falsely identified in less than 50% of applications. Overall, the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis maintained a true-positive rate of 0.80 and a false-positive rate of 0.57.

Discussion

Although synthesized contingency assessments are effective at identifying the variables that maintain problem behavior and are reasonably accurate at predicting efficacious treatments, they may be less likely to rule out variables that do not maintain problem behavior or distinguish between sources of reinforcement (e.g., Rahaman et al., 2024; Tiger & Effertz, 2021). Therefore, more detailed quantitative analyses are needed to clarify function-specific trends related to correspondence. The current study sought to extend the recent literature on the validity of synthesized contingency assessments by conducting a quantitative analysis of their correspondence with functional analysis outcomes to gain a more complete understanding of what these synthesized contingency assessments can and cannot detect.

Using the receiver-operating-characteristic approach, we assessed the prevalence of true-positive and false-positive rates across studies and test conditions for standardized-synthesized contingency analyses and interview-informed synthesized contingency analyses published in the literature. Overall, both assessment types were prone to false-positive errors. False positives were the primary error type across studies and test conditions for the standardized-synthesized contingency analysis, whereas the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis demonstrated considerable variability in error type both within and across studies and functions. For example, the range of true positive rates across studies was 0.0–1.0, 0.0–1.0, and 0.5–1.0 for attention, tangible, and escape functions of problem behavior, respectively. Additionally, the range of false-positive rates for this same approach was similarly extensive across studies, with rates of 0.3–1.0, 0.0–1.0, and 0.0–1.0 for the attention, tangible, and escape functions of problem behavior, respectively. For neither metric were the aggregated data reflective of the wide distribution in outcomes obtained for any function of responding when examined on a study-by-study basis.

The results of the present study suggest that synthesized contingency assessments may introduce different error types, depending on the assessment approach and test conditions. For both assessment types, false positives were prevalent across test conditions. It is worth noting that four of the six studies that included escape test conditions in an interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis had false-positive rates below 0.5. Readers might interpret this finding as suggesting that false positives are less likely when escape is included in the synthesized contingency. However, it should be noted that most studies with low false-positive rates had a small number of participants, and this was also associated with a low true-positive rate in one of the studies. Moreover, the mean false-positive rate for the escape test condition was above 0.5. Thus, false positives were a common finding across test conditions for the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis. This finding is consistent with other studies evaluating their validity (e.g., Rahaman et al., 2024; Tiger & Effertz, 2021).

Another error type that stood out from this analysis was the low true-positive rates for the attention test condition in the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis. Specifically, only one of the six studies that tested for an attention function had a true-positive rate above 0.5. This is compared to true-positive rates above 0.5 for both escape and tangible test conditions. One interpretation of this finding is that the reasonably high true-positive rates of interview-informed synthesized contingency analyses for escape and tangible test conditions are hindered by their suboptimal true-positive rates across most studies in detecting the attention function of problem behavior. It remains unclear what accounts for this rather surprising finding (cf. Thompson & Iwata, 2007) and whether attempts to address it are warranted, given the high false-positive rates we observed with this approach.

The coding methods in the current study were based on the findings of McCabe et al. (2025), which found that interactive effects are relatively uncommon. Accordingly, when a synthesized assessment was differentiated in the current analysis, each putative source of reinforcement included in the synthesized contingency was evaluated against the functional analysis outcomes to identify true and false positives and negatives. For example, if a synthesized assessment evaluating escape, attention, and tangible reinforcement was differentiated, but the functional analysis identified only attention as a functional reinforcer, we coded one true positive (attention) and two false positives (escape and tangible). An alternative approach favoring interactive effects would involve coding this outcome as one false positive (i.e., the synthesized interaction) and one false negative (i.e., isolated attention). We did not adopt this approach because current evidence provides limited support for doing so. Nevertheless, future researchers could apply alternative methods to examine how different conceptual assumptions influence conclusions about the validity of synthesized assessments.

Similarly, alternative methods for analyzing and comparing true/false positives and true/false negatives could provide additional information about the validity of synthesized assessments relative to functional analyses. For example, the true-positive rate in the current study was the proportion of actual reinforcers correctly identified by the synthesized assessment. This calculation (see Table 2), which is commonly used in receiver-operating-characteristic analyses (Muschelli, 2020), included false negatives in the denominator because they represent reinforcers the assessment failed to identify. Thus, this measure reflected how sensitive the assessment was to detecting true reinforcers. An alternative approach could involve calculating the proportion of positive assessment outcomes that were correct (i.e., TP / [TP + FP]). In this calculation, false positives are included in the denominator because they represent cases in which the assessment incorrectly identified a reinforcer. Thus, this measure would reflect the accuracy of positive assessment outcomes. A similar approach could be applied to negative outcomes (i.e., TN / [TN + FN]) to evaluate the accuracy of non-reinforcer classifications.

One might ask “what is the harm?” when discussing the high false-positive rates associated with synthesized contingencies, as including additional contextually relevant variables could enrich the treatment process. Recent basic (Smith et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2026) and human operant (Smith et al., in press) research suggests that resurgence is greater following treatment with synthesized reinforcement relative to treatments with isolated reinforcement. Additional research in the clinic is needed before firm conclusions can be made, but preliminary data suggest that including stimuli identified as false positives could make treatments more susceptible to resurgence. It is also worth discussing the impact of false-negative findings, as not including functionally relevant stimuli is likely to lead to ineffective treatment from the outset. Synthesized assessments are not designed to rule out functional reinforcers with only a single test condition. However, false negatives might occur if the synthesized assessment is undifferentiated and/or if the preliminary assessment process (e.g., caregiver interview) does not nominate all functionally relevant stimuli for inclusion in the combined contingency. Undifferentiated synthesized assessments are uncommon (e.g., Jessel et al., 2016), and published comparisons suggest that preliminary assessment outcomes are rarely false negative (e.g., Greer et al., 2019; Irwin & Van Camp, 2020). Thus, including false positives in treatment might pose a greater risk than omitting false negatives when using synthesized contingency assessment to inform treatment.

Given the high false-positive rates identified in the current study and the low prevalence of interactive effects reported by McCabe et al. (2025), we recommend that behavior analysts initially forgo the use of synthesized contingencies and consider them only when a functional analysis with isolated contingencies suggests they are necessary. For example, if an interview with caregivers suggests multiple sources of reinforcement and the initial functional analysis is undifferentiated, or there are other indicators of incomplete reinforcement (e.g., target responding during the reinforcement interval), progressing to a synthesized contingency assessment would be reasonable. If a synthesized contingency assessment is indicated, we recommend that behavior analysts opt for the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis over the standardized-synthesized contingency analysis1, as the latter was originally designed as a control condition for comparison to the interview-informed approach, rather than as a clinical assessment. Moreover, the results of the current study suggest that the caregiver interview and structured observation reduce the false-positive rate compared to assuming multiple control. When using the interview-informed approach, we recommend obtaining a second opinion on preliminary indirect assessments to clarify which components should be included and which should not. For instance, both a caregiver and another stakeholder (e.g., a teacher) could complete the open-ended interview before the assessment. If both informants agree that a particular stimulus change is likely related to problem behavior, the behavior analyst should consider including it in the synthesized contingency. Conversely, if there is disagreement, the behavior analyst should strongly consider collecting additional data (e.g., through descriptive assessment) before including this stimulus change, as its inclusion may lead to a false-positive result. However, behavior analysts should be aware that high interrater agreement may still yield false positives due to inaccurate caregiver report (e.g., Iwata et al., 2013; Kelley et al., 2011), and the predictive validity of such indirect assessments may be limited with agreement between only two respondents (Smith et al., 2012).

If a synthesized contingency assessment must be conducted as the first course of action, such as when there is a lack of training or administrative support for a functional analysis (e.g., Oliver et al., 2015), we recommend conducting a component analysis following the initial assessment. This would involve testing each component of the synthesized contingency to see if responding maintains when the putative establishing operations and consequences are isolated. Behavior analysts can use a multielement functional analysis to evaluate isolated test conditions and modify the assessment to increase its efficiency. For example, Irwin Helvey and Van Camp (2022) used extended single-condition evaluations that continued minute-by-minute until responding either extinguished or reached a steady state. If responding extinguishes under these conditions, the test stimulus may be a false positive. Although this approach may improve the accuracy of outcomes following the interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis, it will also increase overall assessment time, potentially undermining the primary benefit of synthesized contingency assessments: efficiency. Additional research is needed to assess the efficiency of this process compared to starting with a functional analysis from the outset. That said, the pursuit of efficiency and acceptability should not compromise the accuracy and validity of an assessment (Kranak & Briggs, 2025).

Moreover, given that false positives are likely following synthesized contingency assessments, potential iatrogenic effects (e.g., the emergence of a novel function; Retzlaff et al., 2020; McCabe et al., 2025) may be more likely and would be impossible to identify post hoc. Although iatrogenic effects can occur during functional analysis (Fernandez et al., 2024; Rooker et al., 2011), our results do not suggest a clear path forward to address these concerns regarding synthesized contingency assessments. Future research should investigate whether the increased rate of false-positive findings with synthesized contingency assessments complicates treatment, particularly when examining treatment adherence and procedural fidelity among both providers and caregivers, especially when components for additional, nonfunctional stimuli are included in terminal treatment procedures.

Finally, for behavior analysts concerned about the risks of evoking and reinforcing problem behavior, an alternative approach is to forgo the assessment process and to probe a standardized treatment package that includes common social functions of problem behavior (i.e., escape, attention, tangible). This approach may implicate both functional and nonfunctional stimuli during treatment, but recent research has shown that including nonfunctional stimuli does not compromise initial treatment efficacy (McCabe et al., 2025; see also Call et al., 2024). If the treatment is effective, it may provide an opportunity to refine the intervention, such as removing a potentially unnecessary contingency. Conversely, if the treatment is ineffective, it may identify the need for a more systematic evaluation of each suspected contingency maintaining problem behavior—a functional analysis. Importantly, although initial treatment efficacy may not be affected, behavior analysts are cautioned that unnecessarily synthesizing stimuli could negatively impact treatment durability and lead to greater resurgence later (Smith et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2026; Smith et al., in press). Thus, this approach is recommended only when reasonable safety accommodations (e.g., Brown et al., 2025) cannot be made during a functional analysis, and the potential risks associated with unnecessarily synthesizing contingencies are outweighed by concerns about assessment feasibility or safety.
