Summary

# Summary: Speaker-Aware Engagement Modeling for Children with ASD This study investigated whether speaker diarization (automated separation of child and caregiver audio) improved acoustic engagement modeling during socially assistive robot interventions with children with autism spectrum disorder. The researchers trained classical machine learning models on acoustic features extracted from seven children's month-long in-home interactions, comparing models using diarized versus undiarized audio data. Diarized audio produced higher AUROC values across participants, though differences were not statistically significant, and the relative importance of child versus caregiver acoustic features varied substantially across individual participants. These findings demonstrate that speaker-specific acoustic information can be systematically isolated in naturalistic interactions and that engagement-related acoustic features differ meaningfully across children with ASD, extending evidence of individual variability to the acoustic domain. For BCBAs and autism practitioners, these results suggest that engagement assessment tools incorporating acoustic analysis may need individualized calibration rather than standardized feature weighting, and that naturalistic in-home data collection using technology can capture clinically relevant behavioral information despite complex interaction patterns.

Abstract

Prior work on engagement modeling in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has demonstrated substantial individual variability in behavioral features, particularly in visual modalities. In this work, we extend the literature to acoustic features in naturalistic interactions, where multiple speakers and atypical interaction patterns complicate speaker-specific analysis. We investigated whether speaker diarization improved acoustic engagement modeling during month-long, in-home socially assistive robot (SAR) interventions with seven children with ASD. We trained classical machine learning models using acoustic features extracted from diarized child and parent/caregiver audio and compared their performance with models trained on undiarized audio. Diarized audio consistently yielded higher area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values, although these differences were not statistically significant. More importantly, the relative contributions of child and caregiver acoustic features varied across participants, indicating that the acoustic information associated with engagement was not consistent across children. These findings extend evidence of participant-level variability in ASD engagement modeling to the acoustic domain and demonstrate the feasibility of isolating speaker-specific vocalizations for studying engagement in complex, naturalistic interactions.
