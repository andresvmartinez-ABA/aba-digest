Emerging information technologies across the autism service and prevention continuum: a narrative review of screening, diagnosis, intervention, and health equity

Abstract

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a major global public-health concern, yet timely identification and support remain difficult to access for many families, especially in rural, low-resource, and otherwise underserved settings. Emerging information technologies have been proposed as tools for narrowing this service gap. These include artificial intelligence (AI), wearable sensors, and the Internet of Things, mobile health applications and digital therapeutics, telehealth, immersive virtual and augmented reality, social robotics, and large language models. This narrative review organizes the evidence across an autism service and prevention continuum: upstream risk awareness and health education, early screening and diagnosis, and intervention, management, and long-term support. We use this continuum strictly as a public-health service-organization framework, not to imply preventing the birth or existence of autistic individuals, consistent with a neurodiversity-affirming perspective. Across each stage, we apply a health-equity lens centered on reach, affordability, scalability, health-system integration, and equity of performance. We summarize current applications, appraise recurring limitations, and synthesize cross-cutting issues of equity, data governance, ethics, neurodiversity, and implementation. Recurring limitations include small and heterogeneous studies, dataset bias, weak external validation, short follow-up, and uneven regulatory maturity. Emerging information technologies show genuine promise as complements to conventional autism services, particularly where specialist resources are scarce. Realizing population-level benefit will require equity-centered design, validation in diverse and low-resource settings, larger and longer trials, interoperable standards, and meaningful involvement of autistic people and their families.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterized by differences in social communication and interaction, together with restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior and interests. Once considered rare, ASD is now understood to be common and to constitute a substantial and growing public-health burden worldwide. Estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021 indicate that approximately 61.8 million people, about one in every 127 individuals, were on the autism spectrum globally in 2021, and that ASD accounted for roughly 11.5 million disability-adjusted life-years, ranking within the top ten causes of non-fatal health burden among people younger than 20 years (1). Complementary GBD analyses place neurodevelopmental and other disorders affecting the nervous system among the leading contributors to global disease burden, with ASD specifically identified among the ten conditions with the highest age-standardized burden (2), and document the persistent and rising contribution of mental and neurodevelopmental disorders to population health loss over recent decades (3,4). These figures establish ASD not merely as a clinical entity managed at the level of the individual, but as a population-level concern that demands public-health strategies for prevention, early identification, and support.

A defining feature of the autism field is the gap between what is known to improve outcomes and what most affected children and families can actually access. There is broad consensus that early identification and intervention, delivered when neural plasticity is greatest, are associated with better developmental trajectories (5,6). Yet the conventional diagnostic pathway depends on lengthy, specialist-administered behavioral assessments that are time-consuming, partly subjective, and concentrated in urban centers and high-income settings (5,7). The consequences are long waiting lists, late diagnosis, and pronounced disparities: children in rural areas, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and from marginalized or socioeconomically disadvantaged communities are systematically underdiagnosed and underserved (8), while families in better-resourced settings increasingly access early screening and intervention (9). The result is a global “autism service gap”: measured prevalence and recorded burden are strongly shaped by diagnostic capacity, while under-ascertainment, under-diagnosis, and limited access to trained professionals and validated tools remain especially pronounced in LMICs and other under-resourced settings (1,9).

Against this backdrop, emerging information technologies have attracted intense interest as potential tools for narrowing the autism service gap. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning, the proliferation of low-cost sensors and wearable devices, the maturation of telehealth, the spread of smartphones even in resource-limited settings, and the emergence of immersive media and conversational AI have created new opportunities to extend screening, diagnosis, intervention, and long-term support beyond conventional service models (10,11). However, these technologies differ substantially in their scalability and resource requirements. Community-oriented approaches, such as telehealth and app- or video-based screening, may reduce dependence on geographically concentrated specialist services and expand access in underserved settings, whereas neurophysiological, biomarker-based, and other resource-intensive approaches may require specialized equipment, technical expertise, and infrastructure. Across this heterogeneous technological ecosystem, potential benefits must therefore be weighed against risks of bias, widening rather than narrowing inequities, premature clinical adoption ahead of robust validation, and insufficient attention to the priorities of autistic people themselves.

Against this background, the present narrative review brings together evidence on emerging information technologies across the autism service and prevention continuum from a public-health and health-equity perspective. Specifically, this review has four main objectives: (1) to map the current applications of emerging information technologies across upstream public-health action, early screening and diagnosis, and intervention, management, and long-term support; (2) to critically appraise the strength, consistency, and major limitations of the evidence supporting these technologies; (3) to evaluate their public-health relevance using five equity-oriented criteria—reach, affordability, scalability, health-system integration, and equity of performance; and (4) to identify the ethical, regulatory, data-governance, neurodiversity, and implementation issues that may determine whether these technologies reduce or widen existing disparities in autism-related services.

To address these objectives, the review was guided by three research questions. First, how are emerging information technologies currently applied across upstream public-health action, early screening and diagnosis, and intervention, management, and long-term support for autistic individuals? Second, what are the principal strengths, limitations, and levels of evidence supporting these technologies across the different stages of the autism service and prevention continuum? Third, under what conditions are these technologies likely to reduce, reproduce, or widen inequities in access, affordability, scalability, health-system integration, and performance across different populations and settings?

The distinctive contribution of this review is not simply to describe individual technologies, many of which have already been examined in technology- or stage-specific reviews (12–18). Rather, it integrates a fragmented evidence base within a single public-health service continuum and evaluates these technologies using a consistent health-equity framework. This approach shifts the central question from technical performance alone to whether a technology expands access, for whom it works, in which settings it can be implemented, and at what cost. The prevention continuum is used solely as a framework for organizing evidence on developmental surveillance, early identification, intervention, and support. It is not intended to imply that autism or autistic individuals should be prevented, but rather to examine how timely, accessible, and equitable support can be strengthened across different stages of service delivery.

Discussion

Autism is a major and growing global public-health concern marked by a profound gap between the interventions known to help and the services most affected people can access (1,9). Emerging information technologies offer realistic opportunities to narrow this gap, although their contributions differ substantially across technological modalities. Community-oriented approaches such as telehealth and app- or video-based screening may extend reach and reduce dependence on geographically concentrated specialist services, whereas more resource-intensive approaches, including neurophysiological and biomarker-based technologies, may provide complementary forms of assessment or measurement but require greater expertise and infrastructure. These technologies include AI and machine learning, wearables and IoT, mHealth, telehealth, immersive media, robotics, and generative AI. The strongest near-term evidence is at the screening and diagnostic stage, with a growing but more qualified evidence base for intervention (17,28,31). Whether these opportunities translate into equitable population-level benefit, however, will depend on external validity, representativeness, affordability, accessibility, ethics, data governance, regulation, health-system integration, and implementation in real-world and resource-limited settings. Treated as complements to, rather than replacements for, conventional care, and designed from the outset around health equity and the priorities identified by autistic people, families, and the wider autism community, emerging information technologies can become valuable components of a more accessible and equitable autism service system across the service and prevention continuum.
