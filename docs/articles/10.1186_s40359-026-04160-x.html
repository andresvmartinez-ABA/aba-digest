Effects of virtual reality exercise on social skills and emotional recognition among children with autism spectrum disorder: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.

Cui, Tiankuo, Ariffin, Rafidah Binti, Wang, Xinyuan et al.  ·  BMC psychology  ·  2026
85/100This meta-analysis directly addresses the clinician's interest in virtual/augmented reality programs for children with autism, focusing on social skills and communication outcomes in the target population.
Summary

This meta-analysis examined the effects of virtual reality (VR) exercise on social skills and emotional recognition in children with autism spectrum disorder. The researchers conducted a systematic search of four databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and EBSCO) from January 2005 to October 2025, including randomized controlled trials that compared VR exercise interventions with standard treatment in children with ASD. The results showed that VR exercise significantly improved social skills (SMD = 0.94) and emotional recognition (SMD = 0.42) compared to control groups, with longer interventions (>14 weeks) producing greater social skills improvements than shorter ones (<14 weeks). Additionally, VR exercise demonstrated significant improvements in cognitive function and reductions in anxiety, though effects on language function and depression remained unclear.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a condition that is characterised by repetitive behaviours and issues with social communication. Over the years, the number of individuals diagnosed with the condition has revealed a significant increase, which is a public health issue that needs to be urgently addressed [1]. Such individuals diagnosed with ASD usually lack the ability to first interpret and process the emotional behaviours of other individuals and face substantial challenges in initiating and maintaining any form of social contact. Notably, emotional recognition (ER) and social skill (SS) are two of the most important areas of concern and are essential deficits when it comes to the overall functioning of people diagnosed with ASD [2]. Such behaviours can also significantly hinder children’s ability to engage in community and school activities, with significant correlations observed with various psychological and behavioural issues. Examples of these issues entail impulsivity and anxiety [3].

One novel method in achieving enhanced ER and SS within children with ASD revolves around the use of virtual reality (VR). This novel technology can help children to practice social situations repeatedly without feeling anxiety about real-life social failures [4]. Specifically, this outcome can be achieved by providing a secure, manageable, immersive setting compared to conventional interventions. The VR technology may also demonstrate improved engagement in the intervention when content customisation is done for different children’s cognitive abilities and interests [5]. Simultaneously, VR exercise can harness the neuroplastic effects of physical activity, with moderate exercise facilitating the release of neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors in critical brain areas. The amygdala and prefrontal cortex are major examples of these brain areas, and performing this process can increase the executive functions crucial for SS and ER [6, 7].

VR exercise stands out for its unique integration of physical activity with immersive virtual scenarios, setting it apart from other virtual reality intervention paradigms [8]. Therapy-based VR focuses on functional restoration rather than the physiological adaptations induced by exercise [9]. In contrast, VR exercise combines the neuroplasticity effects of moderate physical activity by facilitating the release of neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors in critical brain areas and enabling low-anxiety social simulation [10, 11]. This dual advantage addresses both the social-emotional deficits and relevant physiological functions in children with ASD, yet existing research often conflates VR exercise with non-exercise VR interventions, leading to insufficient understanding of its specific effects on social skills and emotional recognition.

Most research concerning children with ASD has still not effectively examined the present effects of VR exercise on SS and ER, with positive relationships being reported between them in certain studies [12, 13]. Conversely, some studies have demonstrated no significant impact from VR exercise [14, 15]. Mittal et al. also already examined the effects of VR on SS and ER in children with ASD [16]. Nevertheless, the reported meta-analysis excluded exploring VR exercise in intervention programmes, which this review aimed to investigate. The study also possessed a limited search breadth, with only four studies included, and the evaluation indicators were not comprehensive enough. Therefore, this study mainly evaluated the effects of VR exercise on SS and ER in children with ASD through meta-analysis, with additional secondary indicators including cognitive function (CF), language function (LF), anxiety, and depression.

Methods

PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and EBSCO databases were searched from January 2005 to October 2025. The search strategy encompassed the following keywords: virtual reality, autism spectrum disorder, and children. Randomized controlled trials recruiting children with ASD and comparing VR exercise with standard treatment were included.

Results

Resultantly, compared with the control group, VR exercise could enhance SS (SMD, 0.94 [0.71, 1.17], p < 0.05, I2 = 74%) and ER (SMD, 0.42 [0.18, 0.65], p < 0.05, I2 = 0%). The subgroup analysis revealed that interventions lasting less than 14 weeks (SMD, 0.63 [0.36, 0.91], p < 0.05, I2 = 0%) and those lasting more than 14 weeks (SMD, 1.70 [1.27, 2.13], p < 0.05, I2 = 44%) could substantially improve SS. In addition, VR exercise has improved cognitive function (SMD, 0.49 [0.06, 0.93], p < 0.05, I2 = 0%) and reduced anxiety (SMD, −0.56 [−1.10, −0.02], p < 0.05, I2 = 0%).

Discussion

VR exercise can significantly improve children’s SS and ER, and has a moderate and cautious explanatory effect on improving cognitive function and reducing anxiety. Nonetheless, the effects on language function and depression remain unclear. VR exercise may be a potential intervention for children with ASD.
