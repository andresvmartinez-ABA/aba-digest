Are social robots more interesting than humans? Quantitative assessment of Joint Attention in autistic and typically developing children

Silvia, Annunziata, Fassina, Gabriele, Brivio, Michelle et al.  ·  Asian Journal of Psychiatry  ·  2025
85/100 This study directly aligns with the profile's interest in social robots, technology-based interventions for children with autism, social skills outcomes in preschool populations, and provides empirical data on robot-assisted therapeutic interventions rather than being purely theoretical.
Summary

This observational case-control study investigated joint attention (JA) behaviors in 10 preschool children with ASD and 16 typically developing toddlers matched for developmental age when interacting with both human agents and social robots. Participants completed two joint attention tasks (Gaze Following and Object Spectacle) administered by both a human examiner and the NAO social robot, with gaze tracking technology used to measure five quantitative indicators including response frequency, latency, and fixation time. The results showed that typically developing children responded more consistently to human social cues and demonstrated greater visual interest in human agents across both conditions, while children with ASD showed comparable levels of attention to the robot across groups. The findings suggest that children with ASD may exhibit greater attentional preference for robot agents compared to human agents, which the authors propose could be leveraged for robot-assisted therapeutic interventions.

Abstract

INTRODUCTION Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is characterized by deficits in social interaction and communication, including joint attention (JA) impairment. This observational case-control study explores quantitative JA metrics in ASD preschool-aged children and typically-developing (TD) toddlers, interacting with both a human agent and a social robot.

MATERIAL AND METHODS Ten ASD and sixteen Typically Developing (TD) children, matched for developmental age, participated completing two JA tasks of the "Échelle par la Communication Sociale Precoce"(ECSP)-Gaze Following and Object Spectacle-administered by both a human (ECSP-H) and a social robot NAO (ECSP-R). A Kinect Azure Camera was used to track children's gaze and five performance indicators were defined to quantify JA-related behaviors: Number of Responses to JA, Time Latency to response and Fixation Time, both towards robot and human agent.

RESULTS Significant group differences emerged in responsiveness to social cues, particularly in the Gaze Following task. TD children responded more consistently to human prompts (p = 0.01) and showed greater visual interest in the human agent in both ECSP-H (p < 0.01) and ECSP-R (p = 0.02) conditions. In contrast, percentage of fixations on the robot was comparable across groups.

DISCUSSION This study introduces unobtrusive, gaze-based quantitative measures to assess joint attention in ASD. Significant differences between ASD and TD groups suggest that children with ASD exhibited greater attentional preference to the robot agent, giving potential for these metrics in distinguishing attentional patterns in these two populations. This feature may nonetheless be clinically relevant, as it may be leveraged to facilitate attention, promote participation, and support robot-assisted therapeutic interventions.
