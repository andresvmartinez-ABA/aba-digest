Evaluating the Effects of Skill-Based Treatment: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Single-Case Studies.

Nathan Bunyan, Clarissa Feuerer, Athanasios Vostanis  ·  Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders  ·  2026

Summary

# Summary: Skill-Based Treatment for Challenging Behavior This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to investigate the application of skill-based treatment (SBT)—an intervention combining functional communication training with delay and denial tolerance training—across diverse populations, settings, and target behaviors, while evaluating its effectiveness and social validity in reducing challenging behaviors. The researchers searched seven databases, extracted data from 28 studies representing 99 individual SBT applications, and assessed literature quality using the Evaluative Method and Single Case Design Risk of Bias Tool, with outcomes quantified using Baseline-Corrected Tau (Tau-BC). SBT demonstrated good reduction in challenging behavior with a mean Tau-BC of 0.47 (range: −0.35 to 1.00), and studies provided evidence supporting the social validity of the intervention; however, quality ratings indicated varying literature quality, with 37% rated as weak, 43% as borderline-adequate, 13% as adequate, and only 7% as borderline-strong. The authors conclude that SBT is an emerging evidence-based practice for addressing challenging behavior, though they recommend strengthening the literature base and note that most applications occurred in controlled settings with skilled implementers. BCBAs should recognize SBT as a potentially evidence-based practice while being mindful that generalization to less controlled community or home settings requires further investigation, and practitioners should consider implementing SBT with attention to the quality standards identified in this review.

Abstract

PURPOSE: The skill-based treatment for challenging behaviour (SBT) is an intervention package that combines functional communication training with contingency-based delay and denial tolerance training. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to (a) investigate the application of SBT across diverse populations, setting, and target behaviours; and (b) to evaluate the effectiveness and social validity of SBT in reducing challenging behaviours. METHODS: This review was pre-registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024561455). Seven databases were searched. Data were extracted on characteristics of studies, participants, and applications of SBT. Literature was assessed for quality using the Evaluative Method and Single Case Design Risk of Bias Tool. SBT outcomes were quantified with Baseline-Corrected Tau (Tau-BC). RESULTS: A total of 28 studies were reviewed, representing 99 individual SBT applications. SBT has been applied across participants with varying diagnoses, challenging behaviours, and levels of ability, though mostly in controlled settings by skilled implementers. Studies provided evidence for the social validity of SBT. Baseline-Corrected Tau indicated SBT shows good reduction in challenging behaviour (M = 0.47, range: - 0.35-1.00). The Evaluative Method indicated the literature to be of varying quality (borderline-strong = 7%; adequate = 13%; borderline-adequate = 43%; weak = 37%), and the evidence-based practice score suggests SBT to be a potentially evidence-based practice. CONCLUSION: The evaluative method indicates SBT to be an emerging evidence-based practice for addressing challenging behaviour, although the majority of the literature scored lower than adequate. We make recommendations for strengthening the literature base and outline key implications for practitioners.

Introduction

Challenging behaviours can impact the safety and quality of life of the person and those around them and may result in responses that are restrictive or exclusionary (Emerson et al.,2014; Holden & Gitlesen,2006). Increased presentation of challenging behaviours correlates with communication difficulties (e.g., Holden & Gitlesen,2006), and research indicates that challenging behaviours often serve a communicative function (Melanson & Fahmie,2023). Therefore, behavioural interventions are often directed towards teaching communication skills that reduce or replace the need for challenging behaviour (e.g., Carr & Durand,1985) along with additional ecological manipulations (Gore et al.,2013).

One such intervention that has received great attention in recent years is skill-based treatment (SBT). First introduced by Hanley et al. (2014), SBT is purported to be an efficient, effective, and safe way to address challenging behaviours (Jessel et al.,2018,2022). The SBT intervention package comprises two core components: functional communication training (FCT) and delay and denial tolerance training. FCT is one of the most widely researched behavioural interventions, with a growing number of systematic reviews and meta-analyses attesting to its efficacy in addressing challenging behaviour (Chezan et al.,2018; Gerow et al.,2018; Heath et al.,2015). In FCT, the interventionist teaches an alternative functional communication response (FCR) that fulfils the same function as the challenging behaviour (Carr & Durand,1985). Because this FCR is initially on a dense schedule of reinforcement, the schedule must then be thinned to increase the likelihood of the outcomes being maintained under natural conditions (Hagopian et al.,2011; Muharib et al.,2019; Tiger et al.,2008). Put simply, while initially the FCR achieves its consequence every time, this is gradually reduced to a level that is feasible to sustain in the person’s daily life. In SBT, the method of schedule thinning is referred to as delay and denial tolerance training. The following sections will outline the SBT process in more detail.

Methods

This review was pre-registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024561455). Seven databases were searched. Data were extracted on characteristics of studies, participants, and applications of SBT. Literature was assessed for quality using the Evaluative Method and Single Case Design Risk of Bias Tool. SBT outcomes were quantified with Baseline-Corrected Tau (Tau-BC).

Results

A total of 28 studies were reviewed, representing 99 individual SBT applications. SBT has been applied across participants with varying diagnoses, challenging behaviours, and levels of ability, though mostly in controlled settings by skilled implementers. Studies provided evidence for the social validity of SBT. Baseline-Corrected Tau indicated SBT shows good reduction in challenging behaviour (M= 0.47, range: − 0.35–1.00). The Evaluative Method indicated the literature to be of varying quality (borderline-strong = 7%; adequate = 13%; borderline-adequate = 43%; weak = 37%), and the evidence-based practice score suggests SBT to be a potentially evidence-based practice.

Discussion

The evaluative method indicates SBT to be an emerging evidence-based practice for addressing challenging behaviour, although the majority of the literature scored lower than adequate. We make recommendations for strengthening the literature base and outline key implications for practitioners.
