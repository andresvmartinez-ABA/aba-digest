Serious Games for Education and Intervention in Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Scoping Review of Learning Theories, AI, VR, XR, and Robotics

Summary

# Summary: Serious Games for Education and Intervention in Autism Spectrum Disorder This scoping review aimed to map the literature on Serious Games for individuals with ASD, examining which learning theories and pedagogical approaches underpin their design and how emerging technologies (AI, VR, XR, and robotics) are integrated into such interventions. The researchers conducted a systematic search across four major databases (Scopus, ERIC, Web of Science, and ACM Digital Library) from January 2005 to June 2025, resulting in 40 studies included in the formal evidence base, which were analyzed through descriptive and thematic synthesis organized by theoretical foundations, intervention characteristics, technological modalities, and reported outcomes. Key findings revealed substantial theoretical diversity underpinning Serious Game design—including constructivism, socio-cultural theory, social learning theory, self-determination theory, and behaviorism—with AI primarily supporting personalization and adaptive feedback, VR/XR enabling immersive and controlled practice environments, and robotics facilitating structured embodied interaction. Reported challenges limiting clinical application include accessibility barriers, high costs, privacy concerns, cybersickness, methodological inconsistencies across studies, and insufficient evidence regarding transfer and maintenance of learned skills. For BCBA practitioners, these findings suggest that while Serious Games show promise as complementary intervention tools, further empirical investigation is needed to establish efficacy, generalization outcomes, and implementation feasibility before widespread adoption into practice.

Abstract

Serious Games (SGs) have increasingly been investigated as tools for education and intervention for individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), while emerging technologies such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), Virtual Reality (VR), Extended Reality (XR), and Human–Robot Interaction (HRI) are expanding their potential for personalization, immersion, and adaptive interaction. However, the literature remains fragmented across pedagogical theory, Serious Game design, and technology-focused interventions, making it difficult to understand how these dimensions are connected within the ASD context. This scoping review therefore maps the available literature on Serious Games for individuals with ASD, focusing on two complementary questions: (RQ1) Which learning theories, pedagogical approaches, and educational or developmental outcomes underpin the design and implementation of Serious Games for individuals with ASD? and (RQ2) How are intelligent and immersive technologies, including AI, VR, XR, and HRI/robotics, integrated into Serious Games, and what benefits and challenges are reported? A structured literature search was conducted across Scopus, ERIC, Web of Science, and the ACM Digital Library, with Google Scholar consulted as a supplementary source, covering publications from January 2005 to June 2025. Following duplicate removal, title/abstract screening, and full-text assessment, 40 studies constituted the formal evidence base. The evidence was organized through descriptive and thematic synthesis, with studies mapped according to theoretical foundations, Serious Game or intervention characteristics, technological modalities, and reported outcomes. The review identified substantial theoretical diversity, including constructivism, socio-cultural theory, social learning theory, self-determination theory, behaviorism, developmental approaches, and intervention-oriented frameworks. AI was mainly associated with personalization, learner modeling, behavioral analysis, and adaptive feedback, whereas VR/XR primarily supported immersive and controlled environments for experiential learning and skills practice. Robotics and HRI were associated with structured and embodied interaction. Reported challenges included accessibility, cost, privacy, cybersickness, methodological heterogeneity, and limited evidence concerning transfer and maintenance of skills. Overall, the review provides a structured overview of the field and indicates priorities for further empirical investigation.
