Immersive simulation interventions for children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder: technologies, cognitive and socio-emotional development, and AI-driven emotion estimation: a systematic review

Mohammad Mominur Rahman, Khansa Chemnad, Marwa Al-Assi, Areej Babiker, Achraf Othman  ·  Frontiers in Virtual Reality  ·  2026
TECHNOLOGY IN ABA
78/100Highly relevant systematic review directly addressing technology-based interventions (VR/AR/XR) and AI-assisted tools for children and adolescents with ASD, with focus on social-emotional and communication outcomes, though limited direct connection to core ABA methodology and functional assessment approaches central to the profile.
Summary

# Summary This systematic review synthesized evidence from 141 studies (2015–2025) to examine the effectiveness of immersive technologies—including Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), Mixed Reality (MR), and Extended Reality (XR)—as interventions for children and adolescents with Autism Spectrum Disorder. The methodology involved searching seven major databases and evaluating studies using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) to assess methodological quality across qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods designs. The review found that immersive interventions—predominantly VR-based with increasing integration of AI-driven emotion recognition and adaptive feedback—produced improvements in social-emotional skills, cognitive abilities, daily living skills, motor coordination, and adaptive behaviors, with studies frequently reporting large effect sizes (d > 0.5) and machine-learning models achieving up to 97% accuracy in emotion recognition; however, evidence quality was substantially limited by small sample sizes (>80% of studies with <30 participants), non-randomized designs, lack of long-term follow-up, underrepresentation of females and lower-functioning individuals, and inconsistent outcome reporting. For BCBA practitioners, these findings suggest that immersive technologies show promise as adjunctive intervention tools for skill development in ASD; however, current evidence is insufficient to support broad clinical adoption without larger, rigorous comparative studies, standardized outcome measures, and investigation of generalization across settings and over time. Future implementation by BCBAs should prioritize technologies supported by robust evidence, remain cautious regarding small-sample findings, and emphasize the need for long-term outcome tracking and cultural adaptation for diverse populations.

Abstract

Virtual Reality (VR) and related immersive technologies are increasingly being explored as innovative intervention tools for children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), offering controlled, repeatable, and engaging environments for skill development.

This systematic review synthesizes evidence from 141 studies (2015 –2025) examining the use of immersive technologies, Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), Mixed Reality (MR), and Extended Reality (XR) for interventions targeting children and adolescents with ASD. The review addresses key research questions regarding participant characteristics, technology types, AI-enabled features, comparator conditions, therapeutic outcomes, and methodological quality.

Findings indicate that immersive interventions are predominantly VR-based, with growing integration of AI-driven emotion recognition, adaptive feedback, and multimodal sensing (e.g., eye-tracking, motion capture). Studies report improvements in social-emotional skills, cognitive abilities, daily living skills, motor coordination, and adaptive behaviors, with quantitative gains frequently evidenced by large effect sizes (e.g., d > 0.5) and machine-learning models achieving up to 97% accuracy in emotion recognition, though evidence is often limited by small sample sizes, non-randomized designs, and inconsistent outcome reporting. Geographically, research is concentrated in North America, Europe, and parts of Asia, highlighting global disparities in access and implementation.

The review identifies significant methodological gaps, including a lack of long-term and generalization studies, underrepresentation of females and lower-functioning individuals, and limited use of rigorous comparative designs. Future research should prioritize larger, more diverse samples, standardized outcome measures, longitudinal designs, and ethically informed AI integration to advance the field toward evidence-based, scalable, and inclusive immersive interventions for ASD.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition affecting an estimated 1 in 127 persons worldwide in 2021 (2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD), 2024). Children with ASD show impairments across multiple domains, including social communication and interaction, motor abilities, attention, and emotion recognition (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). These difficulties can affect their self-concept, interpersonal relationships, and capacity for independent living (Martí-Vilar et al., 2023). This unique combination of characteristics makes each autistic individual distinct and sometimes challenging to understand and support. These differences significantly influence their social participation (Taheri et al., 2016) and affect the daily lives of their families (Karst and Van Hecke, 2012;Tonkin et al., 2014). We believe that in an inclusive society, all children and their families should have access to the same activities and opportunities to participate and interact with their community members. Although social participation and leisure activities are recognized as fundamental rights, they also contribute significantly to the well-being of children and their families (Abbott, 2006;Coyl-Shepherd and Hanlon, 2013;Menear and Neumeier, 2015). Conversely, limited participation increases the risk of additional social and psychological challenges for autistic individuals (Kawachi and Berkman, 2001). To promote an inclusive society, community activities should engage all members, including families, while supporting the development of individual competencies that enable autistic children to participate actively, meet their sensory, emotional, and social needs, and enhance communication, emotional regulation, and social skills.

, immersive technology is an effective tool for encouraging autistic students to learn and develop new skills. A variety of immersive technologies, such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), mixed reality (MR), and extended reality (XR), can promote skill development and social engagement. They are effective mainly because they can create immersion. Immersion refers to how real the experience feels, which leads to a strong sense of presence. This presence is a mental and emotional state that makes the user feel as if they are truly inside the virtual environment (

). Immersive technologies encompass several distinct modalities that can be positioned along

Virtual reality (VR): Fully or predominantly computer-generated environments that largely replace the user’s perception of the physical world, providing a high level of immersion and presence.

Augmented reality (AR): Digital information or virtual objects are overlaid onto the real-world environment while maintaining the user’s awareness of and interaction with the surrounding physical space (Van Krevelen and Poelman, 2010).

Mixed reality (MR): Physical and virtual objects coexist and interact dynamically in real time, enabling users to engage simultaneously with both real and digital elements within a shared environment (Rebbani et al., 2021).

Extended reality (XR) or xReality: An umbrella term encompassing VR, AR, MR, and related immersive technologies that extend human perception and interaction beyond conventional physical environments (Khan, 2023). XR represents a broad conceptual framework rather than a distinct technology itself. Some authors further extend this concept through terms such as *R or All Reality to describe a wider spectrum of present and future reality-mediating technologies (Khan, 2023).

VR technologies vary in how deeply they immerse the user, supported by work showing that immersion ranges from basic screen-based systems to fully surrounding virtual environments (Beck et al., 2019). At the lowest level, non-immersive VR relies on standard computer screens with limited sensory engagement. Semi-immersive setups, such as large projections or multi-screen displays, enhance presence through wider visual fields (Castronovo et al., 2013). Fully immersive VR (IVR), most notably head-mounted displays, delivers synchronized stereoscopic images that respond to head movement, creating a compelling 3D experience (Wenk et al., 2023). These systems require real-time, fluid rendering to maintain comfort and prevent issues such as motion sickness, underscoring the balance between visual realism and performance.

In recent years, IVR has shown genuinely promising potential for supporting children and adolescents with ASD, especially in the area of social communication. A growing body of systematic reviews and meta-analyses reinforces this trend. For example,Mittal et al. (2024)reported notable gains in social skills (SMD = 1.43; 95% CI: 0.01–2.84), emotional understanding (SMD = 2.45; 95% CI: 0.21–4.18), and broader cognitive abilities following VR-based interventions. Similarly,Yang et al. (2025), who reviewed 14 studies, found consistently positive effects, with high-functioning autistic individuals appearing to benefit the most from immersive environments. Not only social skills (Cheng et al., 2015;Ip et al., 2018;Lorenzo et al., 2013;Matsentidou and Poullis, 2014) but also related studies show the potential of IVR interventions for ASD in various areas, such as emotion recognition (Ip et al., 2018;Lorenzo et al., 2013), daily living skills (Adjorlu et al., 2017), vocational skills (Bozgeyikli et al., 2017), and phobias (Maskey et al., 2014;Maskey et al., 2019). Overall, these findings emphasize that VR can serve as a valuable and evidence-based tool for improving essential developmental skills in people with ASD.

Several systematic reviews have examined VR-based interventions for children and adolescents with ASD over the past decade.Mesa-Gresa et al. (2018)provided one of the earliest evidence-based reviews, integrating clinical and technical databases and reporting moderate support for VR treatments, although they did not differentiate immersive from non-immersive technologies or focus specifically on children and adolescents. They combined clinical and some technical databases, but they did not include Scopus (the largest multidisciplinary database for tracking citations) or IEEE Xplore (the primary source for VR/AR/MR engineering-focused studies).Pivotto et al. (2024)explored cave automatic virtual environment (CAVE)-based immersive environments, highlighting benefits such as multi-participant engagement, sensory comfort, and social skills development, but their review was limited to CAVE systems and recreational applications, without assessing other immersive technologies or broader outcomes. They conducted a broad search across seven databases, including APA PsycINFO, ERIC, ProQuest Advanced Technologies, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science; however, they did not specifically include IEEE Xplore.

Li et al. (2023)conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of social and affective skill training, summarizing intervention effectiveness while noting limited theoretical adaptation for VR, but they did not examine the design features, technological affordances, or alignment with participant characteristics. They focused on clinical and biomedical databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane, and Embase; however, they did not include IEEE Xplore or PsycINFO (psychology/behavior-focused research relevant to ASD interventions).Gandall and Sanchez Peral (2025)focused on self-regulation and interoceptive skills in children with ASD, emphasizing human factors in VR design and did not consider broader skill domains, strict inclusion criteria, and comparator groups. Their reviews searched PubMed, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar to capture clinical and psychological studies; they did not include Scopus, Web of Science, or IEEE Xplore, limiting the coverage of highly cited, interdisciplinary, and technology-focused research.

Han et al. (2025)synthesized VR applications for social communication disorders, including ASD, examining usability, gaze tracking, and inclusive education. Immersive technology types and intervention design features were not systematically investigated. They searched through PubMed, Scopus, ERIC, CINAHL, and PsycINFO to cover biomedical, educational, and psychological studies but did not include IEEE Xplore or Web of Science. Finally, the most recent study byAzman et al. (2025)reviewed VR-based social skills training for high-functioning ASD, reporting gains in social interaction, cognition, and nonverbal communication. The review was limited by the small number of studies and lack of analysis of technological features or comparator conditions. Their systematic review searched PubMed, Cochrane, Scopus, and Embase to capture clinical and biomedical studies but did not include IEEE Xplore or PsycINFO.

Collectively, these reviews show the potential of VR interventions for children and adolescents with ASD. However, important gaps remain. These include the need for a systematic inclusion of various immersive technologies, a detailed examination of technological and sensory features, complete reporting of intervention design, careful characterization of participant profiles, and consideration of suitable comparison groups. To address this gap, this study plans to conduct a systematic review using multiple high-quality databases. These include PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and IEEE Xplore. The goal is to ensure thorough coverage of both health-related outcomes and technological advancements in VR, AR, MR, and XR interventions for children and adolescents with ASD.

Methods

The studies were assessed for their methodological quality using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), version 2018 (Hong et al., 2018). This tool is validated for evaluating the quality of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies. Each study was classified based on its design type: qualitative, quantitative randomized controlled trial (RCT), quantitative non-randomized, quantitative descriptive, or mixed methods. They were then evaluated using the five relevant criteria. Individual study MMAT scores are summarized inFigure 9, and the complete list of MMAT questions, organized by study design, is provided inSupplementary Appendix E. This assessment allowed for systematic, comparable evaluation of methodological quality across diverse study designs and informed the interpretation of results regarding the robustness of the findings.

Discussion

The observed temporal growth in VR/AR/MR/XR research involving children and adolescents with ASD reflects a broader maturation of immersive technologies and their increasing accessibility within clinical and educational contexts. The marked increase in publications after 2019, with peaks in 2022 and 2024, coincides with advances in consumer-grade head-mounted displays, improved motion tracking, and the integration of intelligent adaptive features (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Alvarado et al., 2023;Amat et al., 2021;Cao et al., 2025;Ide-Okochi et al., 2024;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Sanku et al., 2023;Sayis et al., 2020), making immersive interventions more feasible for pediatric neurodevelopmental populations (Carreon et al., 2024a;Cecil et al., 2017;Junaidi et al., 2020;Malihi et al., 2020a). This trend also aligns with heightened interest in technology-mediated interventions following the COVID-19 pandemic, during which remote, simulated, and technology-supported therapeutic approaches gained prominence (Al Kheraif et al., 2024;Alcañiz et al., 2022;Bozgeyikli et al., 2018;Carreon et al., 2024a;Smith et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings suggest a shift from exploratory feasibility studies toward more structured intervention research (Akhtar and Guha, 2019;Boyd et al., 2022;Koirala et al., 2021;Schwarze et al., 2019;Tran, 2024) although the sustained variability in annual output indicates that the field is still evolving rather than fully consolidated (Herrero and Lorenzo, 2020;Krishnan et al., 2024;Zhang et al., 2015).

Geographically, the concentration of studies in North America, Europe, and parts of Asia highlights substantial regional disparities in research capacity and adoption of immersive technologies for ASD interventions (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Alvarado et al., 2023;Amat et al., 2021;Carreon et al., 2024a;Carreon et al., 2024a;Jeyarani et al., 2024;Junaidi et al., 2020;Koirala et al., 2019;Maskey et al., 2019;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Sanku et al., 2023;Sayis et al., 2020;Shahidi et al., 2023;Thomsen and Adjorlu, 2021;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Yeh and Meng, 2020;Zhao et al., 2016;Zhao et al., 2017). The dominance of the United States, China, Spain, and India likely reflects a combination of technological infrastructure, research funding availability, and established ASD research networks (Jeyarani et al., 2024;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2016;Zhao et al., 2017). In contrast, the limited representation from Africa, South America, and Oceania underscores persistent global inequities in access to advanced therapeutic technologies and research dissemination (Amat et al., 2021;Carreon et al., 2024a;Maskey et al., 2019;Sayis et al., 2020;Thomsen and Adjorlu, 2021). This imbalance raises concerns regarding the cultural, contextual, and socioeconomic generalizability of current evidence, as intervention design, usability, and acceptability may vary significantly across regions (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Carreon et al., 2024a;Junaidi et al., 2020;Koirala et al., 2019). The underrepresentation of low- and middle-income countries also suggests that existing findings may not fully capture implementation challenges related to cost, technical support, or contextual appropriateness (Alvarado et al., 2023;Yeh and Meng, 2020;Zhao et al., 2017).

The participant characteristics reported across the included studies reveal substantial heterogeneity and several recurring methodological limitations. The predominance of small-scale samples, with more than 80% of studies involving fewer than 30 participants, reflects the logistical, financial, and recruitment challenges associated with immersive technology research in pediatric ASD populations (Amat et al., 2021;Jeyarani et al., 2024;Koirala et al., 2021;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Sayis et al., 2020;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Thomsen and Adjorlu, 2021;Tokarskaya et al., 2020;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2016;Zhao et al., 2017). This imbalance raises concerns regarding the external validity of the current evidence base, as the usability, acceptability, and therapeutic response to immersive interventions may differ across sexes, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to the broader ASD population. While such sample sizes are common in early-stage or feasibility-oriented VR research, they constrain statistical power and limit the generalizability of findings (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Alvarado et al., 2023;Carreon et al., 2024a;Frolli et al., 2021;Junaidi et al., 2020;Koirala et al., 2019;López-Bouzas et al., 2024;Porayska-Pomsta et al., 2018). The strong male predominance mirrors known ASD prevalence patterns but also highlights the continued underrepresentation of female participants, which may obscure gender-specific responses to immersive interventions (Amat et al., 2021;Carreon et al., 2024a;Frolli et al., 2022;Koirala et al., 2021;Narayanan, 2019;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tokarskaya et al., 2020;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2017).

Although most studies employed established diagnostic tools (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Alvarado et al., 2023;Amat et al., 2021;Carreon et al., 2024a;Frolli et al., 2022;Jeyarani et al., 2024;Koirala et al., 2021;López-Bouzas et al., 2024;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Porayska-Pomsta et al., 2018;Sanku et al., 2023;Sayis et al., 2020;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Thomsen and Adjorlu, 2021;Tokarskaya et al., 2020;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Yeh and Meng, 2020;Zhao et al., 2016;Zhao et al., 2017), reporting of ASD severity, functional level, and comorbid conditions was often inconsistent or incomplete (Amat et al., 2021;Carreon et al., 2024a;Frolli et al., 2022;Jeyarani et al., 2024;Koirala et al., 2021;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tokarskaya et al., 2020;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2017). Given the high prevalence of co-occurring neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions in ASD, this lack of systematic reporting limits interpretability and hampers cross-study comparisons (Amat et al., 2021;Frolli et al., 2022;Koirala et al., 2021;Narayanan, 2019;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tokarskaya et al., 2020;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2017). Exclusion of participants with common comorbidities further narrows the ecological validity of findings and may result in overly optimistic assessments of intervention feasibility or effectiveness (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Alvarado et al., 2023;Carreon et al., 2024a;Junaidi et al., 2020;Sanku et al., 2023;Sayis et al., 2020;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023). Collectively, these results indicate a need for larger, more diverse samples and more standardized reporting of participant characteristics, including comorbidities and functional profiles, to strengthen the evidence base and improve the clinical relevance of immersive technology-based interventions for ASD.

The technological landscape of immersive technology-based interventions for children and adolescents with ASD is substantially diverse, with a clear dominance of immersive VR and HMD-based systems. VR-based interventions dominated the literature, accounting for more than half of all included studies (55.3%), whereas AR (12.8%) and MR (8.5%) were substantially less represented. Consequently, most evidence regarding intervention effectiveness originates from VR-based systems, limiting robust comparisons across immersive technology modalities. Fully immersive HMD-based systems were the predominant technological approach, whereas projection-based and CAVE environments represented only a small proportion of studies (4.3%). This distribution suggests that current evidence primarily reflects highly immersive interventions, with comparatively limited evidence regarding lower-immersion or projection-based approaches. This predominance reflects the relative maturity, affordability, and flexibility of VR platforms such as Oculus and HTC Vive, along with the widespread adoption of game engines such as Unity3D and Unreal Engine, which enable rapid prototyping and customization of therapeutic environments (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Koirala et al., 2021;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Porayska-Pomsta et al., 2018;Sanku et al., 2023;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2017). The comparatively lower uptake of AR and MR technologies suggests that these approaches, while promising for blending real-world context with virtual cues, remain constrained by hardware cost, usability challenges, and limited empirical validation in pediatric ASD populations (Akhtar and Guha, 2019;Amat et al., 2021;Koirala et al., 2019;Samantaray et al., 2024;Schwarze et al., 2019;Zhang et al., 2017;Zhao et al., 2016). Similarly, the smaller number of studies employing haptic-enhanced, projection-based, or CAVE systems highlights ongoing trade-offs between ecological validity and practical feasibility, as these systems often require specialized infrastructure and technical expertise (Akhtar and Guha, 2019;Amat et al., 2021;Junaidi et al., 2020;Koirala et al., 2019;Koirala et al., 2021;Samantaray et al., 2024;Sayis et al., 2020;Tzanavari et al., 2015). The emergence of collaborative virtual environments and sensor-integrated systems indicates a growing shift toward more interactive, socially embedded, and data-rich intervention designs (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Alvarado et al., 2023;Amat et al., 2021;Carreon et al., 2024a;Meng and Yeh, 2022;Narayanan, 2019;Porayska-Pomsta et al., 2018;Sanku et al., 2023;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2017). However, the heterogeneity in hardware configurations and simulation types complicates cross-study comparisons and limits conclusions regarding technology-specific effectiveness (Amat et al., 2021;Ide-Okochi et al., 2024;Junaidi et al., 2020;Koirala et al., 2021;Narayanan, 2019;Schwarze et al., 2019;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Zhao et al., 2017). Overall, these findings suggest that current research prioritizes technological accessibility and immersion over standardization, underscoring the need for clearer reporting, comparative evaluations across technology types, and greater alignment between simulation design and targeted therapeutic objectives.

The increasing integration of AI-enabled components within immersive ASD interventions reflects a shift toward more responsive, personalized, and data-driven therapeutic systems. Emotion recognition, adaptive algorithms, and multimodal behavioral analysis were commonly employed to enhance ecological validity and tailor intervention delivery in real time (Abd El-Sattar, 2025;Bian et al., 2015;Cao et al., 2025;Cecil et al., 2017;Dhamodharan et al., 2020;Ip et al., 2018;Johnston et al., 2022;Junaidi et al., 2020;Minissi et al., 2023;Moya-Albor et al., 2018;Yan et al., 2025;Yeh and Meng, 2020). Compared with conventional immersive interventions that relied primarily on fixed scenarios and pre-programmed feedback, AI-enabled systems offered greater capacity for real-time adaptation based on user behavior, engagement, and performance. This shift suggests a movement from static intervention delivery toward more individualized and responsive therapeutic experiences. These approaches align well with the heterogeneity of ASD presentations, as AI-driven adaptation allows task demands and feedback to be dynamically adjusted to individual performance and engagement levels (Dow et al., 2024;Romadlon Junaidi et al., 2022;Yeh and Meng, 2020;Zhao et al., 2018a).

The frequent use of eye tracking, motion capture, and multimodal sensing further underscores a growing emphasis on fine-grained behavioral measurement, enabling objective assessment of social attention, joint attention, and interaction patterns that are difficult to capture using traditional observational methods. It is important to recognize that the AI-enabled interventions identified in this review represent a continuum of intelligent and adaptive technologies rather than a single homogeneous category. Across studies, AI-related components ranged from machine learning-based emotion recognition and multimodal behavioral analysis to adaptive feedback mechanisms informed by eye tracking and motion-sensing data. Consequently, the sophistication and degree of autonomy of these systems varied considerably. In some cases, sensing technologies primarily supported behavioral monitoring and adaptive feedback rather than advanced artificial intelligence in the strict sense. This heterogeneity complicates direct comparisons and suggests that the current AI evidence base should be interpreted as encompassing varying levels of intelligent functionality and technological maturity.

Another consideration is the validity and reliability of AI-driven emotion recognition and behavioral analysis systems. Given the substantial heterogeneity of ASD presentations, variations in facial expressions, communication styles, sensory profiles, and behavioral characteristics may affect the accuracy and generalizability of AI-based inferences across individuals and contexts. Relatively few studies reported detailed information regarding model training datasets, validation procedures, or algorithmic performance, making it difficult to evaluate reliability, fairness, and robustness. However, despite their promise, AI-enabled features were often implemented in highly bespoke systems, with limited transparency regarding model training, validation, or robustness across participants and contexts (Bian et al., 2015;Cao et al., 2025;Dhamodharan et al., 2020;Ip et al., 2018;Li et al., 2015;Minissi et al., 2023;Moya-Albor et al., 2018;Newbutt et al., 2020;Yuan and Ip, 2018). The limited reporting of training and validation data also raises concerns regarding potential algorithmic bias, particularly if AI models are developed using datasets that are not representative of the broader ASD population. Such biases may reduce system performance for specific subgroups and affect the equity and reliability of intervention delivery.

Few studies evaluated the independent contribution of AI components relative to non-adaptive or rule-based systems, making it difficult to determine whether observed benefits were attributable to intelligent adaptation or to increased immersion more broadly (Cao et al., 2025;Johnston et al., 2022;Minissi et al., 2023;Moya-Albor et al., 2018). Consequently, although AI-enabled interventions appear to enhance personalization and responsiveness, the current evidence base does not permit firm conclusions regarding whether AI-integrated systems consistently outperform comparable non-AI immersive interventions.

Ethical considerations also warrant greater attention. Many AI-enabled immersive systems collect sensitive behavioral, physiological, gaze tracking, and interaction data from children and adolescents, creating important challenges related to privacy, data security, informed consent, and long-term data governance. In addition, some AI-driven adaptive systems operate with limited transparency regarding how decisions or recommendations are generated, potentially reducing interpretability for clinicians, educators, caregivers, and end users. Moreover, the reliance on specialized hardware and computational resources raises concerns about scalability and real-world deployment, particularly in clinical or educational settings with limited technical support (Akhtar and Guha, 2019;Alvarado et al., 2023;Dow et al., 2024;Ip et al., 2016;Miller et al., 2020;Newbutt et al., 2020;Peng et al., 2019;Silva et al., 2025;Tzanavari et al., 2015;Wongwan et al., 2025). Finally, it is important to distinguish between experimental research prototypes and clinically validated intervention tools. While many AI-enabled immersive systems have shown promising preliminary results, most were evaluated in small-scale research settings and have not yet undergone extensive clinical validation or large-scale real-world implementation.

The distribution of comparator conditions across studies highlights a field that remains largely exploratory, with a strong reliance on single-arm and pre–post designs. While these designs are appropriate for early-stage feasibility, usability, and proof-of-concept evaluations, their predominance limits causal inference regarding the effectiveness of immersive VR-based interventions. Within-subject and crossover designs offered improved control over individual variability and consistently demonstrated gains following intervention exposure (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Carreon et al., 2024b;Falivene et al., 2025;Frolli et al., 2022;Junaidi et al., 2020;Malihi et al., 2020a;Naranjo et al., 2017;Nazari et al., 2024;Tokarskaya et al., 2020;Wedyan et al., 2021;Zhang et al., 2020); however, they remain vulnerable to learning effects and temporal confounds. The relatively small proportion of studies using active comparators, treatment-as-usual, or waitlist controls suggests that rigorous efficacy testing against established interventions is still limited.

Comparisons with typically developing peers were primarily used to benchmark baseline performance and characterize ASD-specific profiles rather than evaluating intervention efficacy, which constrains their interpretive value for clinical outcomes. A notable pattern across the evidence base was the predominance of single-arm and pre–post study designs, which accounted for the largest proportion of included studies. While these studies frequently reported improvements relative to baseline, the absence of comparator conditions limits causal inference and increases the possibility that observed gains may reflect factors such as maturation, repeated testing, or participant expectancy effects. Encouragingly, studies that included passive or active control conditions generally reported superior outcomes for VR-based interventions, supporting their potential added value. However, controlled studies represented a substantially smaller proportion of the literature and often varied considerably in comparator type, intervention duration, and outcome assessment. As a result, although positive findings were observed across both controlled and uncontrolled designs, the evidence is generally stronger for studies incorporating active or passive comparison groups than for pre–post evaluations alone. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of comparator types, outcome measures, and intervention targets complicates cross-study synthesis and precludes firm conclusions about relative effectiveness. Overall, these findings underscore the need for more robust comparative designs, including adequately powered randomized controlled trials and standardized comparator frameworks, to advance the field from feasibility toward evidence-based clinical implementation.

The distribution of therapeutic objectives across the reviewed studies underscores the versatility of immersive VR technologies in addressing a wide spectrum of developmental and functional needs in children and adolescents with ASD. The strong emphasis on social and emotional skills reflects longstanding intervention priorities in ASD research and highlights VR’s capacity to simulate socially complex yet controlled environments for practicing interaction, emotion recognition, and regulation (Ju et al., 2024;Koirala et al., 2021;Schwarze et al., 2019;Thomsen and Adjorlu, 2021). Cognitive, educational, and daily living skill interventions further demonstrate the adaptability of immersive systems for supporting learning, independence, and real-world task rehearsal, particularly where repetition and contextual realism are critical (Al-Saddi et al., 2023;Amat et al., 2021;Boo et al., 2022;Lu et al., 2018;Sanku et al., 2023). Across therapeutic domains, a broadly consistent pattern emerged: immersive interventions were associated with improvements in the specific skills targeted by the intervention. Positive findings were reported for social communication, emotional understanding, cognitive performance, educational outcomes, daily living skills, and motor-related competencies, suggesting that the potential benefits of immersive technologies are not confined to a single developmental domain. However, the strength of evidence varied across domains, with social and emotional interventions supported by a larger and more mature evidence base than educational, motor, or daily living applications. Across domains, reported outcomes were generally positive, suggesting gains in targeted skills, engagement, and confidence; however, outcome measures were often task-specific and short-term, with limited evidence of sustained effects or real-world generalization (Fan et al., 2015;Koirala et al., 2019;Maskey et al., 2019;Moon et al., 2024;Porayska-Pomsta et al., 2018;Shahidi et al., 2023;Tokarskaya et al., 2020;Wade et al., 2015;Wedyan et al., 2021;Zhao et al., 2016).

Despite consistent positive findings across domains, comparisons of effectiveness remain challenging because studies differed substantially in intervention duration, outcome measures, participant characteristics, and methodological quality. Consequently, it is difficult to determine whether certain domains benefit more from immersive technologies than others. Importantly, the breadth of intervention targets contrasts with the relatively narrow focus of many individual studies, which typically examine single skill domains in isolation. This fragmentation limits understanding of how improvements in one area may transfer to broader functional outcomes or quality of life. Moreover, reliance on heterogeneous outcome measures complicates cross-study comparison and synthesis (Boyd et al., 2022;Jeyarani et al., 2024;Ju et al., 2024;Salimi et al., 2024;Schwarze et al., 2019;Zhao et al., 2017;Zhao et al., 2018a).

Collectively, these findings indicate that while VR-based interventions show promise across multiple therapeutic domains, future research should prioritize longitudinal designs, standardized outcome frameworks, and evaluation of generalization to everyday contexts to better establish clinical significance and real-world impact. Importantly, many included studies primarily evaluated feasibility, usability, engagement, or proof-of-concept outcomes rather than therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, although positive findings were frequently reported, these results should be interpreted cautiously and should not necessarily be considered evidence of sustained or clinically meaningful treatment effects.

The predominance of non-randomized and small-scale quantitative designs highlights a critical methodological limitation in immersive technology-based ASD intervention research. Although feasibility and exploratory studies are essential for testing novel technologies, the limited number of randomized controlled trials constrains the ability to draw causal inferences regarding intervention effectiveness. Moreover, the underrepresentation of female participants, lower-functioning individuals, and participants with co-occurring conditions indicates that current evidence may not generalize across the broader autism spectrum. Longitudinal evaluation and real-world generalization remain largely unexplored, with few studies assessing whether skill gains persist beyond controlled settings or transfer to everyday contexts such as schools, homes, and community environments. The heterogeneity of outcome measures, task designs, and usability assessments further complicates cross-study synthesis and meta-analytic integration. Technological and usability constraints, including hardware limitations, sensory tolerability, and underutilization of adaptive, multimodal systems, limit the potential for personalized intervention delivery. Collectively, these patterns underscore the need for more rigorous, standardized, and ecologically valid research designs that leverage advanced immersive technologies while ensuring inclusive and generalizable findings across diverse ASD populations.

The quality assessment reveals a clear tension between feasibility and rigor in immersive technology-based ASD research. While a minority of studies met high methodological standards (MMAT Grade A; low RoB), most had a high risk of bias and moderate to low quality, reflecting common challenges such as small sample sizes, non-randomized designs, and heterogeneity in outcome measures. This variability underscores the difficulty of balancing technological innovation with rigorous evaluation, particularly in complex immersive interventions involving children and adolescents with diverse abilities and co-occurring conditions. Importantly, the presence of high-quality studies demonstrates that methodologically robust VR research is achievable, providing exemplary design, reporting, and multimodal assessment strategies.

Overall, across immersive modalities, the findings suggest a general trend toward positive outcomes; however, the strength and consistency of evidence vary substantially by technology type, study design, and outcome domain. VR-based HMD systems dominate the evidence base and therefore disproportionately shape the overall conclusions, while AR, MR, and projection-based systems remain underrepresented, limiting meaningful cross-modality inference. Importantly, differences in immersion level, interactivity, sensory complexity, and system adaptability (including AI integration) were rarely isolated as independent variables, making it difficult to determine whether observed effects are driven by modality-specific mechanisms or by shared characteristics of immersive engagement. Furthermore, the predominance of small-scale, feasibility-oriented, non-randomized, and single-arm studies, together with the moderate-to-high risk of bias identified in many studies, limits the strength of causal inference and necessitates cautious interpretation of reported benefits. Consequently, current evidence supports cautious optimism regarding immersive interventions for ASD but does not yet allow definitive conclusions about which technologies, configurations, or design features are most effective across participant profiles and developmental domains.
