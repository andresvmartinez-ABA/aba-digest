The Relationship Between the Use of Speech-Generating Devices and Verbal Requests in Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder and Intellectual Disabilities.

Moeka Kaneda, Ginga Sasaki  ·  Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders  ·  2025
AAC & SGD
78/100Highly relevant study on AAC/speech-generating devices with school-age children with autism, examining functional communication training, delay tolerance, and reinforcement contingencies—core interests in the profile.
Summary

# Research Summary This study aimed to investigate whether removing synthetic speech output from speech-generating devices (SGDs) and incorporating delayed reinforcement would increase verbal requests in children with autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disabilities. The researchers used an alternating treatment design with three participants, comparing two conditions: (1) SGD use with synthetic speech output and immediate reinforcement versus (2) SGD use without synthetic speech output and delayed reinforcement, with preferred items classified as easier or more difficult to name based on pre-assessment language abilities. Results demonstrated that verbal requests increased significantly in the no synthetic speech output with delayed reinforcement condition across both item difficulty sets, while SGD-based requests remained consistently high, and post-intervention language assessments showed significant improvements in expressive language abilities. For BCBA practitioners, these findings suggest that strategically removing auditory feedback from SGDs while implementing delayed reinforcement may promote vocalization in nonspeaking children with ASD and intellectual disabilities, offering a practical approach to balance AAC tool use with speech development goals.

Abstract

PURPOSE: This study aimed to investigate the effects of removing synthetic speech output from speech-generating devices (SGDs) and incorporating delayed reinforcement on verbal requests in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and intellectual disabilities. Additionally, we examined how participants' receptive and expressive language abilities influenced vocal requests by comparing items categorized as easier or more difficult to name based on pre-assessment results. METHODS: Three children with ASD and intellectual disabilities participated. An alternating treatment design was used to compare two conditions: (1) synthetic speech output with immediate reinforcement and (2) no synthetic speech output with delayed reinforcement. Preferred items were classified into two sets based on pre-assessed receptive and expressive language abilities. The dependent variable was the number of verbal and SGD-based requests. RESULTS: Results showed that verbal requests increased in the no synthetic speech output with delayed reinforcement condition across both item sets, while SGD-based requests remained high. Post-intervention language assessments indicated significant improvements in expressive language abilities. CONCLUSION: These findings suggest that removing synthetic speech output and incorporating delayed reinforcement may effectively promote vocal requests in children with ASD and intellectual disabilities. This intervention provides a novel approach to facilitate speech communication while continuing the use of AAC tools.

Introduction

It is estimated that approximately 30% of individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) never develop functional speech (Lorah et al.,2022). For children with ASD who exhibit these characteristics, Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) strategies have been recommended to facilitate functional communication (Mirenda,2003).

One type of AAC is the Speech-Generating Device (SGD), an electronic communication tool. SGDs produce speech output when users select icons, pictures, or words displayed on the screen (Lorah et al.,2015). As electronic tablets, such as the Apple iPad®, are increasingly utilized as SGDs (Lorah et al.,2022), their adoption as AAC tools has expanded. SGDs offer the advantage of generating consistent speech output and facilitating message interpretation for listeners (Alzrayer et al.,2020). Also, managing all communication symbols is straightforward because they are stored within the device. However, tapping an SGD does not inherently function as a linguistic behavior (Bondy,2012). If tapping the device occurs at a similar frequency regardless of whether a listener is present, the SGD may serve more as a toy than as a communication tool (Bondy,2019). Therefore, it is crucial to determine whether the synthetic speech generated by SGDs functions as communication rather than mere sound output (Bondy,2019).

Interventions involving SGDs have shown promise in promoting vocalizations and speech (Yuan et al.,2024). Yuan et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of studies utilizing SGDs to enhance vocalizations and speech in children with ASD and complex communication needs. The review included 14 single-case experimental design studies and 4 group design studies published between 1990 and 2023. Among 108 participants, 95 (87%) exhibited improvements in vocalizations and speech to varying degrees. Kasari, Kaiser, Goods, Nietfeld, Mathy, Landa, Murphy, and Almirall (2014) suggested that SGD use may facilitate speech acquisition by providing consistent vocal models, increasing their frequency, and presenting symbols alongside speech output on the device screen.

These findings suggest that the synthetic speech output of SGDs may contribute to facilitating vocal requests. Previous studies have investigated the impact of the presence or absence of synthetic speech output from SGDs on vocal requests (Schlosser, Sigafoos, Luiselli, Angermeier, Harasymowyz, Schooley, & Belfiore,2007; Sigafoos et al.,2003). However, none of these studies identified a significant effect of synthetic speech output. Notably, in conditions where synthetic speech was absent, SGD-based requests were immediately reinforced. This immediate reinforcement may have strengthened SGD-based requesting behaviors, thereby reducing the likelihood of vocal requests.

An additional intervention method involves delaying the presentation of reinforcers to promote vocal requests while utilizing SGDs. Studies have indicated that incorporating delayed reinforcement into interventions facilitates both SGD-based requesting and vocal requests (Alzrayer et al.,2020; Bishop, Moore, Dart, Radley, Brewer, Barker, Quintero, Litten, Glifeather, Newborne, & Toche,2020; Gevarter & Horan,2019; Gevarter, O’Reilly, Kuhn, Mills, Ferguson, Watkins, Sigafoos, Lang, Rojeski, & Lancioni,2016). This approach is often combined with other strategies, such as vocal prompting and differential reinforcement (Gevarter & Horan,2019). For instance, Gevarter et al. (2016) conducted an intervention targeting vocal requests in 4 children with ASD, integrating SGD-based requesting with delayed reinforcement and differential reinforcement of vocal requests. As a result, 2 participants demonstrated increased approximations of target words.

Although studies have examined the effects of synthetic speech output and additional interventions on vocal requests, few have evaluated participants’ ability to comprehend and express the names of items used in interventions. While many studies on SGD and vocal requests have assessed participants’ vocal imitation or expressive language skills (Yuan et al.,2024), few have pre-assessed the extent to which participants understand and articulate the names of items. Although some studies have conducted naming evaluations before and after interventions (Brady, Storkel, Bushnell, Barker, Saunders, Daniels, & Fleming,2015; Schlosser et al.,2007), the frequency of vocalizations in real-life requesting situations remains largely unexamined.

Thus, previous studies have not explored how receptive and expressive language variations influence the frequency of vocalizations in real-life requesting scenarios. Assessing participants’ receptive and expressive language abilities related to item names before an intervention could provide insight into whether existing stimulus relationships are reinforced during vocal requests or if new associations emerge through the use of SGD.

This study had two primary objectives. First, we aimed to investigate the effects of removing synthetic speech output from SGDs and incorporating delayed reinforcement on verbal requests in children with ASD and intellectual disabilities. Second, we compared and analyzed the results based on participants’ pre-assessed receptive and expressive language abilities related to item names, specifically distinguishing between easier or more challenging items to name.

Methods

Three children with ASD and intellectual disabilities participated. An alternating treatment design was used to compare two conditions: (1) synthetic speech output with immediate reinforcement and (2) no synthetic speech output with delayed reinforcement. Preferred items were classified into two sets based on pre-assessed receptive and expressive language abilities. The dependent variable was the number of verbal and SGD-based requests.

Results

Results showed that verbal requests increased in the no synthetic speech output with delayed reinforcement condition across both item sets, while SGD-based requests remained high. Post-intervention language assessments indicated significant improvements in expressive language abilities.

Discussion

These findings suggest that removing synthetic speech output and incorporating delayed reinforcement may effectively promote vocal requests in children with ASD and intellectual disabilities. This intervention provides a novel approach to facilitate speech communication while continuing the use of AAC tools.
