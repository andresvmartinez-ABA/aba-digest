Impact of augmented reality on narrative-related outcomes in children with Autism Spectrum Disorder: a within-subject design

Summary

# Summary: Impact of Augmented Reality on Narrative-Related Outcomes in Children with ASD This study investigated whether augmented reality (AR) technology could enhance narrative production, comprehension, mental state language use, and reading-related behaviors in children with autism spectrum disorder. Fifteen children with ASD without intellectual disability completed story retelling tasks in a within-subjects design using two counterbalanced conditions: traditional paper-based format and tablet-based AR format, with narrative performance assessed through microstructure (productivity, lexical diversity, syntax) and macrostructure (main ideas recall) measures, along with comprehension and reading-oriented behaviors. After applying Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, AR demonstrated statistically significant improvements with large effect sizes in mental state term production (d = 0.97, p = 0.002) and reading-orientation behaviors measured by the Children's Orientation to Book Reading scale (d = 0.86, p = 0.005), while uncorrected improvements in narrative microstructure (utterances, lexical diversity, type–token ratio) did not survive correction, and no significant differences emerged for mean length of utterance, subordination index, main ideas recall, or comprehension scores. For BCBA practitioners, these findings suggest that AR technology may serve as an effective tool for scaffolding socio-cognitive development and increasing engagement during language-based interventions for children with ASD, particularly for targeting Theory of Mind concepts and reading motivation, though effects on structural language complexity and narrative macrostructure remain limited based on current evidence.

Abstract

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is commonly associated with impairments in social communication, narrative abilities, and socio-cognitive functioning. This study explored whether augmented reality (AR) could enhance narrative production, comprehension, use of mental state language, and reading-related behaviors in children with ASD.

Fifteen children with ASD without intellectual disability participated in a within-subjects design in which they completed story retelling tasks under two counterbalanced conditions: a traditional paper-based format and a tablet-based format. Narrative performance was assessed through measures of microstructure, including productivity, lexical diversity, and syntax, as well as macrostructure indicators such as recall of main ideas. Oral comprehension and reading-oriented behaviors were also examined.

Results showed that the AR condition significantly improved socio-cognitive and behavioral outcomes. Children produced a higher number of mental state terms and demonstrated greater attention, persistence, and engagement during the tasks. These findings suggest that AR can function as an effective scaffold that facilitates the explicit representation of internal states while increasing attentional involvement.

Consequently, AR technology may represent a promising tool for improving language assessment and fostering social-cognitive development in children with ASD.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent difficulties in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (American Psychological Association, 2020). Narrative discourse is a fundamental pragmatic competence, as it integrates linguistic, cognitive, and social abilities, allowing individuals to organize events, consider the listener’s perspective, and communicate experiences coherently (Norbury et al., 2014). Given its inherently social and cognitively demanding nature, this type of discourse provides a particularly valuable context for both assessment and intervention in ASD (Hewitt, 2019).

Narrative abilities in neurodevelopmental conditions are commonly examined across two complementary levels: microstructure and macrostructure. Microstructure refers to local linguistic features such as productivity, lexical diversity, and syntactic complexity, whereas macrostructure concerns global aspects of organization, coherence, and thematic development (Hughes et al., 1997). In ASD, findings regarding microstructural performance are mixed. A meta-analysis byBaixauli et al. (2016)reported that individuals with ASD without intellectual disability tend to perform poorer than typically developing peers in measures such as total word production, utterance length, lexical diversity, and grammatical complexity, with moderate effect sizes. However, results across studies remain heterogeneous: some report comparable performance in narrative length or syntactic complexity (Diehl et al., 2006;Norbury and Bishop, 2003), whereas others describe reduced lexical variety, shorter narratives, and increased linguistic errors (Adornetti et al., 2024;Peristeri et al., 2017). In contrast, macrostructural impairments appear more robust and consistent across studies and languages, with large effect sizes (Baixauli et al., 2016). Individuals with ASD frequently show difficulties in maintaining global coherence, organizing events causally, and constructing well-structured narrative episodes (Adornetti et al., 2024;Ferretti et al., 2018). They also tend to produce fewer references to internal states (e.g., thoughts, emotions, intentions) (Losh and Capps, 2003;Peristeri et al., 2017), which are closely linked to Theory of Mind, that is, the ability to attribute mental states to oneself and to others, making it possible to explain and predict behavior (Premack and Woodruff, 1978). Importantly, cross-linguistic evidence suggests that macrostructural difficulties persist even when microstructural performance is relatively preserved, particularly in coherence, relevance selection, and episodic organization (Adornetti et al., 2024;Domingo et al., 2026;He et al., 2025;Peristeri et al., 2025).

Results

The primary objective of the present analysis was to compare participants’ performance under the AR condition versus the only printed-book condition across multiple narrative, socio-cognitive, and reading-oriented outcomes, using a within-subject design. Descriptive statistics indicated consistently higher mean scores in the AR condition across most variables, suggesting a general trend toward improved performance. Inferential analyses were conducted using paired-samplest-tests, with the Holm–Bonferroni correction applied to control for multiple comparisons (Table 2).

At the uncorrected level, statistically significant improvements were observed in several indices of narrative microstructural production. Specifically, participants produced a greater number of utterances in the AR condition compared to the printed-book condition,t(14) = 2.32,p= 0.036, with a medium effect size (d= 0.60). Similarly, lexical diversity, as measured by the number of different words, was significantly higher in the AR condition,t(14) = 2.24,p= 0.042,d= 0.58. The type–token ratio also showed a significant increase,t(14) = 2.29,p= 0.038,d= 0.59, indicating enhanced lexical variability. However, after applying the Holm–Bonferroni correction, these effects did not remain statistically significant (Bonferroni-adjustedp< 0.0056), suggesting that although the AR condition may positively influence language productivity and lexical diversity, these effects should be interpreted with caution.

No significant differences were observed for mean length of utterance (MLU),t(14) = 1.42,p= 0.177, or the subordination index,t(14) = 1.61,p= 0.131, indicating that structural aspects of language complexity were not significantly impacted by the AR condition. Regarding narrative macrostructure measures, the main ideas score did not differ significantly between conditions,t(14) = 1.45,p= 0.169. The total comprehension score also did not reveal significant differences between conditions,t(14) = 1.83,p= 0.089, although the latter approached significance and showed a moderate effect size (d= 0.47), suggesting a potential trend toward improvement.

In contrast, robust and statistically significant differences were observed in the use of mental state terms and global reading-orientation behaviors. Participants produced significantly more mental state terms in the AR condition,t(14) = 3.76,p= 0.002, which remained significant after Holm–Bonferroni correction (Bonferroni-adjustedp< 0.0056;Figure 3). The associated effect size was large (d= 0.97), indicating a substantial improvement in this variable. Similarly, the COB revealed significantly higher scores in the AR condition,t(14) = 3.35,p= 0.005, which also remained significant after correction (Bonferroni-adjustedp< 0.0056), with a large effect size (d= 0.86;Figure 4). This finding suggests that the AR condition produced meaningful improvements in overall reading-orientation behaviors.

Taken together, the results indicate a differential pattern of effects across domains. After controlling for multiple comparisons, the AR condition demonstrated robust and reliable effects primarily in socio-cognitive indicators, such as mental state terms, and reading-orientation outcomes, both showing large effect sizes. In contrast, improvements in narrative microstructure measures, although initially significant and of moderate magnitude, did not withstand correction.

Discussion

The primary objective of the present study was to examine the impact of augmented reality on narrative production, comprehension, sociocognitive indicators and book-reading-oriented behaviors in children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). Through a within-subject design, an AR-enriched digital modality was compared to the traditional paper-based condition. The findings reveal a differential pattern of benefits, with significant impacts on socio-cognitive indicators and behavioral engagement. The first specific objective of this study was to determine whether the AR condition would lead to higher levels of reading-oriented behavior compared to the traditional paper mode. As hypothesized, the AR condition resulted in significantly higher scores on the Children’s Orientation to Book Reading (COB) scale. The statistically significant difference and the large effect size indicate that the AR modality substantially improved overall engagement, persistence, and attentional focus. These results align with previous research suggesting that digital and multimodal formats can enhance the motivation and involvement of children with ASD by providing a more stimulating and interactive literacy experience (Richter and Courage, 2017;Chen et al., 2015;Diehm et al., 2020). In fact, digital tools have been shown to act as intrinsic reinforcers that increase enthusiasm in cognitively demanding tasks, such as shared reading. In this regard,Richter and Courage (2017)had already observed that children show significantly greater engagement and higher levels of persistence when interacting with electronic formats compared to printed ones.

The second objective focused on examining the differential effects of AR on narrative production, both at the microstructural and macrostructural levels. It was expected that the enhanced visual support of AR would facilitate richer linguistic production. However, although the initial uncorrected analyses indicated significant improvements in microstructural language parameters—such as the number of utterances, number of different words, and type-token ratio—these effects were no longer significant after applying the Holm–Bonferroni correction. Furthermore, structural measures such as Mean Length of Utterance (MLU) and the subordination index showed no significant differences. This suggests that while AR may serve as a prompt for increased verbal output, it does not fundamentally alter the underlying syntactic complexity of the children’s language. Regarding narrative macrostructure, the results indicated that the ability to convey the main ideas of the story did not differ significantly between the AR condition and the traditional book. This finding suggests that global discourse structure and the prioritization of key information are high-level cognitive processes that exhibit greater stability and resistance to immediate changes derived solely from the presentation medium. Similarly, the total comprehension score did not reveal statistically significant differences between the two conditions. Nevertheless, it is essential to highlight that this result showed a trend toward significance with a moderate effect size in favor of the AR condition. This “trend effect” suggests that while a single exposure session may not be sufficient to drastically alter comprehension levels, the dynamic visual stimulus provided by AR might be facilitating more fluid access to the plot. This positive tendency partially aligns with the findings ofWainwright et al. (2020), who indicate that the value of technology in ASD lies not only in its visual appeal but in its capacity to direct attention toward the critical elements of the story. The fact that comprehension approached significance could indicate that AR might help children connect sequential events that, in a paper format, require greater inferential effort. Moreover, the absence of full significance in comprehension coincides with previous findings suggesting a gap between visual attention and deep information processing (Wainwright et al., 2020). In other words, the absence of statistically robust effects might be due to the “visual attention-to-processing gap”: children may be highly engaged with the 3D images without necessarily translating that visual interest into deep semantic recall. Nonetheless, the observed effect size allows for the hypothesis that, with a larger sample size, the visual scaffolding of AR could become an effective tool for improving oral comprehension. This would be achieved by reducing the ambiguity of static illustrations and providing a more explicit representation of the narrative’s causal relationships. The observed trend suggests that with repeated exposure, the visual scaffolding of AR could lead to better story recall by reducing the inferential load required to connect sequential events. Future studies with larger samples and longitudinal designs are needed to confirm whether these effects strengthen over time and reach statistical significance.

The final objective of the current study was to analyze whether the AR condition promoted a greater use of socio-cognitive references, such as terms related to mental states. As predicted, participants produced significantly more mental state terms in the AR condition. This finding remained significant even after controlling for multiple comparisons and yielded a large effect size. Therefore, these results indicate that the three-dimensional and dynamic nature of AR images may provide essential scaffolding that helps children with ASD interpret and verbalize characters’ intentions, emotions, and beliefs, which are often difficult to grasp from static two-dimensional illustrations. Given that children with ASD process information more efficiently when it is presented in a structured manner, it is possible that the ability of AR to transform static images into predictable dynamic sequences may have reduced the cognitive load. By removing the mental effort required to imagine movement or transitions between scenes, the child can focus their cognitive resources on the narrative content. This observation aligns withDiehm et al. (2020), who argue that dynamic visual supports, such as animation, facilitate access to crucial information regarding actions and event sequencing. Furthermore, this dynamic visual support allows for the explication of socio-cognitive elements that are typically implicit and difficult for this population to decode. AR animation can more clearly represent characters’ facial expressions and intentions—core elements of Theory of Mind (ToM). AsWainwright et al. (2020)point out, the clinical value of this technology lies in its capacity to direct attention toward key plot elements, enabling a deeper interpretation of the story. The robustness of the effect on socio-cognitive indicators (Theory of Mind) is perhaps the most relevant finding of the present study, as these abilities are typically impaired in children with ASD and are closely linked to their narrative production (Losh and Capps, 2003;Peristeri et al., 2017).

From a neurocognitive perspective, the benefits observed in the AR condition may be closely linked to the strong visual processing profiles often characteristic of individuals with ASD. According to the Weak Central Coherence theory, children with autism frequently demonstrate a bias toward local, detail-oriented information at the expense of global integration. In traditional paper-based reading, the demand to integrate static, fragmented illustrations into a coherent narrative whole may overwhelm their integrative processing capacity. However, AR’s ability to present information through simultaneous and explicit multimodal cues—where movement, 3D depth, and spatial consistency are provided in real-time—may mitigate these integration difficulties. By offering a more visually cohesive stimulus, AR likely facilitates the transition from “detail-focused” perception to a more “global” understanding of the narrative context, thereby allowing the child to allocate more cognitive resources to the social and causal dimensions of the story.

In summary, the results of the current study suggest a differential impact of AR depending on the narrative domain evaluated. While basic linguistic structures remained stable, AR significantly boosted the socio-cognitive richness of the narratives and the children’s behavioral engagement. The preference for visual and structured input in children with ASD indicates that AR may act as a “cognitive filter.” By highlighting salient cues and guiding attention toward relevant events, AR may reduce the “noise” often found in complex social scenes. This supports the idea that digital formats are not just more “fun,” but more “legible,” allowing for sustained focus that traditional paper sometimes fails to provide. In this way, AR could serve as a bridge to overcome the gap in Theory of Mind (ToM) by providing a more transparent representation of social causality.
