Impact of the application of the Bee-Bot robot on the non-verbal communication skills of students with autism spectrum disorder

E. Pérez-Vázquez, Alba Gilabert-Cerdá  ·  Education and Information Technologies : Official Journal of the IFIP technical committee on Education  ·  2025
Tech + PECS/SGD
72/100Directly relevant as it addresses robotics/socially assistive robots for autism intervention with school-age populations and communication outcomes, though limited by small sample size and short duration which aligns with profile cautions about methodological rigor.
Summary

# Summary The study aimed to determine whether the Bee-Bot robot could improve non-verbal communication skills in students with autism spectrum disorder. A quasi-experimental pre-test-post-test design was employed with 22 children with ASD (11 experimental, 11 control); the experimental group engaged in Bee-Bot-facilitated activities over two weeks, while the control group participated in non-robotic activities, with assessment across three dimensions: verbal-nonverbal integration, eye contact, and emotional expression through body language. The experimental group demonstrated significant improvements (p < 0.05) in eye contact (r = 0.5584 and 0.4541) and bodily emotional expression (r = 0.4350–0.5691), with 91.67% of evaluated areas showing significant enhancements, whereas the control group showed improvement in only one item related to verbal-nonverbal integration. The findings suggest that the Bee-Bot robot—particularly its mechanical features (lights, sounds) and non-anthropomorphic design—may effectively facilitate non-verbal communication skill development in students with ASD within classroom settings, offering practical value for technology-mediated inclusive educational interventions. However, the authors caution that results should be interpreted carefully due to small sample size and brief intervention duration (two weeks).

Abstract

The integration of Robotic Pedagogy into the education of students diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) has experienced a notable rise over the past decade to develop non-verbal communication skills. Consequently, the primary aim of this research has been to apply the Bee-Bot robot to develop non-verbal communication skills of students with ASD, framed within a social-constructivist and inclusive education approach. A quantitative research study employing a quasi-experimental methodology with a pre-test-post-test design was conducted. The participant sample consisted of 22 children with ASD. Half of these constituted the experimental group, engaging in activities facilitated by the Bee-Bot robot, while the remaining half formed the control group, participating in activities not involving robotics. For data collection, an ad hoc assessment instrument was employed, comprising 44 items distributed across three dimensions: (1) the association between verbal and non-verbal communicative acts, (2) eye contact, and (3) the bodily expression of emotions. Each participant completed ten individual activities. The implementation of the activities, each lasting 40 min, were conducted over a two-week period. The activities were manipulative in nature, highly structured, and supported by visual aids (pictograms), with the aim of facilitating instruction based on the principle of errorless learning. The results, particularly concerning the dimension of non-verbal communication skills indicated significant differences (p< 0.05) in the post-tests regarding eye contact skills and expression of emotions through body language. Conversely, regarding the intra-group results of the control group, significant differences were observed in only one item related to the integration of verbal and non-verbal communicative behaviours. In contrast, the intervention group, involving the use of robotics, exhibited significant enhancements in 91.67% of the evaluated areas. In conclusion, the Bee-Bot robot exhibits promising potential in augmenting non-verbal communication skills among students diagnosed with ASD, offering valuable implications for technology-mediated inclusive educational practices. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to limitations such as the small sample size and the short intervention duration.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), as defined in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) by the American Psychiatric Association (APA,2013), is currently regarded as a neurodevelopmental disorder that manifests in early childhood, leading to difficulties in various areas, including pragmatics, prosody, and non-verbal communication (Wiklund,2016). Drawing on the work of authors such as Mourière and Hewett (2021), the use of non-verbal behaviours by individuals with ASD is considered “unique” and is linked to challenges in encoding and decoding information in interpersonal communication. In all interactions, individuals must read their social environment, a process known as decoding, while simultaneously conveying non-verbal messages to others, referred to as encoding (Mourière & Hewett,2021). Nevertheless, this process is highly complex and demands a strong capacity for encoding and decoding, making it an unconscious, automatic process for neurotypical individuals (Owens,2008). Specifically, non-verbal communication refers to behaviours that convey information without words (Grillo & Enesi,2022). According to Mast and Cousin (2013), this form of communication can encompass facial expressions, posture, and gestures, serving various purposes such as facilitating interpersonal interaction, expressing emotions, and conveying attitudes. In line with these conceptualisations, the DSM-5 (APA,2013) identifies the following non-verbal communicationneedsfor students with ASD: (1) integration of verbal and non-verbal communicative behaviours, (2) maintaining eye contact, and (3) effectively expressing emotions through bodily cues. Given the crucial role of understanding and utilizing non-verbal messages in comprehending social interactions (Mourière & Hewett,2021), it is imperative to employ teaching methods that engage the attention of learners with ASD and incorporate fundamental principles such as positive reinforcement, errorless learning, and structured contexts (García-Guzmán,2021).

This study makes a novel contribution to the existing literature in several ways. Firstly, it applies a ground-based robot,Bee-Bot, to work on aspects of non-verbal communication with students with ASD, which has not previously been used in research. Secondly, this research introduces a novel pedagogical curriculum model, allowing for the implementation of this practice in school classrooms, unlike in previous research (Kumazaki et al.,2019; Syrdal et al.,2020). Thirdly, in line with this innovation, the intervention was conducted in the natural environment of the students, that is, in their classrooms at school, rather than in an artificial setting as is the case with most research studies (Conti et al.,2020; Farhan et al.,2021). Fourthly, another novelty is that this study includes a control group consisting of students with ASD, unlike other studies (Barnes et al.,2021; Cao et al.,2020; Kumazaki et al.,2017; So et al.,2020) which have included control groups of neurotypical students. Fifthly, considering that more than half of the research on this topic involved fewer than ten participants (Pérez-Vázquez et al.,2020), this study includes a larger number of participants. Sixthly, as a novelty, this research includes children with ASD from different levels of the spectrum, which represents a significant development in the field.

Therefore, this study aims to apply the Bee-Bot robot to develop the non-verbal communication skills of students with ASD. In light of these considerations, answers to the following questions were sought.

Are there significant differences in non-verbal communication skills between the control group and the experimental group at pretest?

Are there significant differences in non-verbal communication skills between the control group and the experimental group at post-test?

Are there significant differences in non-verbal communication skills within the control group between pretest and post-test?

Are there significant differences in non-verbal communication skills within the experimental group between pretest and post-test?

Results

Based on the analysis of the collected data concerning non-verbal communication skills and in alignment with the stated research objectives, the following section presents a comparative analysis of pre-test and post-test results, supplemented by a descriptive overview of the data.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to utilise the Bee-Bot robot to enhance the non-verbal communication abilities of students diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). In pursuit of this overarching objective, the research addressed various research questions. Specifically, the findings from Question 1 (which focused on differences in non-verbal communication skills between the control group and the experimental group at pre-test) revealed estimates associated with p-values greater than 0.05. These outcomes align with the conclusions drawn by Lorenzo et al. (2019), who investigated eleven participants divided into two groups (control and experimental). As highlighted by Hirschauer et al. (2022), a p-value exceeding 0.05 is typically considered “statistically non-significant.” This suggests that no significant differences were present between the robot-assisted intervention group and the control group prior to the intervention. Consequently, despite potential differences in sample characteristics between the two groups, this result indicates a balanced distribution of participants, thus ensuring group homogeneity (Sidiq et al.,2021). The rationale underlying these findings, as articulated by Lorenzo et al. (2022), is that each student exhibits behavioural characteristics typical of ASD, regardless of severity level. This perspective is consistent with the broader framework of neurodiversity, which recognises neurological differences such as autism as natural variations within the human genome rather than deficits (Pellicano & Houting,2021). In this sense, acknowledging neurodiversity is essential for advancing educational equity, which requires adapting educational tools and methodologies to meet the diverse learning needs of all students. Therefore, no discernible disparities were observed in the pre-test phase between the control and experimental groups (Hirschauer et al.,2022).

Regarding the second research question, the results varied depending on the specific subcategory of non-verbal communication skills. In terms of the association between verbal and non-verbal communicative acts, the findingsrevealedno significant differences (p> 0.05) between the control group and the robot-assisted intervention group. This outcome may be attributed to the students’ lack of prior knowledge in linking verbal and non-verbal communication, as noted by Akalin et al. (2014), which could have limited their ability to make meaningful progress when interacting with the robot during the intervention. In contrast, when examining eye contact, the results indicate significant post-intervention differences between the control and experimental groups, suggesting improvements in visual engagement. Specifically, item 23 (r= 0.5584) and item 24 (r= 0.4541)showedlarge and moderate effect sizes, respectively (Nakagawa,2004). This suggests that the robot-assisted intervention had a robust impact on participants’ abilities in those specific non-verbal communication domains. This improvement may be due to two key factors: the robot’s mechanical features, such as lights and sounds (Kozima et al.,2009), and its non-anthropomorphic design, which may reduce social pressure and increase accessibility for students with ASD (Cao et al.,2020). These findings are consistent with those of Chung et al. (2024), who demonstrated that robotic tools can effectively foster social skills in students with ASD. Furthermore, in relation to the bodily expression of emotions, significant differences were observed between the control and the robot-assisted intervention group, indicating greater ability among the latter to convey emotions physically. Specifically, item 27 reported an effect size ofr= 0.4350, and item 28 yieldedr= 0.4767. Both values fall within the range of a moderate effect, indicating that the intervention brought about meaningful improvements in these specific behavioural indicators. In contrast, item 25 demonstrated a large effect size (r= 0.5691), suggesting a particularly strong impact of the intervention in that area. Taken together, these results indicate that the robot-assisted intervention was effective in enhancing non-verbal emotional expression, with moderate effects observed for items 27 and 28, and a large effect for item 25 (Nakagawa,2004). The consistency and magnitude of these outcomes underscore the practical significance of the intervention’s influence on participants’ non-verbal communication skills. This improvement may be linked to the feedback mechanisms integrated into the Bee-Bot robot, which emitted lights and sounds when students successfully expressed emotions through body language (Saleh et al.,2021; Seckel et al.,2023). These outcomes echo the results of Tuyen et al. (2020), who reported marked progress in students’ expression of emotions such as sadness and joy.

Turning to the third research question, the results reveal significant differences in one item within the first subcategory for the control group, specifically related to the association between verbal and non-verbal communicative acts. Regarding effect size, item 18 exhibited a Z value of −2.236 with a sample size of 11 participants, resulting in an r value of − 0.741. This value represents a large effect (Nakagawa,2004), indicating a substantial improvement in the ability measured by this item following the intervention. This finding, focused on the identification of emotions through their manifestations (item 18), may be attributed to the use of visual didactic materials designed to support comprehension of instructions and activity content (Mesibov & Shea,2010). As noted by Rutherford et al. (2020), such strategies are well-aligned with the visual thinking style commonly observed in individuals with ASD and contribute to promoting more inclusive educational practices by accommodating diverse cognitive profiles. However, in relation to eye contact, the control group did not exhibit significant differences between pre- and post-intervention performance. This lack of improvement may stem from the absence of novel visual stimuli, as the materials used closely resembled those encountered in the students’ routine learning environments, potentially leading them to be perceived as familiar and thus less engaging (Rutherford et al.,2020). Similarly, regarding the bodily expression of emotions, no significant post-intervention differences were observed in the control group. This may be due to the lack of feedback during the activities, which could have hindered students’ ability to refine and accurately develop the targeted emotional expressions.

Addressing the fourth research question, the findings reveal significant improvements within the experimental group following the intervention with the Bee-Bot robot in non-verbal communication skills, with marked differences observed between the pre-test and post-test. As shown in the table, all items except one exhibited large effect sizes, indicating substantial intra-group improvements in non-verbal communication skills following the robot-assisted intervention (Nakagawa,2004). These gains, especially in the ability to associate verbal acts with non-verbal ones, may stem from the structured, predictable nature of the robot-based activities (Duquette et al.,2008). Consistent with this, Lorenzo et al. (2022) also identified notable enhancements in communication abilities, including non-verbal elements, in students with ASD after employing augmented reality, highlighting the role of inclusive technologies in promoting equitable learning outcomes (Abimbola et al.,2024). Moreover, significant progress was evident in all items related to eye contact within the experimental group, likely influenced by the Bee-Bot’s pet-like design, which fosters a sense of connection akin to human-animal relationships (Haak,2020), again mirroring Lorenzo et al.,’s (2021) results. Importantly, these findings underscore the potential of educational robotics as a catalyst for fostering inclusivity in classrooms by providing adaptable, engaging tools that meet diverse learning needs (Díaz-Boladeras et al.,2023). Lastly, in terms of bodily expression of emotions, the intervention led to significant improvements across all related items, echoing findings by Tuyen et al. (2020), and suggesting that the combination of simple robotics and ASD-specific didacticstrategiescan effectively support the development of emotional competencies (Arocena Pérez et al.,2022).
