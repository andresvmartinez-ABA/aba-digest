Virtual Reality Applications for Children and Adolescents in Mental Health: A Scoping Review of Assessment and Intervention in Clinical Practice

Nina Krupljanin, Lenneke R. A. Alink, Anja van der Voort, Catharina E. Bergwerff  ·  Clinical Psychology and Psychotherapy  ·  2026

76/100Directly relevant as a comprehensive review of VR interventions for youth mental health including autism spectrum disorder, ADHD assessment, and social skills training—core technology and autism interests—though limited to assessment/intervention mechanisms without deeper behavioral analysis of learning processes.
Summary

# Summary for BCBA Practitioners This scoping review examined the validity of virtual reality (VR) assessment tools and the effectiveness of VR interventions for children and adolescents with mental health–related concerns across 43 studies. The methodology involved a systematic search of four databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and PsycInfo) to identify studies using immersive VR for assessment, prevention, or treatment of mental health symptoms in youth clinical populations or at-risk groups. The review found that 13 studies on VR assessment tools (primarily for ADHD symptoms) demonstrated performance advantages over traditional assessments through increased sensitivity and specificity, while 30 intervention studies spanning autism spectrum disorder, anxiety, attention difficulty, and behavior problems reported promising results across three working mechanisms: skills practice, exposure, and relaxation—with the strongest evidence for social skills and communication training in children with autism spectrum disorder. A critical limitation was that no VR application had been tested more than once, and nearly half of studies lacked feasibility measures, with inconsistencies among those that did report feasibility data. For BCBA practitioners, while VR shows clinical promise for assessment and intervention delivery (particularly for social communication skills training in autism), the lack of replication studies and standardized feasibility assessments limits current recommendations for routine clinical implementation.

Abstract

ABSTRACTBackgroundThe purpose of this scoping review was to synthesize literature on virtual reality (VR) applications used in assessment and treatment in youth mental health care. We studied the validity of assessment tools, the effectiveness of (preventive) interventions and their feasibility.MethodThe search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and PsycInfo. Introduction

Mental health services for children and adolescents keep evolving to improve their adequacy and effectiveness. Adaptations involving immersive Virtual Reality (VR) and VR headsets have recently become more popular as the costs for hardware and development have decreased. In the past decades, numerous new diagnostic VR tools and VR interventions have been developed for child and adolescent mental health (Blanco et al. 2024). The question arises whether and to what extent these applications improve mental health services. Therefore, this review aims to synthesize literature on immersive VR applications for youth, including assessment tools, preventive and goal‐directed interventions and their validity, effectiveness and feasibility.

When youth face severe challenges associated with mental health, the first step is often to undergo clinical assessment for a better understanding of the symptomatology. It is important that clinical assessment is reliable, valid and standardized (Kline 2015), as its outcomes are directional for deciding on treatment strategies. Assessments traditionally consist of pen‐and‐paper tasks, (multi)informant and self‐report and (cognitive) tasks administered in laboratory settings. These types of assessments can lead to inaccurate conclusions due to a variety of reasons. For example, some traditional diagnostic tools have low ecological validity (Schmuckler 2001) or rely on subjective (self‐)reports (De Los Reyes et al. 2011). Undiagnosed, misdiagnosed and overdiagnosed youth do not receive appropriate treatment (Aggarwal and Angus 2015; Merten et al. 2017; Sheldrick et al. 2011), which can have negative consequences, such as deterioration of symptoms and unnecessarily long treatments (Webster et al. 2016). To quantify behaviour and symptoms in a way that is ecologically valid, individuals should be assessed in specific and realistic situations. Technological innovations may offer a solution to this need.

In VR, it is possible to create environments that resemble real‐life situations and to measure behaviour without biased (self‐)reports. VR allows for complete control over visual and acoustic stimuli presented to a user, which makes environments highly adaptable to different age groups and varying needs, such as for example different levels of complexity. Additionally, it can combine recordings of behaviour such as movement, reaction time and eye‐tracking, making for a more complete picture of behaviour. Such multimodal assessments are promising for capturing heterogeneous symptom presentations (Wiebe et al. 2023). VR assessment does not only effectively measure executive functioning (EF) but additionally reflects EF in everyday life more realistically than traditional tests and is therefore more ecologically valid (Lalonde et al. 2013). In the context of psychology, the term ‘ecological validity’ describes the degree to which observations in controlled situations can be generalized to real‐life contexts (Kihlstrom 2021). VR technology has the potential to increase ecological validity by incorporating relevant real‐life stimuli. For example, a specific VR tool, the virtual classroom, has been designed to assess cognitive functions associated with attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in a realistic and relatable environment. It has been found to discriminate reliably between a clinical and nonclinical population, and to additionally counter the underestimation of distractibility of traditional cognitive performance tests by using authentic distraction stimuli such as a classroom noise or activity occurring outside of the window (Parsons et al. 2019; Rizzo et al. 2006). VR assessment may thereby have a higher specificity and sensitivity than traditional assessments of ADHD‐related symptoms. It is crucial to evaluate how VR assessments compare to validated traditional tests to conclude on their potential to improve clinical assessment (Bauer et al. 2012).

VR is not only a promising technology for clinical assessment but also poses a potential solution to overcome challenges for psychological interventions for youth. The effectiveness of psychotherapies for anxiety, depression, ADHD and conduct problems in youth has generally stagnated or even decreased over the course of the past 50 years. Possible explanations refer to rigid treatment structure, changing means of communication and the rate at which the nature of youth dysfunction is shifting, which might be faster than treatments are evolving (Weisz et al. 2019). For example, interventions often involve in vivo exposure and skills practice, which can be difficult to implement due to reasons such as time constraints and practical limitations in real‐life settings.

VR technology is highly adaptable in relatively little time, which makes it possible to flexibly tailor treatments to specific needs. VR interventions include both preventive applications (aimed to reduce the risk of developing severe mental health issues) and curative interventions for clinical populations. Besides the potential for increasing ecological validity, VR technology can increase accessibility of intervention situations. For example, social communication skills can be practiced in different social situations with different conversation partners without having to leave the therapist's office. Another advantage is the highly immersive nature of VR: It can be challenging to stay engaged with relaxation exercises or psychoeducation due to external stimuli, which can be minimized by practicing relaxation or giving psychoeducation in immersive VR environments. Immersion can also lead to more authentic emotional engagement than roleplay when practicing skills. For example, not all children with high reactive aggression will be triggered sufficiently in traditional forms of psychotherapy. Evoking moderate levels of aggression in treatment is useful as children can then practice newly learned emotion regulation skills in a safe environment. With VR, the therapist can evoke these emotions by tailoring the behaviour of virtual characters in ways that are triggering reactive aggression in their clients (Alsem et al. 2021). Similarly, social skills for adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) can be trained in safe and realistic environments, meaning that those skills can be practiced in an ecologically valid way without having to be exposed to stress‐inducing real‐life social environments (Chen et al. 2024). However, even though VR applications such as these show promising results, it is still unclear how they compare to traditional interventions.

To evaluate the utility of a VR tool in clinical practice, the VR‐specific feasibility of the application should be assessed. Feasibility is a broad concept that, in the context of VR mental health applications, generally refers to whether the applications can be used as intended in clinical practice. Although general feasibility and VR‐specific feasibility share common aspects, they are conceptually distinct because immersive VR introduces characteristics that are absent from conventional technologies. Beyond being usable and acceptable, VR applications should elicit the psychological experience necessary for the application to be delivered as designed. Accordingly, it has been argued that VR mental health applications should be tolerable, usable, create a high sense of presence and perceived realism and be satisfactory for the user—in order to live up to their promise and be feasible for use in clinical practice (Krupljanin et al. 2025; Mummah et al. 2016; Vasser and Aru 2020). For example, clients will not engage with applications that are intolerable thus prompting negative physical symptoms. This will lead to dropouts and refusal to use the application again. Applications with a high sense of presence, describing feeling present in the virtual environment, are associated with higher engagement and the intensity of induced emotions, which is desirable in exposure therapy for instance (Brade et al. 2017; Jicol et al. 2021). VR exposure applications with a low sense of presence can evoke less intense emotions and lower engagement than expected. As for perceived realism, it is important that the users perceive the virtual environment is coherent and believable rather than as realistic as possible. Humanoid avatars for example can even decrease the sense of presence if they are too realistic (Vinayagamoorthy et al. 2004). Lastly, given that VR systems were initially not built for children, VR headset suppliers issued safety warnings about improper fitting of the headset potentially leading to adverse effects, strongly advising adult supervision and avoiding prolonged exposure. A recent review investigated risks of VR use among children and adolescents and found that health concerns mainly emerge in relation to unsupervised and extensive use of VR, such as VR games, but not in relation to therapeutic applications (Kaimara et al. 2022). VR‐specific feasibility assessments are therefore necessary to monitor potential adverse effects and, given that practical constraints can strongly affect clinical utility, to inform implementation in clinical practice.

This review provides an overview of youth VR mental health applications across prevention, assessment and intervention. It examines the target groups these applications were developed for, their intended goals, and their reported effectiveness or, in the case of assessment applications, their validity and reliability. In addition, it explores how VR feasibility was assessed across these applications and what these evaluations revealed. The goal is to draw a conclusion on the state of the art and contribute to a better understanding of the applicability and utility of VR technology in mental health for different child and adolescent target groups.

Results

A total of 43 studies were included. Thirteen studies investigated the validity of VR assessment tools whereby the majority focused on the assessment of attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder symptoms. These studies found that VR shows a performance advantage over traditional assessment tools by increasing sensitivity and specificity. Effectiveness was studied in 30 studies including youth with autism spectrum disorder, anxiety, attention difficulty and behaviour problems. The working mechanisms of these VR interventions encompassed skills practice, exposure and relaxation. VR interventions show promising results for all working mechanisms, with most evidence for social skills and communication training for children with autism spectrum disorder. Importantly, none of these applications have been tested more than once. Nearly half of all included studies lacked feasibility measures, while the rest showed inconsistencies in their feasibility assessment.

Discussion

VR applications for youth in mental health show promise. More standardized testing and replication studies with transparent VR feasibility assessments are needed to determine their effectiveness and utility in clinical practice.
