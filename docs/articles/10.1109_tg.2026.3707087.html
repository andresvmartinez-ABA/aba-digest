Summary

# Summary: Interface Design in Serious Games for Autism Spectrum Disorder This systematic review aimed to evaluate how different interface architectures and interaction modalities in serious games impact their effectiveness for meeting the needs of children with autism spectrum disorder, shifting focus from general therapeutic efficacy to user interface engineering. The methodology consisted of a ten-year systematic review (2015–2025) analyzing 15 empirical studies across multiple interface types, including graphical user interfaces, tangible user interfaces, mixed reality, and motion-based or virtual reality systems. Results indicated a trend toward embodied and multimodal interaction models, with tangible and hybrid systems demonstrating superior engagement and social initiation outcomes, while mixed reality platforms showed promise for emotion recognition; however, technical limitations in scalability, real-time responsiveness, and technology maintenance were identified across studies. The practical implications for BCBAs include consideration of tangible and hybrid interface designs when selecting or recommending game-based interventions, awareness of the emerging potential of mixed reality for emotion-related targets, and recognition that technical feasibility and system reliability remain important implementation factors. The authors propose a taxonomy of interface architectures and advocate for low-cost, inclusive game engineering frameworks to support broader accessibility in autism intervention contexts.

Abstract

This article presents a ten-year systematic review (2015–2025) of the interface architectures and interaction modalities of serious games designed for children with autism spectrum disorder. The analysis shifts the focus from general therapeutic efficacy toward the engineering of user interfaces, evaluating how different paradigms—graphical, tangible, motion-based, and artificial intelligence-supported—impact the system's ability to meet neurodiverse requirements. A total of 15 empirical studies were analyzed across various interface modalities, including graphical user interfaces, tangible user interfaces, mixed reality (MR), and motion-based or virtual reality systems. Results show a shift toward embodied and multimodal interaction models, highlighting advances in sensor integration, feedback design, and adaptive control mechanisms. Tangible and hybrid systems suggested superior engagement and social initiation outcomes within the analyzed cohorts, while MR-based platforms showed potential for emotion recognition. However, technical challenges persist in scalability, real-time responsiveness, and maintenance of assistive technologies. This article concludes with a proposed taxonomy of interface architectures and recommendations for low-cost, inclusive game engineering frameworks.
