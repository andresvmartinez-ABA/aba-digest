WE PLAY | ON JOUE - Family-Centered Virtual Reality Games Room for Children with Developmental Disabilities and Their Families: A Feasibility Case Study.

TECHNOLOGY IN ABA

Summary

# Summary: WE PLAY | ON JOUE Family-Centered Virtual Reality Games Room This feasibility case study explored whether joint virtual reality (VR) sessions could improve physical engagement in youth with developmental disabilities while simultaneously promoting caregiver relaxation. Four youth–caregiver dyads (youth aged 10–20 years with cerebral palsy, autism with motor dyspraxia, or CHARGE syndrome) completed weekly 30-minute VR sessions over four weeks, with youth using the AstroSurfer app for motor function practice and caregivers simultaneously using the TRIPP® app for relaxation, with outcomes measured using standardized motor assessments (Trunk Control Measurement Scale, Quality of Upper Extremity Skills Test), caregiver stress measures (Parental Stress Scale, Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale), and enjoyment ratings. Although standardized motor outcomes showed limited overall change across the group, one youth participant demonstrated improvements in motor function, three caregivers showed reductions in stress, and enjoyment ratings were high for both youth (7–10/10) and caregivers (9–10/10), with participants identifying engaging gameplay and relaxation as key benefits. These preliminary findings suggest that family-centered VR interventions may be feasible and acceptable in pediatric rehabilitation settings, though the small sample size and limited motor improvements indicate the need for larger controlled trials before drawing firm conclusions. BCBAs considering VR-based interventions should recognize the potential dual benefit for family systems while recognizing that current evidence supports feasibility and acceptability rather than robust motor skill acquisition outcomes.

Abstract

Aims To explore the feasibility, satisfaction with, enjoyment of, acceptability, and preliminary effects of joint virtual reality (VR) sessions targeting physical engagement in youth with disabilities and relaxation in caregivers.Methods In a mini-ethnographic case study, four youth–caregiver dyads completed weekly 30-minute immersive VR sessions over four weeks. Youth (aged 10–20 years) with cerebral palsy (n = 2), autism with motor dyspraxia (n = 1), and CHARGE syndrome (n = 1) used the AstroSurfer app targeting motor functions. Caregivers simultaneously engaged with the TRIPP® app for relaxation and meditation. Pre- and post-intervention measures included the Trunk Control Measurement Scale and the Quality of Upper Extremity Skills Test (youth), the Parental Stress Scale and the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (caregivers). Enjoyment ratings (0–10, at each session) and exit interviews were completed.Results Although standardized motor outcomes showed limited overall change over the intervention period, improvements were observed in one youth participant. Reductions in stress were noted for three caregivers. Enjoyment ratings were high (7–10/10 for youth; 9–10/10 for caregivers), with engaging gameplay and relaxation identified as key benefits.Conclusions Findings provide preliminary support for the potential feasibility and acceptability of family-centered VR interventions in pediatric rehabilitation, warranting evaluation in larger studies.
