A Comprehensive Behavior Analytic Consultation Program in a Pediatric Acute Care Hospital

Elizabeth Klinepeter, J. Reece, Evan Dalton, Sohail Nibras, Marco Costilla, Lisa Ayoub-Rodriguez et al.  ·  Behavior Analysis in Practice  ·  2026

Summary

# Summary: A Comprehensive Behavior Analytic Consultation Program in a Pediatric Acute Care Hospital The primary objective was to describe the development and implementation of the Behavior Analysis and ASD/IDD Team (BAAT) care model in an inpatient pediatric hospital and provide preliminary outcome data demonstrating its potential impact on patient and staff safety and satisfaction. The BAAT service model integrated Board Certified Behavior Analysts (BCBAs) and Registered Behavior Technicians (RBTs) into standing hospital teams with six core components: consultation triage, direct behavior analytic services (assessment, intervention, and caregiver training), staff training, crisis response, behavioral data collection, and multidisciplinary collaboration. Key findings included that BAAT developed behavioral function hypotheses in 84.7% of cases, directly implemented function-based interventions in 74.0% of cases, provided caregiver training to 81.0% of families, and demonstrated promising preliminary trends in safety outcomes and social validity data from families and hospital staff; additionally, over half of the patients had no prior history of behavioral therapy services. For BCBAs and autism practitioners, this study demonstrates that comprehensive behavior analytic consultation can be feasibly integrated into acute care hospital settings to address unmet service needs, support multidisciplinary teams, reduce behavioral crises and safety incidents, and improve hospitalization experiences for children with autism or intellectual and developmental disabilities.

Abstract

Children diagnosed with intellectual and developmental disability (IDD), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), often present to pediatric acute care hospitals for behavioral crises or medical care. Board Certified Behavior Analysts (BCBAs) and Registered Behavior Technicians (RBTs) have important skill sets and can partner with multidisciplinary teams to advance the care of hospitalized patients with ASD or IDD. We describe the care model used by our Behavior Analysis and ASD/IDD Team (BAAT) in an inpatient medical setting. Components of the BAAT approach include: (1) consultation triage, (2) direct behavior analytic services (including assessment, intervention, and caregiver training), (3) staff training, (4) crisis response, (5) behavioral data collection, and (6) multidisciplinary collaboration. We include representative data from 2 years of the BAAT service to demonstrate the individualized and innovative application of our approach, as well as preliminary outcome data to show the potential impact of the program. This study highlights the key role of BCBAs and RBTs in acute inpatient medical settings, particularly in optimizing the care of children with ASD or IDD and behavioral health needs, while disseminating behavior analytic practice.

Introduction

Children diagnosed with intellectual and developmental disability (IDD), including those with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), frequently present to pediatric acute care hospitals for care (Hand et al.,2019; McMaughan et al.,2022). One reason for hospitalization is for behavioral health crises, with data indicating children with ASD may be two to 11 times more likely to be hospitalized for mental health or disruptive behavioral concerns than children without ASD (McMaughan et al.,2023). A second reason for hospitalization is for medical care, as children with ASD or IDD may have challenges with accessing specialized care in outpatient settings (Hand et al.,2019). Once hospitalized, children with ASD or IDD often experience increased boarding time (Lokhandwala et al.,2012). This is particularly true for children experiencing behavioral health crises, who may present to pediatric hospitals for stabilization and eventual discharge home given the shortage of specialized psychiatric placements in the United States (Dalton et al.,2023a; Klinepeter et al.,2024b; Morledge & Diamond,2023; Taylor et al.,2019). Even upon discharge home, there is a shortage of appropriate outpatient intervention services in many communities, perpetuating the cycle of hospitalizations (Greer,2023; Rast et al.,2022).

Regardless of the reason for admission, children with ASD or IDD encounter various challenges related to communication, sensory sensitivities, change in routine, lack of structure, pain, and participation in medical procedures while in the hospital (Dalton et al.,2023b; Huber et al.,2023; Klinepeter et al.,2024a; Muskat et al.,2015; Thom et al.,2020; Wilson & Peterson,2018). Moreover, clinical staff report limited knowledge in meeting patients’ needs (Morris et al.,2019; Muskat et al.,2015; Thom et al.,2020). Together, these challenges can result in increased agitation and aggression, leading to use of chemical and mechanical restraints, patient and staff injuries, and family dissatisfaction (Calabrese et al.,2024; DePorre et al.,2025; Hazen et al.,2020). In addition to the potential for immediate harm, adverse experiences in medical settings may dissuade families from pursuing future care, representing a public health disparity and leading to poor long-term health outcomes (Krahn et al.,2015; Okoro et al.,2018; Vohra et al.,2014).

To address this critical need, researchers have highlighted the role of behavior analysts on hospital-based, multidisciplinary teams (DePorre et al.,2024; Huber et al.,2023; Klinepeter et al.,2024b; Laprime et al.,2024a). Board Certified Behavior Analysts (BCBAs) are graduate-level professionals who are uniquely trained to assess for functions (or “causes”) of behavior, implement function-based interventions, train staff and families, and collect data to inform decisions based on the principles of Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA; Behavior Analyst Certification Board [BACB],2025a). Registered Behavior Technicians (RBTs) are entry-level professionals who are specially trained to augment these services under the supervision of BCBAs (BACB,2025b). In the inpatient medical setting, BCBAs and RBTs can guide use of overarching behavioral support strategies, including during crisis events (Call et al.,2022), collaborate with psychiatrists on necessary psychotropic medication adjustments (Bird et al.,2022), and contribute to medical care plans of children with medical conditions that include a behavioral component (Friman & Piazza,2011). In turn, BCBAs and RBTs can work with hospital teams to advance and improve hospital-based care for individuals with ASD or IDD, answering recent calls to action (Harris et al.,2024; Weitzman et al.,2024).

Despite the promise of their contributions, research directly examining the roles of BCBAs and RBTs in inpatient medical settings is very limited. One pilot study demonstrated the acceptability of a behavior analytic service in a pediatric hospital that included direct implementation of preference assessments, functional analyses, and differential reinforcement-based interventions by BCBAs (Sanders et al.,2020). However, the service was part of a research protocol rather than provided by a standing team. Other studies have pointed to decreases in staff injuries after incorporation of BCBAs or behaviorally-trained clinicians as part of crisis prevention programs or crisis response teams (Bernstein et al.,2022; Dalton et al.,2021; Laprime et al.,2024a; Statile et al.,2023). Although these studies highlighted the role of BCBAs in development and implementation of system-level interventions (e.g., identifying appropriate behavioral personal protective equipment [PPE], aiding in screening for risk of aggression, promoting use of generalized prevention and reinforcement-based strategies, and providing overarching staff training), these studies did not expand on other duties of BCBAs or RBTs in relation to direct care of specific patients.

Notably, we were only able to locate one study (Laprime et al.,2024b) that described the role of both BCBAs and RBTs in a comprehensive behavioral program integrated in an acute medical setting. Laprime and colleagues (2024b) used a case study from one inpatient medical unit to highlight the consultative nature of the service, the workflow used by their clinicians, the components of direct service delivery (including assessment, intervention, and staff and caregiver training), and a method for collecting and analyzing patient-specific data (plan-do-study-act cycles). They also speculated on potential safety outcomes related to the service, provided staff social validity data to highlight acceptability of the service, and provided supplemental data in a parallel study (Laprime et al.,2024a). Although description of the scaling of the service across their hospital and inclusion of program-specific data beyond the case study was limited, their studies together provided an initial example and justification for development of a standing behavior program in an inpatient medical setting.

BCBAs and RBTs have the potential to play important roles as part of standing pediatric hospital teams; however, research on the exact nature of these roles is still in its infancy and multiple exemplars of programs are warranted. Therefore, our aim is to describe the development and implementation of the care model used by our “Behavior Analysis and ASD/IDD Team” (“BAAT”) in an inpatient medical setting at a pediatric acute care hospital. We supplement this description with program data to characterize the BAAT service and provide preliminary outcome data to highlight the team’s potential impact. We conclude with a call for incorporation of BCBAs and RBTs into healthcare systems to promote innovative care practices and positive patient and staff outcomes.

Discussion

Children diagnosed with IDD, including those with ASD, are increasingly presenting to pediatric hospitals for care, often due to unmet behavioral health and medical needs in the community setting. Once in the hospital, children with ASD or IDD are at increased risk of experiencing agitation events that can result in safety concerns for themselves, their family members, and hospital staff. There is a paucity of research on comprehensive behavior analytic consultation programs in acute care hospitals, particularly on inpatient medical units, and we aim to fill this gap through our study. Specifically, we describe a care model that incorporates both BCBAs and RBTs as hospital team members, includes both direct service delivery and crisis response, and incorporates examples of approaches to support multidisciplinary teams and monitor data. Furthermore, data demonstrate that this care model is promising in terms of safety outcomes and is valued by families and hospital staff alike, with the potential to continue improving hospitalization experiences and outcomes through innovation.

Although use of behavior analytic procedures is well established in outpatient settings, we expand on existing literature to demonstrate how direct behavior analytic services can be provided in inpatient medical settings to address the needs of children, families, and hospital staff. For example, our data demonstrate that over half of the patients BAAT followed had no history of behavioral therapy services of any type, with over three-quarters having no history of ABA services specifically. By providing a direct assessment, intervention, and caregiver training service in the hospital, we were able to begin filling an unmet service need for many families. Despite the unique safety concerns in a pediatric hospital environment and the brief nature of service delivery, BAAT was able to develop hypotheses regarding functions of behavior in 84.7% of cases and directly implement function-based intervention components in 74.0% of cases, including providing caregiver training on at least one occasion to 81.0% of families. Therefore, BAAT was able to adhere to the hallmark function-based approach of behavior analytic practice (Cooper et al.,2019). BAAT was also able to provide these services as part of a standing team with promising preliminary trends in safety, behavior reduction, and social validity data, expanding upon research protocols that demonstrate feasibility of direct behavior analytic service delivery in hospitals (Sanders et al.,2020).

In addition, the BAAT model addresses crisis response and staff training needs within pediatric hospitals in a comprehensive and integrated manner. Although the role of BCBAs on crisis response teams has been discussed (Laprime et al.,2024a), we provide a model of a service that includes crisis response and staff training on top of direct service delivery. As part of our integrated model, BAAT provides in-the-moment education, system-level education, and written, individualized recommendations for patients. Despite existing calls for nurses and hospitalists to incorporate such duties into their practice (Scarpinato et al.,2010; Thom et al.,2020), BAAT’s ability to adopt these roles address time and knowledge constraints, which is a highly relevant concern in a large system like ours (Morris et al.,2019). We also emphasize early staff response to agitation and empower our POAs and nurses to call RBTs at early signs of escalating behavior, which has the potential to mitigate avoidable de-escalation events and staff injuries (DePorre et al.,2025). Further highlighting the value of BAAT, over half of the children who met BAAT consultation criteria and were admitted at our community campuses were transferred to our main campus for specialized care. Anecdotally, BAAT also receives frequent inquiries to provide training and education to learners and staff across the wider hospital and local community, highlighting the value of BAAT in broader system wide education.

Finally, the BAAT service model impacts the multidisciplinary care of children with medical needs in hospitals, including through use of our multi-modal data monitoring methods. Specifically, over a quarter of patients BAAT followed had a primary or co-occurring indication of “behavioral-other” or “medical participation,” demonstrating that the role of a behavior analytic team can extend beyond management of disruptive behavioral concerns in inpatient medical settings. That is, BAAT was able to function in a manner consistent with the role of behavior analysts in the field of behavioral pediatrics (Friman & Piazza,2011) offering additional value to the hospital system. In addition, BAAT plays a key role in helping inform decisions of physician teams. For example, concerns such as constipation, sleep, nutrition, and polypharmacy may exacerbate behavioral concerns in children with ASD (Spencer et al.,2013; Thom et al.,2020). In our model, BAAT works closely with pediatric hospitalists and psychiatrists to share data to inform necessary medical interventions or medication adjustments. In turn, such collaborative models can lead to reductions in problem behavior, address family goals and disposition concerns, and improve post-hospitalization outcomes (Bird et al.,2022; Klinepeter et al.,2024b), while simultaneously answering a call and building upon a framework for integrating behavior analysis into non-behavioral communities (Lerman,2024).
