Effectiveness of Virtual Reality Interventions for Enhancing Assertiveness and Conflict Resolution in Adolescents and Young Adults with Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Scoping Review

Alejandro Sánchez-Ronco, Rosabel Roig-Vila, Miguel Cazorla  ·  Review Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders  ·  2026

# Summary This scoping review examined whether virtual reality (VR) interventions improve assertiveness and conflict resolution skills in adolescents and young adults with Autism Spectrum Disorder. The authors analyzed 15 studies published between 2015 and 2024, following PRISMA guidelines, to evaluate VR's effectiveness as a social skills training modality. The reviewed studies demonstrated that VR interventions led to improvements in assertiveness, emotional expression, and social reciprocity, with longitudinal data showing that 10 to 30 sessions of immersive VR produced significant gains in Theory of Mind, empathy, and emotion recognition; however, skill generalization to real-life contexts remained variable across studies, and findings were limited by small sample sizes and lack of rigorous control groups. For BCBAs and autism practitioners, VR interventions may serve as a supplementary tool to provide structured, low-anxiety practice environments for social skills development, though broader implementation requires addressing accessibility barriers (particularly for individuals with sensory sensitivities), conducting larger-scale studies, and integrating VR with other established intervention strategies to maximize generalization to natural environments.

Abstract

This scoping review examines the effectiveness of virtual reality (VR) interventions in improving assertiveness and conflict resolution among adolescents and young adults with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). ASD often involves difficulties in social interaction, communication, and emotion regulation, limiting interpersonal relationships. Traditional training may be less effective due to cognitive and emotional differences, while VR offers immersive, controlled environments for practicing social skills. Following PRISMA guidelines, 15 studies (2015–2024) were analyzed, showing that VR improves assertiveness, emotional expression, and social reciprocity. Immersive VR better supports skill generalization to real-life contexts, though challenges remain regarding accessibility and individual adaptation. Larger studies are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes and explore integration with other intervention strategies.

Results

The reviewed studies provide empirical weight to the theoretical frameworks of Virtual Reality (VR) as a transformative pedagogical tool for individuals with ASD. A primary pillar of this research is the theory that VR acts as a cognitive scaffold, bridging the gap between abstract rules and real-world application. This is strongly supported by longitudinal data from Herrero (2020) and Ip et al. (2024), who demonstrated that repeated exposure ranging from 10 to 30 sessions leads to significant gains in Theory of Mind, empathy, and emotional expression. Similarly, Lorenzo et al. (2016) reinforced this by showing that 10-month interventions lead to sustained social adaptability and improved emotion recognition. These findings suggest that VR environments provide the structured scaffolding necessary for individuals to internalize social reciprocity and build permanent competencies before attempting them in physical social spheres.

The theoretical premise that VR provides a “safe failure” environment–where individuals can practice high-stakes social skills without the debilitating anxiety of real-world consequences–is further reinforced by results concerning negotiation and conflict resolution. Studies by Zhao et al. (2018) and Zhao et al. (2022) demonstrate that collaborative virtual environments foster improvements in group decision-making, though outcomes often depend on the level of active user participation. This is validated by the work of Ke (2018) and Ke et al. (2022), which found that children with high-functioning autism showed marked gains in cognitive flexibility when navigating complex, simulated tasks. By providing a psychological safety net, these interventions enable the trial-and-error process essential for developing assertiveness and mastering nuanced social dynamics.

A significant theoretical shift has also occurred from focusing on “sensory intensity” to “authentic immersion,” as seen in the results of the CHARISMA-VST protocol. Johnson et al. (2022) evaluated this Low Immersion VR (LIVR) tool and found significant improvements in social inference and attribution after 10 sessions. Notably, the study confirmed that structured coaching and the “social presence” of an avatar are the primary drivers of success rather than the hardware’s graphical fidelity. However, a recurring trend across several of these studies, including Moon (2021) and Cheng et al. (2015), is the reliance on small, homogeneous sample sizes, which limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. While these short-term interventions suggest that VR can increase cooperation and social initiation, the consistency of skill generalization to real-life contexts remains variable, as also noted by Rutten et al. (2003).

Finally, the research identifies critical “boundary conditions” that must be addressed to refine the current theoretical framework. Soltiyeva et al. (2023) and Yuan (2018) identify a gap in Universal Design, noting that sensory hypersensitivity in children with severe ASD can render head-mounted displays (HMDs) ineffective. Yuan (2018) explored the CAVE system as a non-wearable alternative that improves emotional expression, though it faces logistical and cost barriers. Furthermore, Ip et al. (2018) observed that while emotional expression improved, recognition did not always follow, suggesting a need for more comprehensive approaches. Consequently, researchers like Ke (2013) emphasize that future frameworks must prioritize personalization, accessibility, and the design of programs that specifically facilitate the transition of learned skills into daily life.

Ultimately, these findings suggest that VR is a valuable tool for teaching social and emotional skills in individuals with ASD. However, larger-scale studies are still required to assess its long-term effectiveness across more diverse populations and severity levels. Moving forward, combining VR with other intervention strategies could significantly enhance these benefits, allowing for more contextualized, sustainable, and ecologically valid learning experiences.

Discussion

This scoping review confirms that VR serves as more than just a technological novelty; it functions as a robust cognitive scaffold that bridges the gap between abstract social rules and functional application for individuals with ASD. The collective evidence suggests that the effectiveness of VR-based interventions is rooted in their ability to provide a “safe failure” environment. By decoupling social practice from the immediate anxiety of real-world consequences, these systems allow for the iterative development of assertiveness and social reciprocity, directly supporting the experiential learning frameworks established in recent literature.

The synthesis of results highlights a critical theoretical shift: the success of an intervention appears to depend less on sensory intensity and more on authentic immersion. The findings indicate that both immersive and non-immersive systems can be effective, provided they prioritize structured feedback and social presence. However, the variability observed in skill generalization suggests that the theoretical design of virtual scenarios–specifically their modularity and ecological validity–remains a determining factor in how successfully a user transitions skills from the virtual to the physical world.

Despite these advancements,the evidence base is currently constrained by consistent methodological biases, including a lack of blinded assessments and a predominance of small-scale pilot studies that lack rigorous control groups. Furthermore, the ’sensory-cognitive gap’ identified in this review highlights a limitation in current Universal Design theories. For VR to be a truly inclusive therapeutic tool, the framework must evolve to include more adaptable, non-wearable interfaces that accommodate the full neurodiverse spectrum, particularly those with severe sensory sensitivities. Future research should prioritize large-scale, longitudinal studies.
