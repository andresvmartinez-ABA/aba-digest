Reward Prediction-Error Fidelity: a testable hypothesis linking reinforcement learning mechanisms to applied behavior analysis for autism and neurodevelopmental conditions.

Cesarano, Sebastian G D  ·  Frontiers in behavioral neuroscience  ·  2026
SOCIAL VALIDITY
78/100Highly relevant as it directly connects reinforcement learning mechanisms to ABA practice with autism/neurodevelopmental conditions, addresses reinforcer assessment and reinforcement science, and proposes testable hypotheses with single-case experimental designs, though it leans slightly more theoretical than clinical application.
Summary

# Summary: Reward Prediction-Error Fidelity Hypothesis The primary research objective is to propose and formalize a testable hypothesis linking computational neuroscience accounts of reinforcement learning (reward prediction error) to applied behavior analysis practices, by identifying the quality of the learning signal produced on each reinforcement occasion as a key moderator of procedural effectiveness. The proposed approach decomposes Reward Prediction-Error (RPE) Fidelity into four dimensions—temporal credit, contingency, prediction, and outcome value—and formalizes them within an actor-critic computational model in which these dimensions combine multiplicatively rather than additively, with effects dependent on learner history. The hypothesis generates nine empirical predictions that can be tested using single-case experimental designs (e.g., factorial manipulations of delay and reinforcer magnitude, trial-level multilevel models, noncontingent reinforcement with signaling manipulations, and crossover interactions between learning stage and predictability). The practical implication for BCBAs is that procedures delivered with equal procedural fidelity can produce substantially different learning outcomes depending on the quality of the learning signal on each occasion, suggesting that optimizing these four dimensions—not merely following written protocols—is essential to effective teaching, with special attention to how effects interact and how the learner's developmental stage and reward processing characteristics modulate these relationships.

Abstract

Applied behavior analysis (ABA) has developed an effective technology of reinforcement delivery, including Discrete Trial Training, Natural Environment Teaching, and token economies, largely on functional-behavioral grounds. Computational neuroscience has independently produced formal accounts of reinforcement learning centered on prediction errors and credit assignment. I propose a testable hypothesis connecting the two: the effectiveness of a reinforcement procedure depends on the quality of the learning signal that its delivery produces on each occasion, a property I term Reward Prediction-Error Fidelity (RPE Fidelity). The property is decomposed into four dimensions, temporal credit, contingency, prediction, and outcome value, each corresponding to a construct long studied in behavior analysis. Formalized within an actor-critic model with eligibility traces, the four dimensions enter the learning update multiplicatively, which implies that they cannot compensate for one another and that the effect of each depends on the others and on the learner’s history. The combination rule distinguishes the hypothesis from the separate behavioral constructs it draws on, and its dependence on learning history distinguishes it from steady-state quantitative models of reinforcement. I situate the hypothesis within an integrative framework, discuss its application to autistic learners, including differences in reward processing and volatility estimation and the role of learner assent, and compare it with procedural fidelity, quantitative behavioral theory, and alternative learning models, including attentional, timescale-invariant, successor-representation, and Bayesian accounts. I derive nine predictions, specifying for each the result that would favor the hypothesis and the result that would favor its strongest competitor, and outline a staged research programme compatible with single-case experimental designs.

Introduction

Reinforcement is one of the few constructs studied with comparable rigor at both the neurobiological and the behavioral level of analysis, yet the dialogue between these two literatures remains surprisingly limited in practice. Neuroscience has, over the past three decades, produced a detailed mechanistic account of how the brain represents and updates value, from the discovery of the dopaminergic reward prediction error to the cellular dynamics of eligibility traces and synaptic plasticity. Independently, applied behavior analysis (ABA) has developed, refined, and empirically validated a technology of reinforcement delivery (encompassing procedures such as Discrete Trial Training (DTT), Natural Environment Teaching (NET), and token economy systems) almost entirely on functional-behavioral grounds, with comparatively little explicit reference to the underlying neurobiology.

This separation is not merely an academic curiosity: it means that many long-standing ABA practice principles (the immediacy of reinforcement, the differential durability of intermittent versus continuous schedules, the value of pairing tangible and social reinforcers) are empirically well supported but rarely explained in terms of the mechanisms that produce them. Conversely, neurobiological findings on reward learning rarely engage with the applied, real-world contingency management systems in which reinforcement is most systematically and repeatedly deployed. This article addresses that gap by proposing a specific, testable hypothesis; it does not attempt an exhaustive review.

I propose the following hypothesis. The effectiveness of a reinforcement procedure depends on the quality of the learning signal produced by each delivery, beyond the identity of the reinforcer and adherence to the written protocol. I term this property Reward Prediction-Error Fidelity (RPE Fidelity) and decompose it into four dimensions: temporal credit, contingency, prediction, and outcome value. Each of these variables corresponds to a construct that behavior analysis has long studied, namely immediacy of reinforcement, response-reinforcer contingency, schedule arrangement, and motivating operations. The claim specific to this article concerns how they combine. When the four dimensions are formalized as terms of a single learning update (Section 2.3), they enter multiplicatively rather than additively. This implies that they cannot compensate for one another: a severe deficit in any one of them limits learning regardless of the others, and the effect of improving one depends on the level of the rest. The combination rule distinguishes RPE Fidelity from the separate behavioral constructs it draws on, its dependence on the learner’s history distinguishes it from steady-state quantitative models of reinforcement, and together they make the hypothesis testable in a discriminating way (Section 7.2). RPE Fidelity is also distinct from procedural fidelity: a procedure can be delivered exactly as written while producing a degraded learning signal.

The argument proceeds from neural and computational foundations to applied reinforcement procedures, competing models, and discriminating empirical predictions.

Discussion

This article has proposed that the effectiveness of a reinforcement procedure depends on the quality of the learning signal its delivery produces, and has formalized this proposal so that its central claim can be tested. The four dimensions of RPE Fidelity correspond to variables that behavior analysis has long recognized; what the formal account adds is a combination rule, under which the dimensions multiply rather than add, and a dependence of their effects on learning history. Placed alongside procedural fidelity, quantitative behavioral theory, and alternative learning models, the hypothesis makes predictions that coincide with these accounts in some respects and diverge from them in others, and the points of divergence define its empirical content.

The literature reviewed here also cautions against treating reinforcement design as a purely mechanical optimization problem. Informativeness that supports learning when calibrated to the stage of acquisition can, if unpredictability is maximized and sustained indefinitely, carry risks analogous to those documented in the behavioral addiction literature (Section 5.2). Withdrawal of extrinsic reinforcement carries a population- and reinforcer-dependent risk of undermining engagement (Section 5.1). Effectiveness itself is not a sufficient criterion of good practice unless the learner’s assent and the social validity of goals and procedures are maintained (Section 6.6).

At the level of a teaching procedure, RPE Fidelity is a moderator: it determines how effective a given procedure is on a given occasion. Because the four dimensions are properties of each delivery rather than of the procedure as designed, they enter statistical models as occasion-level predictors of learning, and the moderation claim corresponds to a specific empirical expectation: the dimensions should account for variance in learning outcomes within procedures, including between occasions on which the same procedure is delivered with equal procedural fidelity. The dimensions can first be tested as trial- or session-level predictors in multilevel models of acquisition, with trials nested within sessions and sessions within learners, controlling for procedural fidelity, reinforcer preference, baseline performance, and task difficulty. A stronger test fits Equation 5 and its competitors directly to trial-by-trial data, preferably estimating parameters for each learner, and compares their predictions on held-out observations. Single-case designs standard in behavior analysis, including alternating-treatments and parametric designs, allow both tests within individual learners.

Holding contingency and predictability constant, a factorial manipulation of the delay to the first reliable reward signal and of outcome value (for example, a highly versus moderately preferred reinforcer, or delivery with and without pre-session access) will produce effects on learning rate that combine multiplicatively. Because a product becomes a sum under a logarithmic transformation, Equation 5 predicts that the two effects will interact on the raw scale of learning rate but will be approximately additive on the logarithmic scale. This result would favor M3. Additivity on the raw scale would favor accounts in which the dimensions contribute independently. Examining both scales also separates a genuine multiplicative combination from floor effects, which can produce apparent interactions on the raw scale alone. Multiplicative combination of reinforcer magnitude and delay is also predicted by hyperbolic discounting accounts of delayed reinforcement (Critchfield and Kollins, 2001) and is compatible with the coupling structure of Killeen's (1994) model. Prediction 1 therefore discriminates M3 from additive, compensatory accounts rather than from M1; the tests against M1 are Predictions 3, 5, and 7. A finer difference concerns the form of the delay function: with a single trace parameter, Equation 5 predicts an exponential decline of the update with delay, whereas discounting accounts predict a hyperbolic decline. Because a mixture of trace time constants can approximate a hyperbolic function, this difference is informative only under the single-parameter assumption.

Noncontingent reinforcement delivered during acquisition slows the acquisition of a target response, an effect already well documented (Rescorla, 1968; Hammond, 1980). The prediction specific to M3 concerns the mechanism. Because contingency acts through the value of the context (Section 2.3), the effect of noncontingent reinforcement should be attenuated when noncontingent deliveries are signaled by a distinct stimulus, which then acquires the value that would otherwise accrue to the context. Attenuation would favor M3. No attenuation would favor accounts in which learning is determined by ΔP as a statistical quantity, since signaling leaves ΔP unchanged. The basic phenomenon has been reported in animal instrumental learning (Dickinson and Charnock, 1985) but has not been tested in applied settings, where noncontingent reinforcement and the acquisition of new responses frequently co-occur within the same programme.

Holding reinforcement density, outcome value, and contingency constant, reduced predictability introduced before a response is reliably established will impair acquisition, whereas the same reduction introduced once the response is well established will support maintenance and resistance to extinction. A crossover interaction between stage of learning and predictability would favor M3, in which the effect of predictability depends on the learner’s current expectation. An effect in a single direction across stages would favor accounts in which predictability acts through a fixed parameter.

Reinforcers showing measurable signs of habituation will be associated with attenuated learning relative to novel or varied reinforcers of comparable nominal value. Pairing a habituating reinforcer with praise will slow this decline, but only for learners for whom praise already functions as a reinforcer, as established by prior reinforcer assessment (Section 6.6). A protective effect of pairing restricted to these learners would favor M3. An effect absent in learners for whom praise is an established reinforcer, or equally present in learners for whom it is not, would count against the placement of social reward as a moderator of outcome value.

At parity of procedural fidelity, reinforcer magnitude, reinforcement density, and task difficulty, the four dimensions will explain a significant increment of variance in acquisition beyond M0 and M1, and in maintenance beyond M0 and the context-reinforcer relation specified by Behavioral Momentum Theory. In addition, Equation 5, once fitted to trial-by-trial data, will predict held-out learning data more accurately than Killeen's (1994) model. Failure of either test would count directly against the hypothesis.

A factorial manipulation of response-reinforcer delay and overall session reinforcement density discriminates M3 from timescale-invariant and retrospective accounts (Gallistel and Gibbon, 2000; Jeong et al., 2022). Under M3, with fixed trace parameters, the cost of a given delay will be independent of density. Under the alternative accounts, tolerance for delay will increase as reinforcement becomes sparser.

Adding noncontingent or alternative reinforcement to a teaching context will slow the acquisition of the target response (M3) while increasing its resistance to extinction or disruption once established (Behavioral Momentum Theory). The two predictions are compatible, and the test concerns whether measuring both outcomes reveals this dissociation. If added reinforcement failed to slow acquisition when timing and outcome value are held constant, the contingency mechanism of M3 would be contradicted.

After a reduction in outcome value before a test session conducted in extinction, responding will change immediately under goal-directed control, immediately for well-practised but not for recently modified response sequences under the successor representation, and only after contact with the devalued outcome under model-free control. This prediction constrains the interpretation of M3 rather than testing it against a single rival: the hybrid account specified in Section 2.3 would be supported if the pattern shifts from goal-directed toward model-free with extended training.

Thinning a schedule gradually or abruptly to the same endpoint discriminates M3 from latent-cause accounts (Gershman et al., 2010). Both predict some advantage for gradual thinning, but they differ in what follows a later change of context. Under latent-cause accounts, abrupt thinning leaves the earlier pattern of responding stored rather than modified, so that this pattern should return after a context change more readily than after gradual thinning. Under M3, earlier learning is modified by the prediction errors produced during thinning and no such differential return is expected. Differential return would favor latent-cause accounts, and the effect should be larger for learners who estimate environmental volatility as higher (Section 6.6).

Predictions 1 and 5 provide the primary tests of the hypothesis as a whole; failures on Predictions 6 to 9 instead discriminate among specific alternative accounts.

Empirical evaluation of RPE Fidelity requires a staged programme, beginning with measurement and behavioral validation before proceeding to neural tests.

Develop operational definitions and a coding manual for the four dimensions. Prediction Fidelity requires particular care: because it is defined relative to the stage of learning, it must be scored from the schedule parameters in place and the learner’s documented history rather than from whether learning subsequently occurred, to avoid circularity. This stage should establish inter-rater reliability, examine the factor structure of the ratings, and develop procedures for estimating the parameters of Equation 5 from trial-by-trial data, including tests of parameter recovery on simulated data.

Record the four dimensions during ongoing teaching and test whether they predict rate of acquisition, trials to criterion, retention, maintenance, and generalization, controlling for procedural fidelity, reinforcer preference, baseline performance, and task difficulty (Predictions 1 and 5).

Manipulate single dimensions and factorial pairs of dimensions while holding the others constant, including signaled and unsignaled noncontingent reinforcement, to establish causal rather than correlational evidence for each dimension and for their combination (Predictions 1–4).

Fit M3 and the alternatives in Table 4 to common trial-by-trial data and compare their predictive performance on held-out data, using the discriminating designs specified in Predictions 5–9.

Only after Stages 1 to 4 provide behavioral support for the construct, extend measurement toward physiological and neural correlates, to test whether the dimensions track the hypothesized computations directly rather than only their behavioral consequences. This stage would also test whether dopaminergic signals track prediction errors, as the name of the construct assumes, or causal associations (Section 6.10). Wearable physiological sensors used to estimate engagement (Bustos-Lopez et al., 2022), reinforcement-learning systems already used to personalize instruction for autistic learners (Semi et al., 2026), and generative AI integrated into structured parent-training protocols anchored to an explicit clinical framework, the Autism Open Clinical Model (Morfini, 2023; Morfini and Cesarano, 2025), could eventually support measurement at this stage, but none is a precondition for Stages 1–4.

Several limitations qualify the claims made here. Most of the foundational neurobiological evidence on prediction errors and reinforcement timing derives from animal models using simple operant or Pavlovian paradigms, and human evidence from applied settings remains sparse. The interpretation of dopaminergic signals as prediction errors is itself under active revision (Section 2.1), which affects the neural interpretation of the construct, though not its behavioral predictions. Evidence on reward processing and volatility estimation in autism derives largely from adult samples and laboratory tasks (Section 6.6). The overjustification literature specific to learners with intellectual and developmental disabilities remains limited in volume, and estimates of the effectiveness of comprehensive programmes are constrained by the scarcity of randomized designs. Finally, Equation 5 is a deliberately simplified model: it treats each teaching trial as ending with the outcome and does not represent the response chains, prompting procedures, and concurrent targets of ordinary instruction, which will require extensions before the model can be fitted to data from applied settings.

The hypothesis would be substantially weakened, or falsified, by any of the following: additivity of the dimensions on the raw scale rather than multiplicative combination (Prediction 1); absence of incremental or out-of-sample predictive value beyond procedural fidelity and quantitative behavioral theory (Prediction 5); absence of a stage-dependent effect of predictability (Prediction 3); or failure of noncontingent reinforcement to slow acquisition when timing and outcome value are held constant (Prediction 7). The hypothesis thus specifies in advance the pattern of results that would count against it.

The central question raised by this article is not whether timing, contingency, predictability, and value matter in reinforcement, which behavior analysis established long ago, but whether a formal account of how they combine, grounded in reinforcement-learning theory, predicts learning better than the accounts already available. If the answer is positive, practitioners would gain a principled way to evaluate not only whether a procedure is delivered as written, but whether each delivery provides the learner with a signal from which learning can occur.
