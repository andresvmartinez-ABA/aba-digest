Lessons Learned from Co-designing an iPad App and Tangible Toys with Proxies for Supporting Social Skills of Autistic Children in a Low-Resource Region

Amani Indunil Soysa, A. al Mahmud  ·  ACM Transactions on Accessible Computing  ·  2026

Summary

# Summary: Co-designing POMA iPad App for Autistic Children in Low-Resource Settings The primary objective was to analyze the methodological process of co-designing POMA (Picture-to-Object Mapping Activities), an iPad application and tangible toy system intended to support social skills development in autistic children in Sri Lanka, using therapists and teachers as proxies when direct child involvement was not feasible. The researchers conducted 10 iterative co-design workshops involving therapists and teachers, progressing through app critique, paper prototyping, wireframing evaluation, and two prototype iterations, and documented methodological challenges and adaptations throughout the process. Key findings identified five major challenges: tensions arising from Western-perspective app critique due to linguistic and cultural differences, resource constraints affecting participation, multidisciplinary design conflicts leading to design divergence and consolidation, material failures in environmental sensing requiring tangible interface redesigns, and participant turnover necessitating repeated onboarding and team alignment. For BCBA practitioners and autism researchers, this work demonstrates that proxy-based co-design in low-resource and culturally specific contexts requires ongoing methodological flexibility and contextual adaptation rather than direct application of established participatory design practices developed in high-resource Western settings. These findings are particularly relevant for practitioners developing or implementing technology-based interventions in diverse cultural and resource-limited environments.

Abstract

Technological interventions for the development of social skills among autistic children have shown promise. However, the majority of the tools have been developed from a high-resource and Western perspective and may not be culturally or materially appropriate for a low-resource environment. While the use of participatory and co-design approaches is strongly advocated for autism-related research, the direct involvement of autistic children is not always feasible due to the presence of social stigma and the constraints of the environment. In such cases, therapists and teachers are often engaged as proxies, yet the methodological implications of proxy-based co-design have not been sufficiently explored. In response to such shortcomings, this article presents a process-oriented methodological analysis of the co-design of POMA (Picture-to-Object Mapping Activities), a tangible UI-based iPad application developed to support the social skills of autistic children in Sri Lanka, with therapists and teachers acting as proxies. Through 10 rounds of iterative workshops, we present the evolution of co-design methods through breakdowns and negotiations across app critique, paper prototyping, wireframing evaluation, and two iterations of prototypes. Our analysis reveals methodological developments from (i) tensions in app critique from a Western perspective based on linguistic and cultural differences, (ii) resource constraints on participation, (iii) tensions in multidisciplinary co-design leading to design divergence and consolidation, (iv) material failures in environmental sensing leading to redesigns in tangible sensing, and (v) participant turnover leading to repeated onboarding and alignment. The findings highlight that proxy-based co-design in low-resource, culturally specific contexts is a dynamic and contingent process, requiring ongoing methodological adaptation rather than the straightforward application of established participatory practices. By focusing on challenges and adaptations, this article contributes empirically grounded methodological insights into proxy-based co-design for autism technologies, with implications for researchers working in resource-constrained and culturally diverse settings.
