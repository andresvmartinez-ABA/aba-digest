Identifying and Describing Best Clinical Practices for Children and Adolescents With Complex Communication Needs: A Scoping Review of Healthcare-Based Interventions.

Resina, Paula, Mezzatesta, Marcela, Elias, Neus et al.  ·  Journal of intellectual disability research : JIDR  ·  2025
85/100 This article directly aligns with the profile's focus on AAC devices and communication interventions for children with autism, though it lacks the specific technology-based AI/VR elements and ABA methodologies that would make it a perfect match.
Summary

This scoping review aimed to identify and describe healthcare-based clinical interventions for improving communication in children and adolescents (ages 0-18) with complex communication needs (CCN), particularly those with autism spectrum disorder. The researchers conducted a systematic scoping review following PRISMA-ScR methodology, searching 797 records and ultimately including 8 studies that met eligibility criteria. The review found that multimodal interventions combining augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), naturalistic strategies, and parent training were most effective in improving both expressive and receptive communication skills. The authors identified significant gaps in the literature, including limited studies with appropriate methodological rigor and lack of standardized intervention protocols for CCN populations.

Abstract

ABSTRACTBackgroundChildren with complex communication needs (CCN), particularly those with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), experience significant communication challenges. This scoping review analyses the existing literature on clinical interventions designed to enhance communication in these children. The aim was to describe which intervention models are being evaluated in studies, which treatments have shown positive effects and what the clinical profile of children and adolescents who benefit from these interventions is.MethodThe scoping review was conducted following the methodology for scoping reviews and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR).ResultsThe search strategy identified 797 records; eight records met all eligibility criteria and were included for review. The review found that multimodal interventions combining augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), naturalistic strategies and parent training were the most effective in improving both expressive and receptive communication.ConclusionsDespite the limited existing research, studies suggest that multimodal interventions can effectively enhance communication in children with CCN. This review highlights significant gaps in the literature, including the scarcity of studies with appropriate methodological rigour and the lack of standardised protocols for CCN interventions. There is a critical need for evidence‐based guidelines, professional training and policy development to ensure equitable access to effective interventions.

Introduction

Children with intellectual disabilities often have delayed or different communication development due to cognitive, motor and sensory limitations (Visser et al. 2017). Their movements may be involuntary, making it hard to interpret their communicative behaviour (Ogletree et al. 2012). They face challenges in both expressive (sending messages) and receptive (understanding messages) communication (Visser et al. 2017).

Dhondt et al. (2019) found these children mainly use pre‐ or proto‐symbolic behaviours to request or reject, with less social intent. Communicative partners must know the child well and use context to interpret messages, which can still be difficult, making the child dependent on others' understanding.

Intellectual disability and communication difficulties are common in children with autism, characterised by social and communication challenges and repetitive behaviours (APA 2013). Autism prevalence is rising, affecting 1 in 36 US children (Maenner et al. 2023), with more cases identified early (Shaw et al. 2025). Around 25%–30% of these children have minimal or no functional language, having complex communication needs (CCN) (Brignell et al. 2018; Norrelgen et al. 2014).

CCN are defined as the needs of individuals with severe disabilities who are nonverbal and have limited comprehension abilities but can benefit from nonverbal communication means such as augmentative and alternative communication (AAC; Reichle et al. 2019). AAC is designed to support both expressive and receptive communication, offering alternative ways to convey messages and to understand them (Light and McNaughton 2012).

Communication is essential for social development, and unmet CCN can lead to social exclusion (Murphy et al. 2018), torpid clinical evolution, low academic achievement and poor quality of life, highlighting AAC's potential to support social inclusion and adaptive functioning. The presence of verbal language at age 5 years has been described as a stable predictor of favourable outcome in adaptive and social functioning later (Anderson et al. 2007) and is related to the person's nonverbal cognitive ability and joint attention span (Mundy et al. 1990; Paul et al. 2008; Watt et al. 2006). In addition, an enhancement in communication leads to an improvement in adaptive behaviour (Dominick et al. 2007).

Most communication‐focused intervention studies target preschool‐age or younger children (Rogers et al. 2012; Vismara et al. 2009), showing that children aged 0–6 with developmental disabilities benefit in expressive and receptive communication, as well as in social interaction, through the use of AAC systems (Leonet et al. 2022). Early intervention usually involves fostering joint attention, as it plays a key role in the development of symbolic communication and language. It is a prerequisite for meaningful communication (Mundy et al. 1990), and difficulties in joint attention are closely linked to language delays, particularly in children with intellectual and developmental disabilities (Paul et al. 2008; Watt et al. 2006).

AAC includes both low‐tech tools—such as symbol boards, communication books, visual schedules and gestures—and high‐tech solutions like speech‐generating devices (SGDs) and communication apps (Beukelman and Light 2020). AAC interventions have proven effective across all age groups, types of disabilities and communication skill levels, for both expression and comprehension (O'Neill et al. 2018). Reviews support the use of AAC with individuals with developmental disabilities (Millar et al. 2006; Dada et al. 2021), including SGDs (Rispoli et al. 2010) and the Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS; Preston and Carter 2009).

Despite its proven benefits, there are no standardised protocols for CCN intervention, and many professionals do not follow evidence‐based practices. Few specialised services offer high‐quality intervention and follow‐up (Reichle et al. 2019). A report by ASHA (2011) revealed that few speech‐language pathologists specialise in AAC, and those who do devote only 5% of their time to AAC programming, highlighting a strong need for better training in AAC assessment and intervention (Reichle et al. 2019).

Although children with ASD fall under the broader group of individuals with CCN, their communication profiles are often uniquely complex and require tailored interventions. ASD‐specific research is extensive and methodologically robust, particularly regarding evidence‐based practices rooted in behavioural techniques, such as functional assessments, antecedent modifications, reinforcement strategies and extinction procedures (Steinbrenner et al. 2020; Fuentes et al. 2021).

Early interventions for young children with ASD typically focus on developing joint attention, imitation and play. Programs like Joint Attention, Symbolic Play, Engagement, and Regulation (JASPER) (Kasari et al. 2010), The Hanen Program (Weitzman et al. 2017) and PACT (Green et al. 2010) aim to foster these skills, although some studies report limited impact on word production (Goods et al. 2013). Another intervention paradigm is the early start Denver model (ESDM; Rogers and Dawson 2010), which combines behavioural and developmental principles and has shown positive outcomes in promoting social communication.

Multimodal interventions, integrating speech with AAC, are recommended to enhance communication (Brady et al. 2013). AAC approaches such as PECS (Bondy and Frost 1994) are widely used in autism and linked to gains in verbal language (Preston and Carter 2009; Flippin et al. 2010). SGDs are also effective for children with autism (Rispoli et al. 2010).

Despite promising results, the literature on AAC for children with autism and other developmental disabilities is limited by methodological weaknesses, such as small sample sizes (e.g., n < 10; Charlop‐Christy et al. 2002). More rigorous research is needed to validate outcomes (Crowe et al. 2022).

Moreover, there is a gap in the literature concerning communication interventions that are implemented by multidisciplinary teams, including speech therapists and health specialists, in clinical settings. This health context may facilitate the implementation of multimodal interventions that combine AAC with other treatments.

To address these challenges, it is necessary to conduct a scoping review to identify evidence‐based treatments available for children and adolescents with CCN in healthcare settings, as well as to highlight key gaps in the current literature.

Given these gaps, a scoping review is needed to identify existing evidence‐based treatments for children and adolescents with CCN in healthcare contexts and to highlight areas lacking sufficient research. Though minimally verbal children with autism are part of the broader CCN category, their distinct communication traits—such as atypical joint attention, symbolic play and pragmatic language—justify their separate analysis in reviews (Tager‐Flusberg et al. 2005; Kasari et al. 2010; Paul et al. 2008). This distinction allows for more targeted and effective intervention planning.

This scoping review explores research on practices to improve communication in children with CCN, implemented by clinicians, speech therapists and other healthcare professionals. By mapping these interventions, it provides an overview of current best practices to guide professionals and enhance communication outcomes. The focus on clinical settings reflects the need to integrate communication interventions within the overall treatment plan to support holistic development.

Identify which interventions for children and adolescents aged 0–18 years with CCN have been described in the literature as showing promising results.

By addressing these objectives, this scoping review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of existing practices and recommendations, ultimately contributing to improving healthcare outcomes and quality of life for children and adolescents with CCN.

Results

The search strategy identified 797 records; eight records met all eligibility criteria and were included for review. The review found that multimodal interventions combining augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), naturalistic strategies and parent training were the most effective in improving both expressive and receptive communication.

Discussion

Despite the limited existing research, studies suggest that multimodal interventions can effectively enhance communication in children with CCN. This review highlights significant gaps in the literature, including the scarcity of studies with appropriate methodological rigour and the lack of standardised protocols for CCN interventions. There is a critical need for evidence‐based guidelines, professional training and policy development to ensure equitable access to effective interventions.
