Application of artificial intelligence techniques for supporting people with ASD: a systematic literature review

Summary

# Summary This systematic literature review examined the application of artificial intelligence techniques to support individuals with autism spectrum disorder by analyzing 21 peer-reviewed studies following PRISMA guidelines and Kitchenham and Charters methodology. The review characterized AI-based interventions across multiple dimensions, including target population demographics, AI technologies employed (machine learning, natural language processing, virtual agents), assessment methods, and implementation barriers. Key findings revealed that approximately 62% of AI-based interventions focused on improving social and communication skills primarily in children, while only 24% targeted daily living activities and independence—predominantly for adults—and that severity levels were frequently unspecified across studies, with effectiveness predominantly measured through user experience questionnaires and usability tests rather than standardized longitudinal frameworks. For BCBA practitioners, this review highlights a critical gap in evidence-based AI applications for supporting adults with Level 1 ASD in practical daily tasks; practitioners should recognize that most current AI tools prioritize social-communication training in younger populations, while ethical considerations such as privacy, technology dependence, and personalization to individual needs remain substantial implementation barriers requiring attention.

Abstract

The application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in assisting individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has gained significant attention in recent years. However, existing studies exhibit notable differences in their objectives, targeted populations, and methodological approaches. This systematic literature review (SLR) followed the widely recognized guidelines of Kitchenham and Charters and the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement, resulting in a final selection of 21 studies. The review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of AI-driven solutions designed to support individuals with ASD. The review examines key aspects such as the age and severity level of the target population, the specific objectives of AI-based solutions, the employed technologies, the methods used to assess their effectiveness, and the challenges faced in their implementation. A comparative analysis of existing SLRs highlights gaps in current research, including the lack of standardized evaluation metrics and the limited focus on personalized, real-world applications. Findings indicate that most AI-based interventions prioritize improving social and communication skills, especially in children, whereas significantly fewer solutions focus on assisting with daily tasks to enhance independence, particularly in adults. While AI technologies such as machine learning, natural language processing, and virtual agents offer promising avenues for assistance, ethical considerations, accessibility constraints, and adaptation to individual needs remain critical challenges. This review provides a structured synthesis of the state-of-the-art in AI-assisted interventions for ASD, offering insights into future research directions aimed at developing more inclusive and effective support systems.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurological condition that affects the way people communicate, learn, behave and interact with their environment and with others [1]. Autistic individuals often experience challenges to communicate and interact with other people in their environment, as well as limited autonomy due to difficulties performing certain daily tasks at work, education, or in any other area of life.

In the last 50 years, the prevalence of ASD has been increasing exponentially, reaching an increase of approximately 700%. So much so that, in 1975, one case was detected for every 5000 children, rising to one for every 36 in the last decade [2].

The National Institute of Mental Health [3] defines certain aspects on which support for people with ASD should be focused:

Reduction of behaviors that may interfere with daily functioning;

According to the criteria of the American Psychiatric Association (APA) in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) [4], ASD can present in three levels of severity, depending mainly on two factors: deficits in communication and social skills, and repetitive and restrictive behavior patterns. These levels are:

Requiring support (Level 1). The individual can show interest in certain social interactions but has difficulties in initiating them. In addition, he/she has problems when changing activities and loses autonomy in new contexts or in those in which there is not a defined structure.

Requiring substantial support (Level 2). The existing deficit in verbal communication is more notable and their social behavior is more atypical than in level 1. In addition, the reaction to changes is worse, even presenting anxiety, and repetitive behaviors alter their relationship and adaptation to the environment.

Requiring very substantial support (Level 3). The individual has very limited communication and may even be incomprehensible. In addition, behaviors are so stereotyped that they affect all aspects of daily life.

Levels 2 and 3 logically receive the most attention and resources, while level 1 tends to be overlooked in terms of assistance and support.

Most initiatives targeting this group, such as research funding or dedicated support, are primarily directed toward children with ASD, even though 1 in 45 adults are affected by the disorder [2] and have their own support needs. These challenges are often related to difficulties in maintaining employment or managing daily tasks, which prevents them from developing with complete independence [5].

In this area, the use of Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) offers numerous advantages [6]. Through interactive and personalized approaches, such as virtual reality and the use of mobile applications, it is possible to improve the social, communicative and practical skills of these people, as well as to support them in their daily life activities [7]. ICTs allow the solutions offered to be adapted to the needs of each user, thus improving accessibility, customization and user experience.

Within the broad field of ICT, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as one of the most transformative enablers of adaptive and personalized digital support. AI encompasses a wide range of techniques, including machine learning, natural language processing, and expert systems, which allow digital tools to perceive, learn, and respond intelligently to user needs. The rise of AI has led to the recent emergence of a new generation of conversational systems, such as Google’s Gemini or OpenAI’s ChatGPT. Thanks to their natural language processing capabilities, these Large Language Models (LLMs) have become powerful tools to support a multitude of tasks [8], both in the form of chatbots [9] and virtual assistants [10]. These systems can be applied to multiple fields, including customer service or healthcare [11]. In this area, the use of LLMs offers a wide range of advantages and opportunities [12], highlighting the possibility of providing continuous assistance without time and location constraints. This is particularly beneficial for patients living in rural or remote areas, as it minimizes the need for face-to-face care and helps to reduce costs. These models also facilitate personalized care, as they are based on the analysis of the patient's clinical history and adapt the context according to their conditions, needs and preferences. It has been proven that their use promotes adherence to treatment and accompaniment in complying with medical instructions, and can help optimize the diagnostic process, providing improvements in assessment and intervention support [13]. In addition, these conversational systems improve communication with the patient, which is particularly useful in the case of care for people with difficulties in expressing themselves or understanding complex sentences and contexts [14]. Consequently, virtual assistance based on the use of LLM can be particularly valuable in developing technological solutions to support people with ASD to improve their daily life skills [15,16,17].

Concrete examples of AI-based interventions have already demonstrated their potential impact [18,19,20]. For instance, social AI-powered robots have been successfully used to foster social interaction and emotion recognition in people with ASD, showing improvements in engagement and turn-taking behavior [21,22,23]. Similarly, virtual reality environments that simulate real-life social scenarios, such as shopping or classroom situations, have proven effective for training social and communication skills in both adolescents and adults in a safe and controlled setting [24,25,26,27]. Recent pilot studies using ChatGPT-based conversational systems have also shown promise for children and adults with ASD, assisting them in planning daily routines, managing anxiety-provoking situations, and engaging in adaptive conversational training [28,29,30]. These cases exemplify how AI can effectively complement therapeutic or educational interventions, enhancing autonomy and self-confidence in people on the spectrum.

However, despite the growing presence of AI-based solutions in healthcare and assistive technologies, there is still a major challenge: most existing proposals are focused on children or on the educational and therapeutic context, leaving aside adults with ASD, especially those with level 1 severity. This group faces significant difficulties in performing daily activities such as planning routines, moving around independently, managing unexpected events, or interacting socially in open environments [31]. Yet, technological proposals to assist them in these everyday contexts remain scarce or fragmented.

This challenge motivates the present study, which proposes systematically analyzing scientific literature on the use of AI to assist people with ASD, with special attention to adults with level 1 severity. The main objective is to identify, classify and analyze the state-of-art AI based solutions developed to support individuals with ASD, especially those focused on improving autonomy and independence in daily life. To achieve this goal, the study addresses the following specific objectives (SOs):

To characterize the profile of the target population for AI-based solutions to support individuals with ASD as described in the literature, identifying their ages, severity levels, and the areas of daily life for which those solutions are designed;

To categorize the AI techniques employed in the existing AI-based solutions to support individuals with ASD and analyze their integration with other assistive technologies;

To examine the methodological quality of the metrics and assessment methods employed to evaluate the effectiveness of the current AI-based solutions to assist autistic people;

To identify the technical, ethical, and usability barriers preventing the successful adoption of AI-based assistive solutions for people with ASD.

By addressing this research gap, this work contributes a comprehensive overview of the current landscape of AI-based support for ASD, highlighting unexplored opportunities and challenges that need to be overcome to design more inclusive and effective assistive technologies for adults on the spectrum.

Discussion

The discussion synthesizes and contextualizes the overall findings of the SLR, highlighting key patterns, implications, and the scientific contribution of this study. It emphasizes broader insights and contextualizes the results relative to prior work.

The distribution of studies by publication year (Fig.2) shows a clear acceleration in interest over the past five years, a trend also observed in reviews such as Balobaid and Said [33], Barua et al. [36], and Iannone and Giansanti [37], particularly driven by the rise of generative language models such as ChatGPT. This reflects a shift from rule-based or sensor-driven systems toward more adaptive, conversational, and context-aware technologies, as evidenced by Rehman et al. [34].

In contrast with prior reviews such as [32,37] (Sect.2), which primarily examined educational, communicative, or healthcare-related applications, the present SLR broadens the analytical scope by integrating demographic and methodological dimensions. Whereas Jaliawala and Khan [32] and Barua et al. [36] emphasized children-focused interventions, the analysis of the primary studies included in this review confirms that this emphasis persists, while also revealing that adults, particularly those with ASD Level 1, remain significantly underrepresented. As shown in Table5, only a small number of studies explicitly target adults (e.g., Xygkou et al. [42], Jang et al. [43], and Choi et al. [44]), and even fewer focus on supporting autonomy and daily living activities. Similarly, while Rehman et al. [34] and Balobaid and Said [33] highlighted mobile and AI-driven tools, they did not systematically assess methodological quality or evaluation metrics, aspects explicitly addressed in this review. This review explicitly identifies how effectiveness has been measured across studies, revealing a predominance of user experience questionnaires and usability tests over standardized or longitudinal evaluation frameworks. Finally, our synthesis aligns with Iannone and Giansanti [37] in recognizing ethical and accessibility concerns but extends this discussion by systematically identifying recurring technical, ethical, and methodological drawbacks reported in the primary studies, such as privacy issues [44,47], technology rejection [48,55,58], and the limited ability of generalized solutions to address individual needs [43,57].

The scientific contribution of this SLR lies in three main areas. First, it identifies critical research gaps, particularly the scarcity of interventions that foster independence in daily activities for adults with ASD. Although some studies address autonomy and routine planning (e.g., Choi et al. [44], Gyori et al. [53], and de Oliveira Barros et al. [59]), these represent a minority compared to the large number of works focusing on social and communication skills. Second, it provides a structured mapping of existing interventions, offering guidance for future research and development. Third, it highlights the potential of emerging generative AI technologies and multimodal systems for delivering personalized and context-adapted assistive solutions.

In addition, the synthesis reveals that while a subset of studies (approximately 30% of the total) demonstrates relatively higher methodological rigor, such as Jang et al. [43], Choi et al. [44], and Porayska et al. [56], many papers rely on limited participant descriptions and short-term evaluation methods. As summarized in Table5, participant severity levels are frequently unspecified, and effectiveness is often measured through self-reported questionnaires or basic usability tests. This pattern, observed in studies like Franze et al. [45] and Singh et al. [48], suggests that methodological maturity in AI-for-ASD research remains uneven, with few studies adopting standardized, replicable, or longitudinal evaluation designs.

A clear pattern observed in the reviewed studies, exemplified by the robotic interventions proposed by Guerrero et al. [57] or the virtual agents by Razavi et al. [58], is the predominant focus on improving social and communication skills, particularly for children and adolescents with ASD. Out of the 21 studies analyzed, approximately 62% addressed social skill development, whereas only around 24% targeted the support of daily living activities and independence, mainly for adults (e.g., the routine planner by Choi et al. [44]). As illustrated in the concept map (Fig.4, Sect.5), the prevalence of interventions focused on social and communication skills contrasts sharply with the scarcity of studies addressing autonomy and daily life activities. This highlights a significant research gap in AI-driven interventions aimed at practical, everyday tasks, especially for adults with ASD Level 1, a population largely underrepresented in prior literature [75].

The implications for practice suggest that AI interventions are primarily oriented toward domains with easily measurable outcomes, such as communication and social interaction (e.g., virtual agents and conversational systems designed for social skill training [56,57]), while support for independence requires more complex, context-sensitive solutions. Studies addressing daily living skills and autonomy (e.g., Gyori et al. [53] and Choi et al. [44]) highlight the need for personalized, adaptable systems capable of operating in real-world contexts. Supporting adults in daily life activities (including self-care, planning, and task organization) could significantly enhance autonomy and quality of life [76,77]. The findings also reveal central challenges regarding ethics and usability, including privacy concerns, accessibility limitations, over-dependence on technology, and potential biases in AI models, particularly in cloud-based and generative AI systems [44,78,79]. These challenges underscore the need for standardized evaluation frameworks and adaptive solutions that account for the heterogeneity of ASD [80].

Overall, the synthesis underscores a research landscape that is both promising and fragmented. The growing adoption of AI-based assistive technologies reflects a positive trend toward inclusivity and personalization, yet the field still requires consolidated frameworks for evaluation, ethical validation, and real-world deployment. Strengthening interdisciplinary collaboration between AI researchers, ASD experts, and end-users will be essential to ensure that future developments translate into tangible improvements in autonomy, quality of life, and social integration for adults with ASD [81,82,83].
