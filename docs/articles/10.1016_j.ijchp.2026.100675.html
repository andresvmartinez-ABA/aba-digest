Adaptive VR intervention on social-cognitive skills in children with ASD: a feasibility study

Luna Maddalon, Alberto Altozano, M. E. Minissi, J. Marín-Morales, Gary Lovatón Romero, Thomas D. Parsons et al.  ·  International Journal of Clinical and Health Psychology  ·  2026
TECHNOLOGY IN ABA
72/100Highly relevant feasibility study on VR-based social skills intervention for school-age children with ASD using adaptive technology and behavioral outcome measures, directly aligned with core interests in technology-assisted communication/social skills and ASD populations, though limited by small sample size and absence of functional analysis methodology.
Summary

# Summary: Adaptive VR Intervention on Social-Cognitive Skills in Children with ASD This feasibility study examined whether a novel adaptive virtual reality (VR) system could enhance social-cognitive abilities in children with autism spectrum disorder. Twenty-three children aged 6–8 years with Level 1 ASD completed four sessions of VR-based training that presented Theory of Mind tasks through gamified scenarios with a virtual character, supported by an online adaptive engine providing real-time hints and feedback and an offline engine adjusting difficulty levels between sessions based on performance. Significant improvements were observed on the Theory of Mind Test Battery (ToMTB), with post-test scores (M = 13.2, SD = 1.8) exceeding pre-test scores (M = 11.9, SD = 2.1, p = 0.041); additionally, participants demonstrated progressive gains in accuracy across sessions (χ² = 20.62, p < 0.001) and reduced reliance on hints (χ² = 46.05, p < 0.001), while kinematic data showed increased whole-body movement and electrodermal activity indicated elevated tonic and phasic activation levels (all p < 0.001 for Session 1 versus Sessions 2–4). However, no significant changes emerged in caregiver-reported Social Responsiveness Scale scores (p = 0.656), suggesting the intervention produced measurable training-specific cognitive gains that have not yet translated to broader real-world social functioning as perceived by parents. BCBAs implementing VR-based social-cognitive interventions should recognize that adaptive systems show promise for targeted skill instruction in controlled environments, though practitioners should plan extended intervention durations and implement additional generalization strategies to support transfer of learned skills to naturalistic settings, while remaining cautious about interpreting brief improvements as evidence of generalized social behavior change.

Abstract

This study introduces and evaluates a novel virtual reality (VR) adaptive system that advances the state of the art by tailoring social-cognitive training to individual performance, addressing gaps in traditional interventions for children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). A sample of 23 children (aged 6–8 years) diagnosed with Level 1 ASD participated in a four-session program integrating traditional Theory of Mind tasks with gamified, adaptive VR environments. The online adaptive engine provides real-time support with hints (question repetition), feedback, and reinforcement strategies, while the offline engine adjusts difficulty levels between sessions based on performance. Pre- and post-test assessments using the Theory of Mind Test Battery (ToMTB) and Social Responsiveness Scale evaluated participants' progress. Statistical analyses, including generalized linear mixed models and the Aligned Rank Transform, were used to examine behavioral and biosignal metrics such as motor movement and electrodermal activity (EDA). Results revealed significant improvements in ToMTB post-test scores, suggesting that the adaptive system positively influenced ToM abilities, while no significant changes were observed in the SRS-2 scores. Behavioral analysis indicated progressive accuracy gains and reduced reliance on hints across sessions. Kinematic analysis revealed that whole-body acceleration and displacement increased across sessions, while EDA indicated a significant rise in tonic mean, tonic variance, and phasic variance levels. These findings demonstrate the feasibility and potential efficacy of using adaptive training VR systems to address social-cognitive challenges in individuals with ASD. Future research should expand sample sizes and explore follow-up effects to optimize VR-based interventions.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by challenges in social interaction, communication, and restricted, repetitive behaviors and interests (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). A recent systematic review estimates global ASD prevalence at ∼1% (∼1 in 100 children; Zeidan et al., 2022), whereas a WHO review estimated ∼1 in 127 children as of 2021; Fig.s vary by methodology and region (World Health Organization, 2021). This growing prevalence highlights significant clinical challenges and an increasing demand for innovative therapeutic approaches to address the limitations of traditional treatments, including accessibility, time, and financial burdens (Alcañiz et al., 2022a). Recent research has increasingly focused on interventions to enhance social-cognitive skills in children and adolescents with ASD — particularly in Level-1 severity — using approaches such as social and social-cognitive skills training, and technology-mediated approaches such as VR and computer-based social training (Didehbani et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2023; Narzisi et al., 2024; Soares et al., 2021; Tseng et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2025). Socio-cognitive skills exist along a continuum of complexity, beginning with foundational abilities like face perception and coordinated attention, and extending to more sophisticated mental processes such as understanding others' beliefs and intentions, making moral judgments, and navigating interpersonal choices (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Hutchins et al., 2008; Wellman et al., 1990). These deficits often become more pronounced during the preschool and early school years, typically around the age of six, a critical developmental period for Theory of Mind (ToM)—the cognitive ability to attribute thoughts, beliefs, and intentions to oneself and others (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Parr & Waller, 2011). To address these deficits, traditional therapies like Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) combine structured and naturalistic methods to promote social-cognitive development. ABA employs reinforcement techniques to encourage positive behaviors and decrease problematic ones, enabling gradual skill acquisition (Lovaas, 1987). Standardized tools like the Theory of Mind Battery Test (ToMTB) are also used in programs to enhance children's understanding of social cues, perspectives, and appropriate social responses (Hutchins et al., 2008). Within this broad socio-cognitive framework, ToM is a higher-order construct that involves the ability to infer others’ beliefs, intentions, and perspectives. In the present study, ToM was considered the primary target construct guiding the intervention design, as the training activities were directly inspired by the developmental structure of the ToMTB. Foundational abilities such as emotion recognition and desire-based reasoning were therefore addressed as supporting processes contributing to ToM development, rather than independent training targets

Building on the theoretical foundations and evidence supporting technology-based interventions, the field is increasingly adopting integrated approaches that combine traditional methods with technology-driven solutions, such as virtual reality (VR) (Minissi et al., 2024; Parsons, 2016). This fusion offers a promising avenue for enhancing ASD interventions by creating more personalized and engaging therapeutic experiences (see Lorenzo et al., 2020; Mesa-Gresa et al., 2018; Parsons, 2016, for reviews). These tools can complement therapists' efforts by improving precision, personalization, and engagement in therapeutic care. Technology-driven methods provide interactive and enjoyable experiences for children that enhance motivation and participation in treatment (Doulou & Drigas, 2022; Parson & Carlew, 2016; Parsons et al., 2019; Venturini & Parsons, 2018). Among emerging technologies, VR stands out because it creates immersive environments where users can practice real-world skills in a safe, controlled setting, enabling the transfer of skills to everyday contexts (Bölte et al., 2016; Chaidi & Drigas, 2022a, 2022b; Parsons et al., 2017; Slater et al., 2009). This makes VR effective for addressing various ASD-related challenges, such as social skills, emotional regulation, and motor coordination, by providing safe, ecological simulations that may be difficult or overwhelming for children with ASD to navigate in daily life (Chaidi et al., 2024; Hocking et al., 2022; Moon & Ke, 2024; Serret et al., 2014; Vallefuoco et al., 2022). While such environments may support the development of transferable skills, the present study focuses primarily on feasibility and engagement within the training context, rather than directly evaluating real-world behavioral generalization.

The ecological validity of adaptive VR interventions is particularly relevant for their translation into educational and therapeutic contexts (Drigas & Sideraki, 2024). Compared to traditional computer-based programs, VR offers immersive, context-rich environments that simulate everyday social settings, enabling children to experience and practice social interactions in realistic yet controlled conditions (Ke & Im, 2013; Parsons, 2015; Zeh & Meng, 2025). A key strength of VR is its adaptability, enabling customized environments that align with specific therapeutic goals. Adaptive VR systems have gained attention for their potential to deliver personalized interventions that align with the diverse needs of children with ASD (Maddalon et al., 2024a). Adaptive interventions are guided by clinical decision-making protocols that specify when and how to adjust treatment intensity or dosage based on key performance indicators (Alcañiz et al., 2022b). Unlike nonadaptive methods—where the settings remain constant or are adjusted without regard to the participant’s progress—adaptive systems continuously tailor the intervention to optimize individual outcomes (Zahabi & Abdul Razak, 2020). VR systems with adaptive engines can automate and adjust therapeutic content, using performance metrics such as accuracy and reaction time to adapt the therapeutic process to the child's responses (Maddalon et al., 2024a). This could be accomplished through a programmable engine that autonomously gathers input data, such as performance metrics, stores them, and uses pre-programmed heuristic rules to make decisions to adapt the training and deliver it to the user as output (Vaughan et al., 2016). Adaptive interventions vary in the degree of adaptation, ranging from highly structured to more flexible. They can involve behavioral adaptations or integrate biosignals, enabling a dynamic adjustment of the therapeutic process based on the child’s responses (Maddalon et al., 2024a; Parsons & Reinebold, 2012). The adaptive component further enhances ecological validity by dynamically adjusting task difficulty, pacing, and feedback according to each child’s psychophysiological and behavioral responses. By continuously modulating task load and emotional pacing in real time, the system approximates the individualized scaffolding that teachers or clinicians naturally provide, thereby promoting ecologically valid practice that transfers more effectively to everyday school or therapy routines (Alcañiz et al., 2022b; Maddalon et al., 2024a).

The use of psychophysiology and biosignals in clinical research involving VR and gaming technologies is advancing consistently (Kowallik & Schweinberger, 2019). Based on the embodiment of human perception, emotion, and behavior, psychophysiology reveals cognitive and emotional processes through brain and body signals, complementing traditional behavioral observations (Cacioppo et al., 2007). Studies on ASD populations typically collect clinical, behavioral (e.g., performance scores, reaction times), self-report, or psychophysiological data. Clinical data is considered the gold standard for assessing symptoms, while behavioral data often tracks task performance. Although they provide insights into arousal, stress, and emotion, self-reports are limited by participants' misunderstandings, inaccurate recollections, and automatic affective processes that bypass conscious control (Parsons & Courtney, 2016; Parsons et al., 2013). This is especially relevant in child studies, where self-reports can be unreliable. Psychophysiological metrics, or biosignals, such as motor movements, electrodermal activity (EDA), and eye movements, provide insights into cognitive and emotional internal states not always visible through behavior, enhancing non-verbal communication and social behaviors in ASD population (Alcañiz et al., 2020a; Alcañiz et al., 2022a; Kowallik & Schweinberger, 2019; Maddalon et al., 2024a). Biosignals improve assessments of emotional states in children who struggle with verbal or facial expression despite challenges in cost-effectiveness and ease of use (Liu et al., 2008).

EDA reflects sympathetic activation and has been widely used to index emotional and attentional engagement (Boucsein, 2012; Critchley, 2002). Within the current feasibility framework, EDA served as an exploratory, secondary indicator of participant engagement during training. According to the tripartite model of affective states in interactive learning (Chanel et al., 2011), optimal engagement is characterized by moderate arousal situated between boredom (low activation) and anxiety (high activation). Indeed, consistent and moderate increases in tonic and phasic EDA components across sessions can indicate sustained engagement and adaptive emotional activation, suggesting that participants tolerate and interact meaningfully with the virtual environment without overstimulation or disengagement (Kowallik & Schweinberger, 2019; Parsons & Courtney, 2016). Conversely, abnormally low or highly variable arousal levels could reflect inattention, stress, or poor system usability. Thus, EDA serves as a nonverbal index of emotional feasibility, complementing behavioral metrics by quantifying whether the intervention elicits appropriate levels of activation compatible with effective learning and comfort in children with ASD. As state-of-the-art, existing adaptive systems employed in ASD children's training rely on strategies like level switching, adjusting task difficulty based on predefined performance thresholds, and delivering feedback to tailor interventions to individual needs (Maddalon et al., 2024a). Most studies emphasize real-time adaptivity, in which systems dynamically adjust content during sessions to enhance engagement and learning outcomes (Jyoti & Lahiri, 2019, 2022; KB & Lahiri, 2016; Kuriakose & Lahiri, 2016; Lahiri et al., 2012). Research indicates that integrating a scaffolding strategy by adjusting difficulty and feedback has consistently improved training by adapting intent and capabilities and fostering critical thinking (Maddalon et al., 2024a). Current studies target social-cognitive deficits but primarily focus on usability or address treatment for other ASD-related deficits, such as gross motor skills (Hocking et al., 2022) or executive functions (Vallefuoco et al., 2022). Focusing mainly on system usability or short-term outcomes leaves a significant gap in understanding how these systems can be applied in longitudinal therapeutic studies aimed at developing socio-cognitive skills in children with ASD (Maddalon et al., 2024a). To our knowledge, there is a literature gap in the absence of studies that use performance data to adapt training for socio-cognitive skills in treatment studies rather than focusing on usability. Therefore, it is essential to move beyond a primary focus on usability and explore the feasibility of utilizing adaptive VR interventions for training socio-cognitive skills in children with ASD. To address this gap, we have developed a VR-based adaptive intervention designed to adjust based on children’s performance to enhance socio-cognitive abilities. This study seeks to evaluate the feasibility of the intervention and its impact on children's outcomes, providing valuable insights into the potential for longitudinal therapeutic applications.

Drawing upon advancements in VR-based ASD interventions that integrate traditional techniques with advanced technologies (Minissi et al., 2023b; Parsons & Reinebold, 2012; Vallefuoco et al., 2022), a novel treatment inspired by the traditional ToMTB was developed. ToMTB is a psychometric assessment and does not provide repetitive practice, immediate corrective feedback, or adaptive difficulty—features necessary for skill acquisition. We therefore preserved ToMTB’s construct structure while translating each domain into interactive, adaptive VR modules. The VR format allows children to practice ToM-related reasoning within context-rich, multimodal social situations that closely resemble everyday experiences, ensuring theoretical coherence between the adaptive VR intervention and the underlying construct. This combination of in-task ecological realism and adaptive personalization offers clear advantages over static, paper-based, or video-based assessments: it enhances motivation, provides immediate feedback, and aligns with evidence suggesting that immersive, adaptive interventions can improve learning outcomes and engagement in ASD (Alcañiz et al., 2022a; Ke & Im, 2013).

The intervention was carefully structured following an in-depth analysis of the ToMTB’s core components. The original tasks were organized and condensed into six key clusters that reflect the ToM developmental trajectory, then gamified for delivery in a customized VR environment. Each game corresponded directly to established developmental stages, as operationalized in the ToMTB (Hutchins et al., 2008), with individual activities targeting specific areas to comprehensively address social-cognitive functioning.

Children interacted with a virtual human in a park setting and participated in six games. The intervention dynamically adapted to participants’ performance using ABA principles, with behavioral data guiding adjustments. The adaptive system operated based on pre-programmed rules informed by the children's performance, providing two types of adaptations: online adjustments (e.g., hints) during gameplay and offline modifications (e.g., changes in difficulty levels) between sessions.

The present study extends preliminary findings reported in Maddalon et al. (2024b), which focused on a single game (Game 3) and a smaller subset of participants. Here, we report the full six-game adaptive intervention and examine behavioral, physiological, and kinematic outcomes across the complete sample.

This exploratory study aims to assess the feasibility of the VR-based adaptive intervention described above for promoting ToM development in children with ASD. Feasibility was evaluated using a combination of primary clinical indicators and secondary process measures. Primary outcomes included pre–post changes in social-cognitive functioning assessed through standardized questionnaires (ToMTB and SRS-2), which served as exploratory indicators of potential training responsiveness. Secondary process measures included behavioral performance during gameplay (accuracy and reliance on hints) and kinematic and electrodermal activity (EDA) patterns recorded during the sessions. These measures were used to examine participant engagement and interaction with the adaptive VR system. It was hypothesized that post-test results would show significant improvements over pre-test results. Secondary hypotheses included: (a) children’s accuracy in responses would improve across sessions as the need for external support decreased, aligning with findings that adaptive interventions foster skill acquisition and independence over time (e.g., Almirall, & Chronis-Tuscano, 2016; Cengher et al., 2018); and (b) biosignals data such as EDA and motor movements would reflect any significant changes in children's implicit activation levels throughout the sessions, potentially indicating the feasibility of the intervention and its impact on training outcomes. This is consistent with evidence suggesting that physiological and kinematic metrics are reliable indicators of the impact on training outcomes (e.g., Lee et al., 2016; Minissi et al., 2023a; 2023b).

Methods

Twenty-three children diagnosed with ASD, aged 6 to 8 years, participated in this study. The sample included 20 males (M = 91.75 months, sd = 10.30) and 3 females (M = 91.00 months, sd = 12.28). ASD diagnoses were clinically confirmed through the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2; module 2 or 3). The gender imbalance in the sample (approximately 7:1 male-to-female ratio) slightly exceeds the commonly reported 4:1 male-to-female ratio in ASD diagnoses (Loomes et al., 2017). Participants met DSM-5 criteria for ASD, Level 1 (requiring support), as determined by specialized psychiatrists integrating clinical records, ADOS-2 findings, and adaptive behavior profiles (Vineland-II; Lord, 1999; Sparrow, 2005). All participants exhibited an intellectual quotient (IQ) of 85 or higher, as assessed by the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fifth Edition (WISC-V) or the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV), depending on their age (Wechsler, 2014, 2015). These assessments also evaluated memory, verbal comprehension, and visual-spatial ability required for the designed VR intervention. Three months prior to the VR intervention, the Child Behavior Checklist 4-18 (CBCL/4-18) was administered to screen for potential comorbidities (Achenbach, 1991). Children with severe conduct disorders, severe anxiety, or epilepsy were excluded from participation. Social-cognitive abilities were assessed at baseline through individual administration of the ToMTB (Hutchins et al., 2008) and the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS; Constantino et al., 2003). Recruitment was conducted through the Fundació Assistencial Mútua Terrassa, a specialized clinical center for ASD in Spain, without offering monetary compensation. Caregivers received full details of the study procedures and provided written consent for their children’s participation. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethical Committee of Fundació Assistencial Mútua Terrassa (ID: P/23-111/).

A novel portable hybrid reality device integrated into a custom-designed environment optimized for semi-immersive experiences was implemented in this study. Here, ‘portable’ denotes transportable across clinical/school sites (assembled on location) rather than handheld/wearable; the configuration avoids a fixed CAVE installation while retaining large-screen benefits. The effectiveness of this system in facilitating immersive human-computer interaction has been previously validated with the ASD infant population (Maddalon et al., 2023). The hardware configuration featured an 86” LG 16/7 SoC TV signage display, a computer with an Nvidia GeForce GTX 1070 graphics card, and an IntelOnline adaptive engine i7-7700 CPU (3.60 GHz) alongside a desktop system used to project a control panel. Unlike traditional VR setups with head-mounted displays (HMDs), which are often poorly tolerated by children with ASD due to sensory sensitivities and device constraints, this semi-immersive system uses a stationary display for a more comfortable, accessible experience (Alcañiz et al., 2020a; 2020b). Building on the proven efficacy of the CAVE system, which requires ample fixed space, this portable hybrid reality device offers a flexible and accessible alternative while retaining the benefits of large-screen projection (Maddalon et al., 2023; Minissi et al., 2024).

Additionally, an Emotibit wearable physiological sensor was integrated into the system for EDA recording, providing a non-invasive method to monitor physiological responses, particularly important for children with heightened sensory sensitivities. The Azure Kinect DK (Microsoft Corporation, 2019) was positioned 50cm above the ground, directly beneath the display (see Fig. 1). The Azure Kinect DK was conFig.d with a depth camera operating at 640 × 576 pixels and 16 Hz, enabling gesture-based interaction and projecting an unfilled virtual body that mirrored real-time participant movements. The participant's interaction area was confined to a 2-square-meter space demarcated on the floor, located 1.5 meters from the screen, ensuring accurate body tracking and natural interaction within the virtual environment. This gesture-based interaction method has been validated in previous studies and found effective for the target population (Maddalon et al., 2023, 2024b; Minissi et al., 2023b). The control panel, developed with the Laravel framework, communicated bidirectionally with the 3D environment via the MQTT protocol, enabling real-time interaction with the virtual environment. This exploratory study primarily aims to assess the feasibility of the VR-based adaptive intervention while providing preliminary exploratory data on social-cognitive outcomes. Clinical experts agreed on a minimum duration of one month for an effective training program, with participants attending approximately one session per week. The in-game difficulty was incrementally adjusted based on the participant's performance, while the number of sessions remained fixed to control this variable. If a positive linear progression in accuracy was observed, the sessions were distributed across three difficulty levels, culminating in a final session focused on learning consolidation. This approach was made possible by creating two versions for each game's difficulty level to minimize potential learning effects. If participants failed to pass a level or completed all three difficulty levels of one version, the alternative version reinforced the learned skills. An increase in the difficulty level of a game introduced more answer options (ranging from 2 to 4 answer options; see Adaptive engine section for more details).

The VR game, developed using Unity3D software, featured interactive scenarios in which participants engaged with a virtual human resembling a child in a virtual playground environment (see the Modeling virtual environment and virtual human section of the supplementary material for details). To ensure natural, realistic communication, the virtual human was enhanced with lifelike facial expressions and a natural voice using Audio2Face and Autodesk Maya, enabling precise lip-syncing.

The intervention consisted of a virtual experience that included a tutorial (only at the onset of the first session), a baseline scene for EDA recording, a presentation scene, six games with two versions, and a final scene. A custom-designed tutorial was developed to assess participants' interaction with the virtual elements before the training began. The tutorial scene has been taken from a previous study (Maddalon et al., 2023). The baseline scene featured a 90-second EDA recording period, during which participants stood still, avoided limb movement, and focused on their breathing, guided by a virtual human in a relaxation scene. The experimenter monitored participants during the breathing exercise, ensuring contextual consistency by recording the EDA baseline within the virtual environment rather than using traditional methods. The presentation scene introduced the virtual human and familiarized participants with the virtual playground where the games occur. The final scene concluded the session and dismissed the participant.

Each game followed a consistent structure: an introductory scene in which the virtual human provided context for the upcoming task, verbal questions with time for the participant to respond, and a final scene marking the game's completion. For response selection, participants' virtual bodies were displayed alongside choice options, and their choice was indicated by holding their hands within the designated interaction area of the chosen option for 1.5 seconds (see Adaptive engine section for more details). The six games were designed to foster individual training in the social-cognition subskills measured in the ToMTB (see Fig. 2). Although the overall game structure remained consistent, the system dynamically adapted the training method for each participant based on their performance, using a combination of online and offline engines.

The online adaptation engine monitored participants’ actions and provided real-time support through hints, explanations, feedback, and reinforcement strategies. For instance, if a child exhibited delayed responding to a task, the system provided hints, such as repeating the question. It also clarified correct answers and provided corrective feedback when errors occurred. When correct responses were detected, positive feedback and reinforcement (e.g., encouraging messages) were delivered.

The offline adaptation engine analyzed performance metrics collected during sessions—such as accuracy scores and the hints usage—and adjusted the difficulty of subsequent tasks to align with each participant's evolving abilities. A simplified pseudo-code summary of the adaptive engine is provided in the supplementary material to enhance transparency and replicability.

Hints (question repetition) were triggered if no response occurred within 10-seconds and could be delivered at most twice (maximum response window 30 seconds). If there is still no response after two repetitions, the trial timed out, and the correct answer was shown. An exception was made in Game 2, where participants had to match two distinct virtual elements, each granting 10 seconds. This simple repetition strategy was chosen to maintain engagement while minimizing external guidance. Unlike the graduated prompting hierarchy used in ABA, the present design implemented minimal, non-intrusive prompts to support autonomous learning within the adaptive VR environment.

Feedback was given immediately after each response. Correct answers were highlighted with green borders and an auditory cue, while incorrect answers were marked with red borders and a corresponding auditory cue. If participants failed to respond within the 30-second window, the correct answer was displayed visually, and an error was recorded. In Games 3 to 6, the virtual human provided verbal and visual explanations for the correct answers at the end of each task. Still, in Games 1 and 2, explanations followed each incorrect response. Upon completion of a game, a visual "Completed Game" message signaled the end of the session.

Reinforcement strategies combined both visual and auditory rewards. In Games 1, 3, and 5, participants were rewarded with a “Good Job!” message for achieving a 50% accuracy rate, while Games 2, 4, and 6 featured a colorful blowing-bubble animation as a reward.

Progression to higher difficulty levels in subsequent sessions was contingent upon achieving a 50% accuracy threshold. This was chosen because the limited number of responses in each game made majority-rule criteria unsuitable. Participants who failed to meet the 50% accuracy threshold repeated the same difficulty level in the next session.

To mitigate learning effects, two distinct versions were created for each difficulty level, featuring different stimuli and locations. The variants differ in characters and visual cues (e.g., gender, appearance), as well as task-relevant elements (e.g., perspective angles, object positions, or belief cues). This design required children to apply the same social-cognitive reasoning across varied but structurally similar scenarios, promoting within-task generalization. When participants repeated the same level in later sessions, they were presented with an alternate version of the game. The number of response options determined task difficulty: Level 1 offered two options, Level 2 three, and Level 3 four.

In Games 1 and 2, the required answers ranged from 2 to 4, starting with 2 options in Level 1 and increasing by one option in each subsequent level. In Games 3 to 6, the required answers remained fixed at 2 throughout all levels (see Fig. 1SM).

Feasibility was evaluated through primary clinical outcomes comprising standardized pre- and post-tests of social-cognitive functioning. Secondary process outcomes—including behavioral metrics (accuracy and level of external support) and exploratory biosignals (EDA and kinematics)—were employed to characterize the training process and participant engagement, rather than as direct indicators of clinical efficacy

Two assessments were employed to measure the impact of the intervention on participants' social cognition, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the VR experience's effectiveness from both the participants' and caregivers' perspectives. The ToMTB was selected as it represents a highly reliable test for evaluating the specific training conducted in this study. Additionally, the SRS-2 was used because it is a widely adopted measure for assessing the effectiveness of social training in ASD. This combination enabled a pre-post evaluation tailored to the study's objectives while incorporating a widely used research measure for broader comparability.

ToMTB (Hutchins et al., 2008). ToMTB is a standardized tool designed to evaluate ToM abilities, focusing on understanding and inferring others' mental states, such as thoughts, beliefs, desires, and intentions (Hutchins et al., 2008). The battery comprises 15 questions distributed across nine structured tasks, presented as short vignettes with increasing levels of ToM complexity. In addition to the test questions, memory control and justification questions are administered before and after the main test items to ensure comprehension. Each test question is scored on a binary scale (1 = correct, 0 = incorrect), resulting in a maximum total score of 15. The ToMTB is a widely recognized tool for assessing ToM capabilities in children with ASD (Hutchins et al., 2008).

SRS-2 (Constantino et al., 2003). The SRS-2 is a caregiver- or parent-reported questionnaire comprising 65 items to assess social communication difficulties commonly associated with ASD (Constantino et al., 2003). Each item evaluates different aspects of reciprocal social behavior, with responses rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from "0" (never true) to "3" (almost always true). The SRS-2 generates an overall score and separate scores for five subscales: social awareness, social cognition, social communication, motor mannerisms, and routines/repetitive behaviors. Higher SRS-2 scores indicate a more significant impairment in social communication skills.

Behavioral data were gathered through a custom logging system built in Unity3D, which captured key aspects of the adaptive engine and the participant's VR interactions. This system tracked various variables, such as the game number, difficulty level, version, correctness of responses, and the number of hints utilized.

Biosignal data were collected for offline analysis of kinetic and EDA metrics.

Motor movements data. Participant location data were captured through the Azure Kinect DK using the Azure Kinect Body Tracking software, seamlessly integrated into Unity3D. This software continuously tracked 32 body joints, offering an accurate model for motion analysis.

EDA data. Participants wore an Emotibit sensor on the wrist of their non-dominant hand to collect physiological data, including baseline and in-game activations, for subsequent analysis. This biometric sensor recorded EDA at 10 Hz and transmitted data via direct UDP communication through its control software, Oscilloscope (OpenBCI). Emotibit, an Arduino-based open-source sensor, used Ag/AgCl electrodes to measure physiological responses linked to sympathetic nervous system activation, reflecting cognitive and emotional arousal (Montgomery et al., 2024).

The experimental study was conducted between September 2023 and February 2024. Sociodemographic data and inclusion criteria were assessed during the three months preceding the intervention. Pre-intervention evaluations using the ToMTB and SRS-2 measures were conducted approximately two weeks prior to the first session to establish a baseline for participants' social-cognitive skills. Participants were familiarized with the portable hybrid VR system at the onset of the first session through a custom-designed tutorial. Participants chose between a male or female virtual body to enhance meta-self-recognition, which they used to navigate and interact within the system. Following the familiarization process, participants wore Emotibit sensors and then engaged in the VR experience. Each training session was conducted in a controlled environment with a room temperature of 24°C and consistent lighting conditions. In each session, children completed an initial EDA baseline recording, a presentation scene, six sequential games, and a final scene. This procedure was repeated across four sessions, each lasting approximately 30 minutes, spaced approximately one week apart to allow for rest and skill consolidation. All sessions were conducted individually in a quiet, controlled environment by trained staff experienced in working with children with ASD. Visual and auditory stimuli were kept at moderate levels to prevent sensory overstimulation. Throughout the sessions, the experimenter monitored participants' needs, providing support and recording observational notes as needed. No adverse events were reported, confirming that the intervention was well tolerated and conducted in a safe and non-threatening setting.

Post-intervention assessments were conducted around 2 weeks after the last session to evaluate changes in social-cognitive skills resulting from the VR-based intervention program.

Behavioral data was processed to derive two primary metrics: accuracy and number of hints. Accuracy was determined by analyzing individual responses recorded, coded as Correct (1) or Incorrect (0). The second metric, instead, referred to the total number of hints utilized to complete each game. Both metrics were calculated for all six games, providing objective measures of participant performance.

Initial visual inspection of the raw data revealed substantial noise in the joint position estimates, evidenced by jittering in tracked body movements, likely due to the sensor's limited precision. A smoothing procedure was applied to mitigate this issue using a moving average with a uniform window size of 5 samples (Edwards & Green, 2014). This process was iteratively repeated 5 times to generate smooth, derivable position trajectories.

Once the data were cleaned, key metrics were developed to characterize motor behavior during each game. Specifically, mean acceleration and total displacement, normalized by game duration, were calculated for each joint and grouped by body part. These metrics were chosen to reflect motor characteristics often associated with ASD. Metrics such as velocity and acceleration have been shown to characterize atypical motor signatures in individuals with ASD, prompting the use of the proposed mean acceleration metric (Alcañiz et al., 2020b). On the other hand, total displacement has also been found to distinguish ASD from TD individuals in goal-oriented scenarios (Cook et al., 2013). However, the present study normalized total movement per time to account for differences in game duration.

For total displacement, the distance covered by each joint was computed as the sum of Euclidean distances between consecutive joint positions over time. The resulting values were then normalized by dividing by game duration to account for variations in session length. To facilitate analysis at the body-region level, displacement values were averaged across joints within key anatomical regions, namely the arms, legs, torso, and head (Alcañiz et al., 2020b).

Acceleration metrics were derived by applying two successive discrete-time derivatives to the joint position data, yielding a 3D joint acceleration time series. The modulus of the 3D acceleration vector was calculated for each joint to quantify absolute acceleration. These absolute values were then averaged over the game duration for each joint and subsequently aggregated by body region, following the same grouping as displacement.

The EDA biosignal included measured changes in the skin's electrical conductance, reflecting sweat gland activity linked to sympathetic nervous system function. The EDA is commonly decomposed into two components: tonic and phasic. The tonic component reflects slow changes in baseline activation, while the phasic component captures rapid fluctuations, such as skin conductance responses (SCRs), associated with immediate sympathetic responses to stimuli.

EDA signals, however, are prone to artifacts introduced by external factors, including equipment noise, gross motor movements, speech, or irregular breathing (Society for Psychophysiological Research Ad Hoc Committee on Electrodermal Measures et al., 2012). Proper artifact detection and removal are critical for reliable analysis. Although automated methods for artifact detection are available, visual inspection is recommended for assessing data quality, especially in settings involving substantial gross motor movements, as in this study (Posada-Quintero & Chon, 2020). For this reason, manual data cleaning was performed for all EDA signals using MATLAB LEDALAB open-source software to ensure the highest possible data integrity.

After artifact correction and visual validation, subject-specific differences in physiological baselines were accounted for to enable meaningful inter-individual comparisons (Greco et al., 2015). This process involved normalizing the EDA signal recorded during the VR experience with the signal captured during the baseline resting state. The baseline's minimum and maximum EDA values were extracted, and the normalized EDA signal was computed using Equation (1) (Ghaderyan & Abbasi, 2016; Singh et al., 2013).(1)signalnormalized=signal−signalrestminsignalrestmax−signalrestmin

Once normalized, the EDA signal was filtered and decomposed using the Python NeuroKit2 package. The signal was filtered with a Butterworth filter of order 4 and a high-cut frequency of 3 Hz. Tonic and phasic components were separated via cvxEDA, a robust deconvolution method (Greco et al., 2015). To identify stimuli responses within the phasic component, NeuroKit2’s peak-finding method was employed, with a minimum peak height threshold of 0.1 µSiemens.

Key metrics were extracted to characterize the participants’ EDA responses during the relevant intervals. These included the mean and standard deviation (STD) of both the tonic and phasic components; the mean and STD of SCR recovery time, defined as the time required for an SCR to decline from its peak to half its amplitude; the mean and STD of SCR amplitude, calculated by averaging peak heights; and the number of SCRs per minute, obtained by dividing the total number of detected SCRs by the duration of the interval.

Although task parameters were individually adapted, behavior and biosignals data were analyzed across four standardized training stage sessions, representing sequential exposures to the adaptive system. This stage-based structure enabled the evaluation of group-level learning and engagement trends within the adaptive framework, while preserving individual tailoring of content and difficulty.

To assess differences between pre-and post-scores for the ToMTB and SRS-2 questionnaires, first, the outliers in both samples using the interquartile range (IQR) method were tested (Jones, 2019) with the threshold set to λ = 3 times the IQR, following standard practices. Subsequently, the normality of the distributions was tested for both samples using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A paired t-test was used to assess differences between groups if both samples were normally distributed. Conversely, if at least one sample failed to support the null hypothesis, a nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was performed instead.

A Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) was employed as the primary statistical approach to evaluate behavioral performance metrics and differences in motor movement biosignals across sessions. GLMM was chosen for its ability to account for the hierarchical structure of the data, incorporating fixed and random effects to isolate the session effect while accounting for variability introduced by participants and games.

The model (2) for each metric was expressed as follows:(2)yijk=β0+β1(Sessioni)+γj(Gamej)+δk(Userk)+ϵijk

Where yijk is the observed value for metric y from the i-th session, j-th game, and k-th participant; β0is the overall intercept; β1 represents the fixed effect of the session factor; γj and δk represent random effects; and ϵijk is the residual error. This model structure ensures that variability introduced by participants and games is properly accounted for while isolating the session effect.

GLMM assumptions were rigorously tested using the DHARMa package in R, including checks for appropriate family distribution fits, within-group deviations, and homogeneity of variance. After validating these checks using an error type I of 0.01, the metrics were analyzed using family distributions and link functions tailored to their distributions. A Gamma family with a log link was used for positively defined metrics, such as kinematic features. Count-based metrics, such as the number of hints required in games, were analyzed using a Poisson distribution with a log link. Binary outcomes, such as correct versus incorrect responses, were analyzed using a binomial distribution with a logit link.

Prior to performing statistical analyses, outliers in motor movement data were removed using the IQR method with a threshold of λ = 3 times the IQR, thereby ensuring the reliability of the motor movement analyses by minimizing the influence of extreme values.

The analysis of EDA metrics presented unique challenges due to substantial missing data and difficulties in meeting the assumptions required for parametric approaches like GLMM. Poor signal quality, device disconnections, and participant-reported discomfort caused missing data. Of the initial 92 EDA recordings (23 participants across 4 sessions), 11 were excluded due to missing baseline measurements, 26 for missing intervention recordings, 16 for poor signal quality, and 9 for excessive missing values. This left 32 valid recordings, significantly reducing sample size and homogeneity, making GLMM inappropriate for most metrics.

Additionally, the heterogeneity of the EDA recordings further hindered the alignment of samples across sessions and the inclusion of participants as random effects. The limited overlap of participants across sessions (ranging from 10% to 40%) meant that treating the dataset as fully repeated measures would have further reduced the effective sample size. Therefore, participant random effects were omitted, and the samples were treated as approximately independent across sessions.

The Aligned Rank Transform (ART) was implemented as a non-parametric alternative for all EDA metrics to address these limitations. ART enabled the creation of a statistical model from data that did not meet normality or homoscedasticity assumptions. The ART model (3) for each EDA metric followed a similar structure to the GLMM while omitting participant random effects:(3)yij=β0+β1(Sessioni)+γj(Gamej)+ϵij

As with motor movement data, outliers in EDA metrics were identified and removed before statistical analysis using the IQR method, with a threshold of λ = 3.

Two approaches were used to evaluate the null hypothesis of no significant differences between sessions for each metric, depending on the analysis method. For GLMMs, p-values were derived using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs), which compared the fit of a full model including session as a fixed effect to a reduced model excluding it, while accounting for random intercepts for User and Game. For ART-based analyses, the data were first aligned and ranked using the ART procedure, after which p-values were obtained from Type III Wald chi-squared tests performed on the resulting mixed-effects model (Wobbrock et al., 2011).

Given the multiple metrics analyzed across the behavioral, motor movement, and EDA data, there was a risk of inflating Type I errors. To control this, the Holm-Bonferroni correction has been applied to the p-values obtained from these tests. This step ensured that family-wise error rates were managed while maintaining statistical rigor. The Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied separately for each group of metrics (behavioral, motor movement, and EDA) due to their potential connections to similar hypotheses. For instance, within the behavioral data, accuracy and number of hints were treated as related but distinct metrics, and corrections were applied collectively. Similarly, metrics were grouped for correction within the kinematic and EDA analyses. Notably, this correction was not applied to the pre-post questionnaire analyses. Each questionnaire assesses a distinct psychological dimension and was thus treated as an independent test. Specifically, while the SRS-2 primarily measures social impairments associated with ASD, focusing on aspects such as social communication, social awareness, social motivation, and social cognition, it does not directly assess ToM as a separate construct. Therefore, the SRS-2 and the ToMTB were considered independent measures, justifying their separate treatment in the analysis.

When significant session-based effects were detected in the statistical analysis, post-hoc comparisons were conducted to identify specific differences between sessions. For metrics analyzed with GLMM, estimated marginal means (EMMs) were computed, and pairwise comparisons were performed to identify significant differences. P-values were adjusted using the Tukey method to account for multiple comparisons within the family of four session estimates. For metrics analyzed with the ART model, post-hoc comparisons involved re-aligning the data for each session factor in question and recalculating ranks for each pairwise contrast. Similar to the GLMM analysis, p-values were adjusted using the Tukey method.

Discussion

This exploratory study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and feasibility of an innovative VR-based intervention designed to enhance social-cognitive skills in children with Level 1 ASD. Twenty-three children, aged 6 to 8, participated in four VR sessions, engaging with a virtual human across six games. Pre- and post-tests assessed the intervention's impact, while behavioral, motor movement, and EDA data were collected to evaluate performance and to test whether biosignals could confirm the intervention's feasibility.

The preliminary findings demonstrate an improvement in post-test ToMTB scores compared to pre-test scores, supporting the hypothesis that social-cognitive skills were enhanced following the VR-based intervention. After four sessions, participants exhibited significant gains, suggesting that the adaptive, game-based VR may be an effective tool to boost social-cognitive abilities in children with ASD.

This improvement could be attributed to the offline adaptive engine, which may have facilitated the consolidation of learned skills. Possibly, the VR intervention allowed children to practice interpreting social cues in a controlled environment, reducing cognitive strain and enabling gradual skill development, consistent with the literature (Cheng et al., 2015; Moon & Ke, 2024). Moreover, VR creates a simulated environment where individuals can iteratively practice social skills, engaging with diverse scenarios without real-world pressures, allowing them to focus entirely on social interactions (Gillies and Pan, 2018). This virtual setting enables children to observe and respond to social situations without being overwhelmed by sensory stimuli common in face-to-face interactions. By managing mental demand effectively, VR supports a gradual and repeatable approach to understanding and responding to social and emotional cues, making it easier for individuals to develop and solidify these skills (Gillies and Pan, 2018).

Another contributing factor could be the consistency and repetition provided by VR sessions, along with the adaptive feedback children received, which may have reinforced learning and facilitated the generalization of skills over the intervention period (Bekele, 2016; Moon & Ke, 2024). VR has enabled iterative interaction with virtual characters and provided real-time feedback, both of which help learning. Moreover, through controlled repetitions and standardized scenarios, participants could have learned, for example, to recognize facial expressions, tone of voice, and gestures representing different emotional states. Repetitive exposure and the ABA approach allowed participants to refine their responses, enhancing their socio-cognitive skills in a targeted, progressive manner. Therefore, these gains should be interpreted cautiously as preliminary indicators of training-related cognitive engagement and system responsiveness, rather than definitive evidence of generalized ToM enhancement.

The lack of significant pre-post SRS-2 score differences may be due to several factors. Firstly, the SRS-2 does not directly measure the child's abilities, as it relies on caregiver responses, making it an indirect measure subject to perception biases. Another possible explanation is that the SRS-2 assesses general social impairments related to ASD rather than measuring ToM as a specific construct. While some items on the SRS-2 may indirectly address aspects of ToM, such as interpreting social cues or understanding others' emotions, the scale is not designed to directly assess ToM. Instead, it provides an overall assessment of social responsiveness and social functioning difficulties. In contrast, the ToMTB assessment was likely significant because it measures the constructs trained during the intervention, aligning with the specific skills targeted by the treatment through its structured design. Although no significant pre–post differences emerged in the caregiver-reported SRS-2, this result should be interpreted in light of the measure's temporal and ecological sensitivity. The absence of significant changes in caregiver-reported SRS-2 scores further highlights the limited duration of the intervention and the challenges of detecting short-term changes in real-world social functioning. Caregiver-based measures may require longer intervention periods and broader ecological exposure to capture meaningful behavioral generalization. The SRS-2 captures broad, everyday patterns of social responsiveness, which may require extended exposure and generalization opportunities to change. In contrast, the in-task cognitive and behavioral improvements observed during training reflect proximal gains in social-cognitive processing that may precede overt behavioral changes recognized by caregivers. This lack of short-term change likely reflects the limited sensitivity of caregiver-reported measures such as the SRS-2 to brief interventions and represents a methodological constraint rather than evidence of absent learning. Such a lag between cognitive acquisition and ecological generalization is consistent with prior intervention research in ASD, where real-world social behaviors often consolidate only after repeated practice and contextual transfer (Ke & Im, 2013; McMahon et al., 2013).

Behavioral results further supported the treatment's feasibility, suggesting that online and offline adaptive engines may have contributed to improved social-cognitive skills. Indeed, results showed that accuracy increased progressively as the number of hints decreased across sessions, supporting the initial hypothesis.

Results revealed significantly higher performance in sessions 3 and 4 compared to sessions 1 and 2, and a decreasing trend in hint usage in sessions 3 and 4 compared to sessions 1 and 2. This suggested a positive impact of the sessions on performance, particularly evident in the second half of the intervention. This progression may indicate a gradual internalization of the socio-cognitive skills practiced during the sessions. However, this pattern cannot be attributed to a simple learning effect. Rigorous control was implemented by varying stimuli across levels to avoid repeated exposure to identical trials and to promote behavioral generalization. Indeed, controlled repetition and various stimuli reduce the risk of learning effects and promote the generalization of acquired skills, making participants more likely to apply those skills in different contexts (Baine & Starr, 1991; Kostek, 2014; Poldrack, 2001). Here, “behavioral generalization” refers to in-task transfer—the ability to apply similar reasoning to new but structurally comparable VR contexts. Although real-life generalization was not directly measured, the design aimed to expose participants to varied and in-task ecologically valid social scenarios. Importantly, previous research has suggested that VR environments can facilitate the generalization of social-interaction learning to real-world contexts, as they provide a safe yet realistic environment for repeated practice and feedback (Ke & Im, 2013).

An increasing accuracy score may also reflect greater self-efficacy in answering questions and relying on their abilities (Bandura & Locke, 2003; Vancouver, 2001). Practicing in a safe, non-judgmental virtual space likely reduced social anxiety, allowing participants to engage more confidently with the tasks (Didehbani et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018). Indeed, VR environments are especially beneficial for children on the autism spectrum (Level 1), who may often avoid social situations in the real world due to fears of failure or being misunderstood (Didehbani et al., 2016; Schilbach, 2012). This is consistent with the progressive reduction in hint usage, which likely reflects a gradual learning process, with increasing task difficulty across sessions fostering the development of participant autonomy. In an ABA-based training context, fading hints (self-fading in the present case) is a strategic approach to promoting independence, helping participants build confidence and competence in performing tasks without assistance (Cengher et al., 2018; Miwa, 2012; Wood et al., 1976). This process mirrors the educational scaffolding model, where initial support is systematically reduced as participants gain proficiency, encouraging cognitive and behavioral independence (Belland, 2014; Bransford, 2000). This method has proven effective across educational settings for promoting incremental skill acquisition and self-confidence (Wood et al., 1976). These findings imply that the ABA-style adaptation for this VR intervention may have positively affected performance outcomes. Indeed, the online adaptive engine might have favored greater accuracy and reduced reliance on hints through feedback, explanations, and reinforcement.

Biosignal data were consistent with behavioral and pre- and post-outcome measures, further supporting the intervention's feasibility. Across sessions, both kinematic and EDA measures showed progressive increases, suggesting that participants became more engaged and comfortable within the VR environment.

Comparisons across 12 kinematic metrics consistently demonstrated an upward trend in acceleration and displacement, with significant increases between session 1 and sessions 2, 3, and 4, and in some cases between session 2 and sessions 3 and 4. The lack of statistical significance between sessions 3 and 4 for some metrics may be due to their similarity, suggesting a possible plateau effect.

Notably, the data revealed sharper accelerations and more abrupt decelerations as sessions progressed, suggesting participants may have experienced increased comfort and self-efficacy. This trend may reflect participants' growing confidence in their abilities, allowing for more deliberate and precise movements, indicative of an enhanced sense of control and familiarity with the task. Similarly, metrics of body displacement increased across sessions, suggesting that participants felt more at ease in the controlled, supportive intervention setting, which encouraged freer movement.

These results can be interpreted within the framework of exposure therapy, which posits that gradual exposure to controlled stimuli can help alleviate anxiety and enhance comfort over time, a process particularly relevant for individuals with ASD in social contexts (Maskey et al., 2014; Rothbaum et al., 2000, 2010). Exposure therapy typically allows individuals to desensitize to the stimulus and experience reduced avoidance behaviors (Rothbaum & Schwartz, 2002). In this intervention, repeated exposure to the structured environment likely facilitated a gradual acclimatization, reducing initial tension—often manifesting as motor “freezing”—and fostering freer motor expressions (Wallbridge et al., 2024). For example, the plateau in the trend between sessions 3 and 4 may indicate that participants reached a point of optimal familiarity with the setup. This adaptability to new environments is particularly relevant for children with ASD, who often exhibit reduced motor activity in unfamiliar settings (Brown & Semple, 1970; Buckle et al., 2021). This tendency aligns with research suggesting that children with ASD may require longer exposure to acclimate to new settings, as they often respond with reduced motor flexibility and increased rigidity in unfamiliar contexts (Trevarthen & Delafield-Butt, 2013; Whyatt & Craig, 2013).In the present feasibility framework, EDA was included as an exploratory indicator of physiological engagement during training, rather than a direct measure of emotional valence or clinical improvement. The EDA findings revealed a significant increase in both tonic mean and the standard deviation of tonic and phasic responses from the first session to subsequent VR intervention sessions, indicating higher physiological activation levels. Notably, these differences were significant only between the first and later sessions, with no difference among sessions 2-4, suggesting that the initial session may have established a lower baseline than later sessions. Interestingly, SCR metrics, which reflect activation in response to stimuli, did not show significant changes. This could be attributed to the small sample size and/or the absence of specific analyses that matched SCR metrics with the stimuli presented in the virtual experience. Similarly to kinematic findings, EDA metrics increased across sessions, suggesting a physiological adaptation to the VR intervention. Children may have required the first session to familiarize themselves with the VR setup and training protocol, after which autonomic responses stabilized at a moderately elevated level. The initial session included a familiarization phase and brief pre-exposure training, which likely mitigated the novelty-related stress response. Some children may have displayed reduced physiological responsiveness during the first session due to uncertainty, attentional disengagement, or cautious exploratory behavior, a pattern previously observed in ASD populations during novel tasks (Hocking et al., 2022; Kushki et al., 2013). As sessions progressed and the environment became more familiar, participants likely felt more comfortable, enabling a more sustained physiological engagement, potentially driven by increased confidence and reduced novelty. The increase in the tonic component of EDA, as indicated by increases in the mean and standard deviation metrics, reflects an increase in background activation that may result from progressive adaptation to the intervention (Kever, 2021; Van Den Bosch et al., 2013). This is supported by educational and therapeutic contexts, where familiarity with VR environments typically enhances emotional engagement and responsiveness to stimuli and virtual interactions (Benoit et al., 2015; Cerda et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2021). This study likely fostered a deeper involvement in VR tasks, with increased attentional resources directed toward the activities between the first and subsequent sessions.

Related research has shown how EDA levels increased upon introducing a secondary stimulus. However, these levels remained unchanged even as the difficulty of the secondary stimulus increased (Mehler et al., 2009; Tao et al., 2019). This observation suggests that EDA may have limitations in detecting variations associated with higher cognitive demands, which can also explain the results of the present study (i.e., no differences between sessions 2, 3, and 4) (Charles, 2019). Indeed, EDA measures can correlate with workload and task difficulty (Charles, 2019; Miyake, 2009; Tao et al., 2019) across various VR applications (i.e., Parsons and Courtney, 2016).

The significant increase in the standard deviation of the phasic component of EDA further supports the idea of more substantial momentary activation peaks during continuous stimulation, indicating that participants responded more acutely to specific stimuli in later sessions. This increase may signal improved attentiveness and responsiveness, suggesting that participants became more immersed in VR activities (Ahmadi et al., 2023; Sousa, 2022; Winkler et al., 2020). Additionally, the gradual increase in intervention difficulty could explain the increase in autonomic activation over time, as more complex tasks likely require greater emotional or cognitive workload (Tao et al., 2019). EDA has been found to respond to sudden stimuli, with evidence indicating that higher workload, increased alongside rising stress levels (Collet et al., 2014). Indeed, extensive research demonstrated that strong test-retest correlations were obtained for EDA and workload, despite notable individual variation (Miyake, 2009).

These adaptive responses, reflected in tonic and phasic measures, may represent temporary physiological shifts associated with learning and activation adjustments as participants acclimate to the intervention demands and become more actively engaged between the first session and subsequent sessions. Previous research has shown that EDA levels increased significantly from baseline but decreased over time (Fairclough & Venables, 2006). This evidence suggests that EDA may be sensitive to sudden changes in task difficulty but less to gradual shifts, potentially indicating a measurement ceiling (Charles, 2019).

Alternative explanations for these increases must be considered. Elevated EDA is bidirectional and can reflect multiple affective and cognitive states, including excitement, frustration, anxiety, and cognitive overload (Boucsein, 2012; Critchley, 2002), as well as physical movement associated with somatosensory interaction. Importantly, children with ASD often display lower initial autonomic reactivity to novel environments (Hocking et al., 2022; Kushki et al., 2013), so gradual increases across repeated exposures may signal a shift toward an optimal arousal range rather than overload. Given that behavioral monitoring by the experimenter showed no distress or withdrawal and that kinematic data indicated freer, more confident movements, the overall pattern likely reflects adaptive engagement rather than stress. However, while EDA patterns suggest increased activation, the lack of concurrent self-report data prevents from definitively distinguishing between productive engagement and increased cognitive demand. Future studies should integrate affective self-reports to disambiguate these physiological signals. Nonetheless, these interpretations remain speculative due to the limited number of recordings (32 of 92) and the absence of multimodal physiological validation.

In summary, the behavioral data suggested that the intervention was effectively structured to progressively increase the complexity of social situations, empowering participants to handle more significant challenges as they progressed, with support from an ABA-informed system. The results showed that participants used fewer hints and responded more accurately to tasks with greater exposure and difficulty. Moreover, the biosignal results were also consistent with the main results of the pre-post test, suggesting that VR technology, combined with well-structured level designs, may be effective in fostering treatment in supportive environments for children with ASD, supporting therapeutic goals, reducing context-specific motor rigidity, and effectively promoting engagement. This was likely due to the online engine, which may have improved accuracy and reduced the use of hints while promoting habituation to the game exposure, as reflected in the biosignals. Meanwhile, the offline engine likely helped consolidate the skills learned, possibly improving post-test results. Nevertheless, biosignals findings should be viewed as supportive of feasibility, complementing the pre-post outcomes that primarily demonstrated the intervention’s acceptability. Indeed, these physiological and movement-related patterns are interpreted as indicators of engagement and feasibility of the adaptive VR training rather than as evidence of functional or clinical improvement.

Although these initial results are promising, further research is necessary to confirm the feasibility of this VR-based approach to social-cognitive development. Additional studies with larger sample sizes, more diverse cohorts, and longitudinal designs would help determine the durability of these improvements and explore how VR interventions can be optimized for broader application in ASD interventions. Scalability toward larger clinical trials will be pursued to validate the system’s clinical impact and generalizability. Moreover, further research is needed to deepen the understanding of the relationship between biosignals data and specific intervention factors, while accounting for task complexity and potential increases in participants’ self-efficacy. Building on these exploratory results, future iterations of the protocol should incorporate multimodal measures, including behavioral indicators (e.g., observed affect, attention metrics, behavioral ratings) alongside facial expressions and heart rate variability, to further clarify the valence and arousal and improve interpretability during adaptive VR training. Nevertheless, a real-time affective adaptation, combining multiple, could dynamically tailor feedback and difficulty levels during gameplay. Future research should also explore individual differences in responses to intervention, examining correlations between participants' unique characteristics and their performance across intervention phases. Such analyses could inform tailored VR-based interventions to better meet the diverse needs of children on the autism spectrum, thereby enhancing their efficacy and applicability. Additionally, investigating the long-term effects of these interventions through ToMTB follow-up assessments would be essential to refine and optimize VR-based approaches. Future studies should include post-intervention naturalistic observations to verify whether the skills acquired in VR environments generalize to daily social contexts. To support real-world implementation, future plans should develop therapist dashboards for continuous progress monitoring and design home-based applications that extend training beyond the laboratory setting. Additionally, interoperability with head-mounted XR platforms is under consideration, contingent on future work addressing sensory tolerability and motion constraints in children with ASD. Finally, an enhanced intervention design could be explored in future research. These extensions will enable larger-scale clinical validation and broaden accessibility in educational and therapeutic contexts. While the findings of this study are encouraging, the study's limitations must be acknowledged. First, the small sample size, partly due to participant exclusions during recruitment, limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the observed improvements (e.g., in ToMTB scores) should be interpreted as preliminary trends rather than definitive clinical effects. Larger, more gender/balanced samples are needed to confirm these findings. This limitation reflects the challenges of recruiting young participants for specialized intervention studies, particularly within the narrow age range targeted. In addition to the small, heterogeneous sample, the lack of a control group limits the interpretability and sustainability of the observed effects. Language abilities, comorbid conditions, and socioeconomic background were not systematically assessed and may have influenced outcomes. Second, the absence of self-report measures to complement biosignal data represents a gap that future research should address to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between physiological and subjective experiences. Nevertheless, self-reports about emotional states in ASD children (Level 1) are challenging due to participant misunderstandings, inaccurate recollections, and automatic affective processes bypassing conscious control. Finally, significant data loss due to poor signal quality led to data exclusions that compromised the homogeneity of the sample, thus preventing more specific analyses with random participant effects. Improved signal acquisition and processing techniques are essential for future studies to effectively address this issue.
