Summary

# Summary: Augmentative and Alternative Communication in Autism Education The objective of this systematic review was to synthesize peer-reviewed classroom-based evidence from 2009–2019 on three widely used AAC modalities (Picture Exchange Communication System, speech-generating devices, and low-technology communication boards) for children with autism spectrum disorder who have complex communication needs. The review examined single-case experimental studies, group comparison studies, and prior meta-analyses, analyzing evidence across requesting, commenting, and social communication use, as well as the role of instructional assistants in mediating AAC implementation. The key finding was that no single AAC modality demonstrated empirical superiority across all learners, developmental profiles, and classroom settings; instead, outcomes varied by individual and context. The review recommends that practitioners adopt a learner-first, modality-flexible framework prioritizing communicative function, naturalistic opportunities, and stakeholder engagement when selecting AAC interventions. Future research priorities include extended follow-up periods, greater examination of cross-cultural implementation, and stronger integration between AAC use and overall curriculum planning in both inclusive and specialist school settings.

Abstract

Communication difficulties remain one of the most defining features of autism spectrum disorder , and a substantial proportion of children on the spectrum enter formal schooling with limited or no functional speech. Augmentative and alternative communication has, over the past three decades, matured from a last-resort intervention into a central component of educational practice for learners with complex communication needs. This systematic review synthesises the peer-reviewed classroom-based evidence published between 2009 and 2019 on three of the most widely deployed AAC modalities for children with ASD: The Picture Exchange Communication System , speech-generating devices , and low-technology communication boards. Drawing on peer-reviewed journal articles, single-case experimental studies, group comparison studies, practitioner-oriented syntheses, and prior meta-analyses, the review maps the theoretical scaffolds of each modality, interrogates the evidence for requesting, commenting, and social use, examines the role of instructional assistants and paraprofessionals in mediating AAC use, and reflects on the methodological limitations of the existing corpus. The review argues that no single AAC modality is empirically superior across all learners, developmental profiles, and classroom ecologies, and that practitioner decision-making should be guided by a learner-first, modality-flexible framework that privileges communicative function, naturalistic opportunity, and stakeholder buy-in. Implications are drawn for teachers, paraprofessionals, speech-language therapists, and school leaders operating in inclusive and specialist settings. The review concludes with a set of propositions for future research, including the need for longer follow-up periods, greater attention to cross-cultural implementation, and stronger integration between AAC provision and the broader curriculum.
