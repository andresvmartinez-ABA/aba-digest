The Intersection of Artificial Intelligence and Augmentative & Alternative Communication (AAC) for Autism

Summary

# Summary: AI and AAC for Autism This article examines how artificial intelligence can be integrated with augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) systems to support individuals with speech and language impairments, particularly those with autism. The authors discuss the current landscape of AAC tools, which range from static symbols and paper-based systems to sophisticated eye-gaze controlled technology and speech synthesizers. Key challenges identified include the substantial burden placed on educators and caregivers in constructing and maintaining individualized AAC materials that match users' active vocabularies, and the complexity of creating universally shared AAC materials without a standardized symbol set across vendors. While the abstract does not report specific empirical findings or effect sizes, it positions AI as a potential solution to reduce the cognitive and administrative load on practitioners and improve the coherence and customization of AAC systems for users with broader communication repertoires. For BCBA practitioners, this suggests that AI-enhanced AAC systems may offer practical improvements in reducing the time and resources required to develop personalized communication supports while potentially increasing the breadth and sophistication of vocabulary available to individuals with autism and related communication disorders.

Abstract

Communication is a crucial aspect of life through which people can express needs, preferences, and emotions, and exchange information. However, it may not always be easy for everyone. For individuals affected by speech and language impairments, conventional dialogs based on spoken language represent a daunting challenge. A viable alternative may entail the adoption of Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC), a spectrum of techniques and tools that aid communication using non-verbal means. In this popular shift, symbol sequences take on the task of conveying meaning; available media include static symbols, paper and mobile-based systems, sophisticated dedicated hardware equipped with speech synthesizers, and eye-gaze controlled technology. The transformative power of AAC is demonstrated by its ability to enable previously impossible dialogs and replace spoken language with symbol sequences that in due time are actively associated with wide arrays of actions and concepts. Future successes come when AAC users exercise broader communication repertoires, mastering not only everyday language but concepts incorporating technical vocabulary or rarely used idioms. However, the construction and maintenance of AAC materials adhering to the paucity of specific characteristics and matching the active vocabulary may place an unsustainable load on the part of educators and caregivers, with the risk that the prepared materials might result in frustrating or disheartening outcomes. Additionally, the creation of coherent and universally shared AAC materials matched against the intended target may require roundtable and the contributions of an ensemble of actors such as educators, caregivers, content experts, and domain experts since no universal minimum subset of symbols exists, resulting in complex layouts where pictograms from the same dictionary have to be distributed amongst different vendors with a similarly opened policy to the symbol set.
