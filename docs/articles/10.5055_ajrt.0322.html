Attitudes of recreational therapists working with individuals with autism spectrum disorder toward adoption and implementation of socially assistive robots

Summary

# Summary: Attitudes of Recreational Therapists Toward Socially Assistive Robots for Autism This study examined the attitudes, acceptance, and adoption intentions of United States-based recreational therapists toward implementing socially assistive robots (SARs) in treatment for individuals with autism spectrum disorder. The researchers employed a nonexperimental, quantitative cross-sectional survey design in which participants completed the Almere model of Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology questionnaire—a validated 32-item instrument measuring multiple domains of technology acceptance. Hierarchical regression analyses identified three significant predictors of SAR acceptance: Perceived Ease of Use, Perceived Adaptiveness, and Perceived Sociability. These findings suggest that recreational therapists' adoption of SARs depends primarily on beliefs about usability, the robot's ability to meet therapeutic needs, and perceived social interaction capabilities. The study supports the need for user-centered design of robotic tools and targeted training and resource allocation to facilitate implementation in therapeutic settings serving individuals with ASD.

Abstract

The integration of socially assistive robots (SARs) into recreational therapy practices offers a promising avenue to enhance therapeutic outcomes for individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). SARs have the potential to foster engagement, promote leisure behaviors, and support social skill development. However, despite their benefits, widespread implementation has been hindered by barriers such as limited training, low self-efficacy, attitudinal resistance, and ethical concerns. This study explores the attitudes, acceptance, and intentions of United States-based recreational therapists toward adopting SARs in their work with individuals with ASD. Using a nonexperimental, quantitative cross-sectional survey design, participants completed the Almere model of Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology questionnaire, a validated 32-item tool measuring domains of technology acceptance. Hierarchical regression analyses revealed key predictors of acceptance included Perceived Ease of Use, Perceived Adaptiveness, and Perceived Sociability. This study contributes to informing strategies for designing user-friendly, engaging robotic tools while underscoring the need for targeted training and resource allocation in therapeutic settings.
