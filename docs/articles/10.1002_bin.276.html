Establishing stimulus control of vocal stereotypy displayed by young children with Autism

John T. Rapp, Meeta R. Patel, Pietro Ghezzi, Christine H. O'Flaherty, Craig J. Titterington  ·  Behavioral Interventions  ·  2009
95/100 Manually curated paper from Research Rabbit library.
Summary

This study examined whether vocal stereotypy displayed by three boys with Autism Spectrum Disorder could be brought under stimulus control using discriminative stimuli. The researchers conducted functional analyses confirming the behavior was automatically reinforced, then implemented an intervention where vocal stereotypy resulted in verbal reprimands (and toy removal for one participant) in the presence of a red card, while being permitted without consequences in the presence of a green card. Results showed that vocal stereotypy decreased in the presence of the red card for all participants, but only one participant demonstrated clear inhibitory stimulus control where the behavior was consistently suppressed only when the red card was present. The findings suggest limited effectiveness of this punishment-based approach for establishing reliable stimulus control over automatically reinforced vocal stereotypy in this population.

Abstract

Abstract We examined the vocal stereotypy of three boys who were diagnosed with an Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). Results of functional analyses indicated that each participant's vocal stereotypy was maintained by non‐social consequences. For two participants, verbal reprimands were provided contingent on vocal stereotypy in the presence of a red card (RC). For the third participant, after verbal reprimands alone did not decrease vocal stereotypy, toys were withdrawn contingent on vocal stereotypy in the presence of the RC. For all three participants, vocal stereotypy was permitted without programmed consequences the presence of a green card (GC). The results showed that vocal stereotypy decreased in the presence of the RC for all three participants; however, vocal stereotypy came under inhibitory control of the RC for only one of the three participants. The potential utility of using punishment to develop stimulus control of automatically reinforced problem behavior in academic settings is briefly discussed.
