Executive function predicts self‐injurious behaviour in autistic children with co‐occurring intellectual disability

C. Laverty, Rory T. Devine, Katherine Marlow, G. Agar, A. Bagshaw, F. Symons et al.  ·  JCPP Advances  ·  2026

Summary

# Summary: Executive Function and Self-Injurious Behavior in Autistic Children with Intellectual Disability This study examined whether impaired executive function (EF) predicts self-injurious behavior (SIB) in autistic children with co-occurring intellectual disability, a population frequently excluded from research due to measurement limitations. The researchers collected informant-report data and administered a novel, developmentally appropriate direct EF task battery to 126 autistic children with ID (mean age 9.95 years; 84.9% exhibited SIB), using structural equation modeling to analyze associations between EF and SIB while accounting for language ability and age. Directly assessed EF showed unique associations with SIB severity and presence, independent of chronological age and language ability, though latent EF scores did not correlate with caregiver ratings of EF and were not associated with aggression or property destruction. For BCBA practitioners, these findings suggest that directly assessed executive function deficits, particularly behavioral inhibition, may represent a modifiable intervention target for reducing SIB in autistic children with intellectual disability. The study also demonstrates that a remote EF assessment battery is feasible and accessible for this population, potentially enabling more individualized functional assessment of behavior in clinical and educational settings.

Abstract

AbstractBackgroundSelf‐injurious behaviours (SIB) occur frequently in autistic children with co‐occurring intellectual disability (ID), a group often excluded from research due to a lack of appropriate measures. Delineating individual characteristics associated with SIB is essential to inform proactive intervention strategies. The present study examines associations between impaired behavioural inhibition and SIB using a novel direct battery of executive function (EF) tasks in autistic children with ID.MethodsInformant report data on SIB, EF and adaptive behaviour and a novel, developmentally appropriate EF task battery were collected for 126 autistic children with ID (mean age = 9.95, 28 female, 84.9% showed SIB). Structural equation modelling was used to examine the association between EF and SIB, and the unique contribution of EF to the presence and severity of SIB.ResultsDirect assessments of EF were feasible, valid, reliable and sensitive to individual difference. A latent factor score derived from the direct EF tasks provided a meaningful metric of individual ability, independent of the child's chronological age and language ability. Latent EF scores were not associated with caregiver‐rated EF, and neither language ability nor age explained significant variance. However, the latent EF variable was uniquely associated with self‐injury, but not with aggression or destruction of property.ConclusionThe results extend previous findings of a robust association between informant‐reported impulsive and overactive behaviours and SIB, although these associations were not unique to SIB but were shared with other behaviours that challenge. Directly assessed EF was uniquely associated with SIB, after accounting for language ability and age. EF may play an important role in SIB development, with EF emerging as a putative intervention target to improve SIB for autistic children with ID. Importantly, the results also demonstrate the feasibility, accessibility and preliminary validity of a novel remote EF battery for children with ID.

Methods

Informant report data on SIB, EF and adaptive behaviour and a novel, developmentally appropriate EF task battery were collected for 126 autistic children with ID (mean age = 9.95, 28 female, 84.9% showed SIB). Structural equation modelling was used to examine the association between EF and SIB, and the unique contribution of EF to the presence and severity of SIB.

Results

Direct assessments of EF were feasible, valid, reliable and sensitive to individual difference. A latent factor score derived from the direct EF tasks provided a meaningful metric of individual ability, independent of the child's chronological age and language ability. Latent EF scores were not associated with caregiver‐rated EF, and neither language ability nor age explained significant variance. However, the latent EF variable was uniquely associated with self‐injury, but not with aggression or destruction of property.

Discussion

The results extend previous findings of a robust association between informant‐reported impulsive and overactive behaviours and SIB, although these associations were not unique to SIB but were shared with other behaviours that challenge. Directly assessed EF was uniquely associated with SIB, after accounting for language ability and age. EF may play an important role in SIB development, with EF emerging as a putative intervention target to improve SIB for autistic children with ID. Importantly, the results also demonstrate the feasibility, accessibility and preliminary validity of a novel remote EF battery for children with ID.
