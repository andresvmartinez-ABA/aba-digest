Designing an LLM-driven mobile AR companion for social interaction support in children and youth with autism: a pilot study

Zhi-Zhu Wu, Man Yang, Jia-Xin Liu, Hai Peng, Jingxi Su  ·  Frontiers in Virtual Reality  ·  2026

Summary

# Summary: Stand By Me LLM-driven Mobile AR Companion This pilot study aimed to explore whether an AI-driven mobile augmented reality system grounded in IEP principles could support social interaction and real-world task participation in children with autism spectrum disorder. The researchers designed Stand By Me, a system integrating a large language model with mobile AR technology featuring an interactive virtual pet, multimodal interaction (speech and gesture), and personalized task generation that dynamically adapts to user input and context, then conducted a descriptive feasibility pilot with 9 participants aged 6–22 years with ASD in a rehabilitation setting. Results demonstrated that participants were able to repeatedly use the application during supervised training, interact with the AR virtual pet, and participate in real-world outing tasks with system guidance and caregiver supervision, with observable trends in engagement and task participation over time. The findings provide preliminary feasibility evidence that LLM-supported mobile AR systems may serve as adaptive social partners offering continuous, context-aware support in everyday environments—a capability that traditional scripted tools and screen-based applications have not provided. BCBAs and autism practitioners may consider these preliminary results as foundational evidence for exploring how AI-integrated AR technologies could extend behavioral intervention and skill-building opportunities beyond structured clinic or classroom settings into community contexts.

Abstract

Children and youth with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) often face persistent challenges in social interaction, communication, and participation in real-world contexts. Although individualized education programs and behavioral interventions play an important role in current practice, existing tools often lack the ability to provide continuous and adaptive support in everyday environments. To address this gap, this paper presents Stand By Me, an interactive system that integrates a large language model with mobile augmented reality.

The system embeds structured goals into everyday activities through an AR virtual pet, multimodal interaction, and personalized task generation, thereby supporting users’ interaction and task participation in real-world settings. Compared with traditional scripted training tools, the system dynamically generates responses based on user input and provides contextualized guidance by incorporating location and task information. This study explores the system through a descriptive real-world feasibility pilot conducted in collaboration with a rehabilitation institution. The pilot included 9 participants with ASD aged 6–22 years.

The results showed that participants were able to repeatedly use the application during supervised training routines, interact with the AR virtual pet, and participate in real-world outing tasks with system guidance and caregiver supervision.

These findings provide preliminary feasibility evidence and design insights into the use of LLM-supported mobile AR systems for situated interaction and real-world task participation in ASD support contexts.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) affects more than 1% of children worldwide, with core characteristics typically emerging in early childhoodNicholas et al. (2008). Children with ASD often experience persistent difficulties in social communication, reciprocal interaction, and adaptive participation in everyday environments. These challenges may manifest as reduced eye contact, limited conversational initiation, difficulty interpreting social cues, and discomfort in unfamiliar social situations. As a result, many children with ASD face barriers to community participation and independent functioningLavelle et al. (2014).

To address these challenges, intervention approaches such as Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA)Cooper et al. (2020)and Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) have been widely adopted. IEPs, in particular, provide structured and personalized educational and developmental plans tailored to each child’s needs, emphasizing measurable goals, continuous assessment, and adaptive intervention strategiesWilczynski et al. (2007);Rotter (2014). However, despite their effectiveness in structured settings, these approaches often face limitations in providing continuous, adaptive, and context-aware support in everyday environments. Existing digital tools, including mobile applications, are frequently confined to screen-based or pre-scripted scenarios, offering limited support for real-world interaction and dynamic adaptationBoucenna et al. (2014).

Recent advances in immersive technologies and artificial intelligence offer new opportunities to address these limitations. Augmented reality (AR) enables the integration of virtual elements into real-world environments, supporting situated interaction and contextualized learningAzuma (1997). At the same time, large language models (LLMs) provide powerful capabilities for generating context-aware responses, allowing systems to dynamically adapt interaction content based on user input and behavioral patterns. Together, these technologies create the potential for more flexible and personalized support for children with ASD.

Immersive technologies such as virtual reality (VR) have also been explored for social skill training due to their ability to create controlled and engaging environments. However, designing immersive systems for children with ASD presents unique challenges, particularly in relation to sensory sensitivity and responses to wearable devices. Prior research indicates that children with ASD exhibit a high prevalence of comorbid psychiatric conditions, such as specific phobias, which may contribute to heightened sensitivity to unfamiliar or intrusive sensory experiences, including wearable equipmentLeyfer et al. (2006). In this context, head-mounted VR devices, which require sustained wearing and introduce immersive visual stimuli, may present barriers to acceptance and practical use. To reduce such barriers and improve usability, this study adopts a mobile AR approach, allowing virtual elements to be embedded into the real-world environment without requiring intrusive wearable devices.

In response to these challenges, we present Stand By Me, an LLM-driven mobile AR application designed to support social interaction in children with ASD. Grounded in IEP principles, the system integrates AR-based virtual companionship, multimodal interaction (speech and gesture), and adaptive task generation to provide personalized and context-aware support. Through interaction with an AR virtual pet, children are encouraged to engage in communication and participate in real-world activities such as guided travel tasks and simple social scenarios. Unlike static applications, the system dynamically adapts its responses and task difficulty based on the child’s profile and interaction history.

This work is designed as a pilot study to explore the feasibility, acceptability, and interaction patterns of such an approach, rather than to establish causal effects on social skill development. Accordingly, we investigate the following research questions:

RQ1: How can an AI-driven intervention system grounded in IEP principles support social interaction in children with ASD?

RQ2: To what extent is an LLM-driven mobile AR system feasible and acceptable for guiding communication and real-world interaction in children with ASD?

To address these questions, we design, implement, and evaluate the Stand By Me system in collaboration with a rehabilitation institution. The contributions of this work are as follows:

We present the design of a mobile AR intervention system grounded in IEP principles to support social interaction in children with ASD.

We explore the integration of LLM-based adaptive interaction and AR-based embodied interaction as a feasible approach for supporting communication and real-world engagement.

We report a real-world pilot study with 9 children with ASD, providing preliminary evidence on feasibility, acceptability, and interaction patterns.

Discussion

This study presents Stand By Me, a mobile augmented reality system integrated with a large language model to support interaction and task participation for children with ASD in real-world contexts. By combining a responsive virtual companion, multimodal interaction through speech and gesture, and personalized task generation, the system embeds individualized goals into everyday activities.

Through a real-world user study conducted in collaboration with a rehabilitation institution, we explored the system’s performance in practice. The results indicate observable trends in children’s engagement, interaction behaviors, and task participation over a period of use. Children were able to sustain interaction with the AR virtual pet and participate in real-world tasks with the support of system guidance.

Overall, this work suggests that LLM-supported interactive virtual characters have potential as adaptive social partners in situated environments. The findings provide a basis for future research on AI-supported embodied interaction, virtual companionship, and real-world social participation for children with ASD.
